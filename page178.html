#5053. Love to Read? Your Book Habit May Lower Alzheimer’s Risk by 33%

📅 25 Oct 2026 | Category: other | Source
  • Research shows that reading for pleasure has various benefits for people of all ages.
  • Reading has been linked to better cognitive skills and mental well-being, as well as lower levels of stress and loneliness.
  • For children, reading for pleasure is associated with better attention, memory, academic achievement, and mental health.
  • In older adults, regular reading is associated with slower cognitive decline and a lower risk of dementia.

Whether it’s a novel, a magazine, or a graphic novel, reading for pleasure can benefit people of all ages, according to a review published in Psychological Medicine on September 15.

Regular reading has been linked to better thinking skills, mental well-being, and a lower risk of cognitive decline. From childhood through older adulthood, this accessible activity may help build cognitive reserve and support brain health.

Alex Dimitriu, MD, double board certified in Psychiatry and Sleep Medicine, and founder of Menlo Park Psychiatry & Sleep Medicine, who wasn’t involved in the review, told Healthline, “Reading builds empathy, improves focus, and offers a healthy digital detox. Reading also fires up the imagination and provides a low enough level of stimulation to inspire us to think and feel on our own.”

While research shows various benefits of reading, it is important to keep in mind that most studies on reading and brain health are observational, showing associations rather than causation. 

Reading may support brain development and mental health in children

The importance of reading for pleasure begins in early childhood (ages 3–10).

Data from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study showed that reading for pleasure during this period was associated with long-lasting cognitive, behavioral, and academic benefits in adolescence. 

Neuroimaging also showed that reading for pleasure was associated with larger cortical areas and greater volumes in brain regions linked to language and emotional regulation.

“Importantly, these associations remained even after accounting for factors such as parental education, household income, and other potential confounders, suggesting that early reading may be an accessible way to support healthy brain development,” said Dung Trinh, MD, internist, of MemorialCare Medical Group and Chief Medical Officer of Healthy Brain Clinic in Irvine, CA, who wasn’t involved in the review.

However, data show that reading among children and adolescents is declining, especially among boys ages 11–16 and those from lower socioeconomic backgrounds. 

Despite this decline in engagement, findings from a 2025 United Kingdom survey highlight opportunities to re-engage these young readers. For example: 

  • 2 in 5 stated they were more motivated to read when the material was related to a favorite TV series or film (38.1%) or matched an interest or hobby (37.1%)
  • 3 in 10 (30.9%) said they were drawn to an interesting cover or title
  • 1 in 4 (26.6%) valued the freedom to choose what they read

Even those who reported low enjoyment of reading acknowledged its educational value. Nearly half said that reading helps them learn new words and new things, with many choosing to read song lyrics, news articles, fiction, comics, and fan fiction. 

These findings suggest that aligning reading with familiar media and personal interests may help encourage children and adolescents to read more.

There are many reasons why reversing this trend is important.

A crucial one is that children and adolescents who enjoy reading, or read every day, are more likely to achieve significantly higher scores on reading assessments.

This highlights the association between reading engagement and literacy attainment.

Reading may also benefit mathematical development, as both these skills share various cognitive and neural mechanisms. 

Evidence also shows that children who begin reading for pleasure early in life show: 

  • better attention, memory, and executive functioning
  • greater academic achievement 
  • fewer mental health problems 
  • healthier lifestyles, including more sleep and less screen time

Finding ways to encourage children to read for pleasure can be important for both their educational development and their overall well-being. 

Reading and “preventive psychiatry”

Reading can be a part of a practice known as “preventive psychiatry.” 

This branch of psychiatry focuses on health promotion and protection from certain mental health conditions. It also aims at early diagnosis, effective treatment, disability limitation, and rehabilitation. 

Preventive psychiatry has been found to be especially effective in reducing the incidence of mental health conditions like depression and anxiety. 

Reading may help slow cognitive decline and reduce dementia risk in older adults

Reading has also been found to offer several other benefits for adults, including overall well-being, reducing feelings of loneliness, and improving social connections. 

In a 2015 Quick Reads survey by The Reading Agency, adults suggested that reading offered significant benefits for their well-being, particularly fostering empathy, personal growth, and stress reduction. 

Among adults who read for just 30 minutes each week, 64% reported a better understanding of other people’s feelings.

Adults also reported that reading inspired positive life changes, such as seeking a new job, ending an unhealthy relationship, or traveling to new places.

Readers also reported positive changes in their personal development, with 20% saying they began taking better care of their health and 19% stating they took up a new hobby.

Half of the readers also noted that they became more tolerant of other people’s beliefs, and 17% said they were able to stay calmer during a disagreement. 

Surveys showed that reading is one of the most popular stress relievers among adults, with 38% choosing books as their preferred way to unwind. More people turned to reading for comfort, with 35% choosing this option compared to 31% choosing wine and 10% a hot bath.

Even just six minutes of reading was found to reduce stress by up to 68%.

Around 74% of adult readers reported that reading has had a positive effect on their mental health and well-being, and 41% said it helps them feel more connected to others. 

Surveys also highlight positive early experiences with reading, with 65% of adults saying they enjoyed being read to as a child.

Among adults ages 18–34, 59% said reading made them feel more connected to others, while 44% expressed a desire to have someone to read with.

“The muscles of sustained focus, patience, slowing down, impulse control, and imagination are essential to defining us as human beings, and among our highest qualities. Reading is one of the few remaining quiet refuges in our modern lives,” said Dimitriu.

Book clubs and shared reading offer social benefits for all ages, but this may be especially important for older adults. 

How to incorporate more reading into your life

  • Start small: Set aside 15–20 minutes a day to read something you enjoy.
  • Join a book club: Try in-person groups at your library or virtual clubs online.
  • Keep books accessible: Place them where you’ll see them easily.
  • Experiment with genres: If you usually read fiction, try nonfiction, audiobooks, or graphic novels.
  • Set realistic goals: Track your reading to help you stay motivated using an app or journal.

How reading can benefit older adults

Evidence from older studies indicates that cognitively stimulating activities, such as reading, are associated with slower cognitive decline and a reduced risk of dementia in older adults. 

More recent research also supports the cognitive benefits associated with reading. For instance, a 2021 study showed that older adults who read regularly performed better on cognitive tests than non-readers.  

“Reading is a cognitively stimulating activity, and one theory is that it may help build cognitive reserve, giving the brain greater resilience as it ages,” said Trinh.  

“[While] longitudinal studies have found that people who read regularly have lower rates of later cognitive impairment or dementia, more research is needed to determine exactly how much of that relationship is causal and whether reading changes Alzheimer’s biology itself.”

Research like this suggests that reading may be a low cost way to support cognition and brain health. It may also help protect against cognitive decline later in life.

Trinh noted that, “Reading should be viewed as one promising component of a broader brain-healthy lifestyle, rather than as a stand-alone treatment or guaranteed way to prevent dementia.”

#5052. Is Diet Quality More Important For Weight Loss Than Counting Calories?

📅 25 Oct 2026 | Category: other | Source
  • A two-year study found that a Mediterranean-style diet and a calorie-focused weight loss program led to similar amounts of weight loss.
  • People following the Mediterranean-style diet saw greater improvements in diet quality and were more likely to say they would recommend the program.
  • Experts say calorie balance remains important for weight loss, while choosing higher-quality foods can provide additional benefits for overall health.

A two-year study tested a Mediterranean-style diet adapted for the Southern U.S. (Med-South) against a calorie-focused program. 

While both groups lost a similar amount of weight, the Med-South group saw a greater improvement in overall diet quality. 

The study, recently published in JAMA Network Open, highlights how different approaches to weight loss may affect diet quality and long-term adherence.

Mediterranean diet vs. calorie counting for weight loss

The study followed 360 individuals from North Carolina primary care practices who had a BMI of 30 or greater and an average age of 55. Most participants also had multiple chronic conditions, such as diabetes and hypertension. 

Participants were randomly assigned to one of two groups: a Mediterranean-style diet (Med-South) or a control weight-loss program. There were 182 in the Mediterranean diet group and 178 in the control group. 

The study lasted 24 months, with assessments at 4, 12, and 24 months. 

The Mediterranean diet intervention included three phases: 

  • Phase 1 (months 1-4): focused on improving diet quality
  • Phase 2 (months 5-12): focused on weight loss
  • Phase 3 (months 13-24): focused on weight loss maintenance 

The control intervention provided access to the workshop and digital components of the WeightWatchers program. 

The Mediterranean-style diet program was led by registered dietitians and emphasized healthy fats, whole grains, fruits, and vegetables, while limiting processed foods and refined carbs. 

The WeightWatchers program focused primarily on cutting calories, while encouraging more fruits and vegetables. 

At the end of 24 months, both groups showed similar average weight loss: 3.4% in the Mediterranean-style group and 3.5% in the control group.

The researchers also found no significant differences between the groups in outcomes such as blood pressure.

Christopher Gardner, PhD, professor of medicine at Stanford Prevention Research Center, who wasn’t involved in the study, noted, “The quality of the diets was a secondary outcome, not the primary outcome. The study was not designed to test that. It can be interesting to look at that, but if quality is the main question of interest, the study has to be designed to specifically test that.” 

Mediterranean diet may improve diet quality without sacrificing weight loss

While both groups lost a similar amount of weight after 24 months, the Mediterranean diet group showed significantly greater improvements in overall diet quality, including greater consumption of nuts, vegetable oils, whole grains, non-starchy vegetables, and fruit.

It has also been associated with a reduced risk of cardiovascular disease, stroke, diabetes, and dementia. 

During the study, 95% of the Mediterranean-style group said they would recommend the program, compared with 68% of the control group.

A 2024 trial, DIETFITS, also found that high dietary adherence combined with high diet quality led to the greatest weight loss; neither alone was sufficient. 

This suggests that following an eating plan you can stick with is important, but diet quality may also play a role in supporting weight loss and overall health.

“A main concept I work with my patients on is the importance of quality over quantity. Though weight loss may be achieved on a lower-calorie diet, the results may not be sustained long term, and the potential loss of nutrients over time could impact overall chronic disease risk and mortality,” Kristin Kirkpatrick, President of KAK, Consulting, LLC, and a dietitian at the Cleveland Clinic Department of Wellness & Preventive Medicine in Cleveland, Ohio, told Healthline. Kirkpatrick wasn’t involved in the study. 

“This study follows the same insight that a high quality diet can beneficially impact not only weight loss success but overall health outcomes as well,” she added. 

Why diet quality matters for weight loss and overall health

Higher diet quality can also provide additional health benefits, such as: 

  • improved blood sugar regulation
  • better cardiovascular health markers
  • reduced inflammation
  • greater satiety and hunger management
  • support long-term healthy habits and sustainability

However, if you are trying to lose weight, calorie balance is still important. Higher-quality foods can make it easier to manage calories, but calories still matter.

“Weight loss is typically achieved by a prolonged period of calorie restriction. It is possible to eat nothing but junk food and lose weight if the amount of total food consumed does not meet the caloric needs of the person,” said Gardner. 

“However, losing weight while choosing higher quality foods (defined by a higher intake of the vitamins and minerals necessary for a healthy metabolism) supports better overall health, in the long term.”

#5051. Are You Eating Enough Whole Grains to Lower Your Heart Disease Risk?

📅 25 Oct 2026 | Category: other | Source
  • A large meta-analysis suggests that eating more whole grains may offer substantial cardiovascular benefits.
  • The findings show that every additional 48 grams of whole grains consumed per day was associated with improvements in body weight, waist circumference, total cholesterol, and inflammatory markers.
  • The greatest cardiometabolic benefits appeared at 60 to 100 grams of whole grains per day, or 4 to 6 servings.
  • Whole grains, such as oats, brown rice, whole wheat, and farro, offer cardiovascular and metabolic health benefits as part of a balanced diet.

Whole grains have long been touted for their heart health benefits. Even a box of Cheerios boasts the trademark heart-healthy claim.

Now, researchers say that loading up on whole grains may offer even greater cardiovascular benefits. 

A large meta-analysis of 87 trials found that roughly 4 to 6 servings of whole grains per day offer substantial benefits for cholesterol, inflammation, blood pressure, and metabolic health. 

The findings, published on September 15 in the European Heart Journal, indicate a “likely causal” relationship between higher whole grain intake and reductions in cardiovascular risk factors.

The benefits, the researchers say, start at around three servings of whole grains per day. That’s a bowl of oatmeal (1 serving) and two slices of whole wheat bread (2 servings).

This suggests that the current recommendations of 2 to 4 servings of whole grains per day set by the U.S. Dietary Guidelines for Americans are sufficient.

However, adding a few more servings to your diet each day could be a boon for heart and metabolic health.

Still, most U.S. adults fall short of getting enough whole grains in their diets, with only 2.9–7.7% consuming the recommended amount each day, meaning that most people aren’t getting enough dietary fiber.

Cardiovascular benefits start at 3 servings per day

The meta-analysis examined randomized trials and identified a dose-response relationship between whole grain consumption and multiple health markers. 

For every additional 48 grams of whole grains consumed per day, participants experienced reductions in:

  • body weight
  • waist circumference
  • total cholesterol
  • interleukin-6 (IL-6), a protein involved in inflammation

For most outcomes, the greatest benefits were observed at approximately 60 to 100 grams of whole grains per day by dry weight, or about 4 to 6 servings.

However, most of the trials analyzed lasted about 8 weeks, so the long-term effects of these improvements are not yet known.

While the findings come from randomized controlled trials, a strong form of evidence, the research is not definitive. 

The observed changes were generally modest, but even modest improvements in cardiovascular risk factors can be meaningful, particularly when whole grains are consumed as part of a healthy, balanced diet. 

“The findings reinforce the broader evidence supporting whole grains as part of a heart-healthy diet,” said
Cheng-Han Chen, MD, board certified interventional cardiologist and medical director of the Structural Heart Program at MemorialCare Saddleback Medical Center in Laguna Hills, CA. Chen wasn’t involved in the study.

Chen said the researchers’ 4-serving recommendation provides a useful, practical target for heart health, but noted that everyone’s nutritional needs are different.

“I would not interpret these findings to mean that everyone needs to reach a specific number of servings to improve their heart health. Rather, they reinforce the importance of making whole grains a regular part of our diets and choosing them consistently over refined grains whenever possible,” Chen told Healthline.

How whole grains may protect heart health

Whole grains may support cardiovascular health through several mechanisms. 

As a key source of dietary fiber, whole grains also contain several important nutrients that support heart health.

“Dietary fiber is likely an important part of the picture, but it is probably not the only factor,” Chen said. “Whole grains contain fiber as well as a variety of vitamins, minerals, antioxidants, and other plant compounds that may work together to support cardiovascular health.”

Michelle Routhenstein, a heart-health dietitian for Entirely Nourished, wasn’t involved in the study, but explained that whole grains contain a combination of heart-healthy nutrients, such as:

  • beta-glucan fiber
  • magnesium
  • potassium
  • B vitamins
  • polyphenols
  • healthy fats
  • phytosterols (derived from wheat germ)

Chen added that dietary fiber may also help support blood sugar management, healthy cholesterol levels, and a healthier gut microbiome.

“Whole grains may influence several of the established risk factors for heart disease, including cholesterol, blood pressure, blood sugar, body weight, and inflammation,” he said.

“Rather than attributing the cardiovascular benefits of whole grains to one particular nutrient, I think of them as a nutrient-dense food group that can positively influence several pathways involved in cardiovascular health.”

Of course, plenty of research supports the cardiovascular and metabolic benefits of whole grain consumption.

A 2020 systematic review and meta-analysis found that replacing refined grains with whole grains can improve total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, hemoglobin A1c, and C-reactive protein.

A 2025 meta-analysis reported reductions in systolic or diastolic blood pressure, although results across studies were somewhat inconsistent.

Whole grains may also help lower IL-6 levels, a marker of inflammation. Elevated IL-6 is associated with inflammation and increased cardiovascular risk, making reductions in this marker potentially relevant to heart health.

Whole grains may also support weight loss and healthy weight management.

While these improvements may benefit cardiovascular and metabolic health, experts note that whole grains aren’t a magic bullet.

“Whole grains are one component of an overall heart-healthy lifestyle,” Chen said.

“In addition to incorporating whole grains regularly, I recommend focusing on a balanced diet that includes plenty of fruits and vegetables, lean sources of protein, and healthy fats while limiting highly processed foods, added sugars, and excessive sodium. Regular physical activity is also important, as are maintaining a healthy weight, not smoking, getting adequate sleep, and keeping conditions such as high blood pressure, high cholesterol, and diabetes under control.”

How many servings of whole grains should you eat?

Refined carbohydrates account for around 42% of total calories in the average American diet. Experts recommend choosing unrefined whole grains over empty, processed carbs when possible.

Routhenstein said that many people could benefit from getting more whole grains in their diet, but not everyone may need 6 servings of whole grains per day.

“What matters most is the replacement of refined grains for whole grains, rather than simply adding more food to the diet,” she said.

For meaningful cardiometabolic benefits, you might aim for around 3 to 6 servings of whole grains per day. For context, a single serving is about 16 grams of whole grains, such as:

  • one slice of whole wheat bread
  • 1/2 cup of cooked brown rice or oatmeal
  • 2 cups of air-popped popcorn

Starting with simple swaps, such as choosing oatmeal over refined cereal or brown rice over white rice, can help you get more whole grains in your diet.

Here are a few practical ideas to help you get started:

  • oatmeal instead of sugary cereal
  • brown rice instead of white rice
  • whole wheat bread instead of white bread
  • farro or quinoa instead of refined grains
  • air-popped popcorn instead of chips

Routhenstein recommended choosing palatable, tolerable, and easily accessible whole grains.

“This can look like oats, bulgur, whole wheat pasta, whole grain bread, quinoa, and farro,” she said. “If you are gluten-free, you can choose options like quinoa, brown rice, amaranth, and gluten-free oats.”

Remember that substitution is key. Simply adding more grain-based foods to your diet may not provide the same health benefits as replacing refined grains with whole grains.

“The goal isn’t to make one perfect dietary choice, but to build healthy habits that can be maintained over the long term,” Chen said. “Small, consistent changes like choosing whole-grain bread, brown rice, oatmeal, or whole-grain pasta instead of refined alternatives can add up and contribute to better cardiovascular health over time.”

#5050. Women, Young Adults Experience Fatigue More Often, Says CDC

📅 25 Oct 2026 | Category: other | Source
  • A recent report from the CDC’s National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) showed that nearly 3 in 4 adults in the United States report experiencing fatigue.
  • Women and younger adults reported the highest rates of frequent fatigue.
  • Adults with frequent fatigue were more likely to report symptoms of depression and anxiety, as well as cognitive difficulties.
  • Frequent fatigue was also associated with limitations in work and social activities.

Fatigue is common among adults in the United States, according to a report published on September 3 from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)’s National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS).

The NCHS found that 71.5% of adults reported experiencing fatigue on at least some days, with 10.7% reporting it on most days.

Frequent fatigue, meaning most days or every day, was reported by 16.6% of adults. 

Fatigue is more than just feeling drowsy or tired. It is an extreme sense of tiredness and lack of energy that can interfere with your daily activities and may not improve with sleep.

“The findings are concerning, but not surprising. Many Americans are trying to balance work, family responsibilities, financial pressures, and ongoing uncertainty about the future,” said Matt Glowiak, PhD, and chief addiction specialist with Recovered. Glowiak wasn’t involved in the study. 

The report didn’t look at the underlying causes of frequent fatigue. However, experts say stress is one of many factors that can contribute to fatigue.

Women and young adults experience fatigue more often

The NCHS report used data that were collected in the 2024 National Health Interview Survey to estimate fatigue symptoms among adults ages 18 and older. 

It found that women were more likely than men to experience frequent fatigue, with 20% reporting it. Younger adults were also more likely to report frequent fatigue, with 19.5% of those ages 18–34 experiencing it. 

“Women and young adults often face unique pressures that may contribute to fatigue. Many women balance multiple caregiving, professional, and personal responsibilities, while young adults are navigating major life transitions, financial uncertainty, and high social expectations,” said Glowiak. 

“Both groups may also be more aware of and willing to acknowledge their experiences with mental health and well-being, which can influence how fatigue is recognized and reported.”

The report also found differences in frequent fatigue by race and ethnicity:

  • American Indian and Alaska Native adults (25.5%)
  • white adults (18%)
  • Black adults (17.3%)
  • Hispanic adults (12.9%)

Frequent fatigue was also more common in certain regions, with 17.7% in the South and 17.5% in the Midwest reporting it.

Frequent fatigue was also more common in rural areas (20.5%) than in urban areas (14.5%).

Fatigue can affect daily functioning

Individuals with frequent fatigue also often reported other functioning difficulties, including: 

  • 69.6% reported symptoms of anxiety
  • 46.1% reported cognitive difficulty
  • 45% reported symptoms of depression

Compared with people without frequent fatigue, those who experienced frequent fatigue were more than three times as likely to have symptoms of depression and twice as likely to report cognitive difficulties and anxiety symptoms.

People with frequent fatigue also found that their work and social lives were impacted. Work limitations were reported by 36% of those with frequent fatigue. 

When it came to social activities, 16.2% reported some difficulty with social activities, while 12.8% reported a lot of difficulty or said they couldn’t engage in these activities at all. 

Intensity of fatigue symptoms

The largest proportion of people (45.3%) with frequent fatigue reported that their symptoms lasted some of the day, while 34.5% reported symptoms lasting most of the day, and 20.2% reported symptoms that lasted all day.

The largest proportion (46.8%) also reported that their fatigue was “between a little and a lot.” Others described their fatigue as “a lot” (38.8%) and “a little” (14.5%).

Chronic stress may contribute to fatigue

Fatigue can be a symptom of other health conditions or a side effect of medications. However, it can also be connected to stress. 

“The possible causes of fatigue, both physical and mental, are vast. Even a single person may experience multiple triggers for their fatigue,” said David Cutler, MD, board certified family medicine physician at Providence Saint John’s Health Center in Santa Monica, CA, who wasn’t involved in the study. 

Chronic stress can lead to prolonged changes in stress hormones such as cortisol and adrenaline, which may contribute to fatigue.

The most recent Stress in America Survey by the American Psychological Association (APA) found that a majority of adults felt isolated, left out, and more stressed about the future than before. 

Glowiak agreed with this and added, “When stress becomes chronic, fatigue can become a common part of daily life rather than an occasional experience.”

Fatigue is also a common symptom of menopause

Fatigue during menopause stems from a complex mix of metabolic, hormonal, and psychosocial changes that can disrupt the body’s energy balance. 

Of all the symptoms that can worsen during the course of menopause, fatigue is clinically significant because it can be disabling. 

It is also one of the most common and distressing symptoms.

One study found that 85.3% of postmenopausal women and 46.5% of perimenopausal women reported physical and mental exhaustion.

How to manage fatigue and when to contact a doctor

You may want to consider speaking with a healthcare professional if your fatigue lasts more than a couple of weeks or if it begins to interfere with your daily activities. 

It is also a good idea to check with your doctor if fatigue is severe or comes with other symptoms, such as unexplained weight loss, shortness of breath, or fever. 

Glowiak told Healthline that managing fatigue begins with identifying potential contributors.

Cutler agreed and added that identifying the cause requires a careful, detailed understanding of all aspects of a patient’s life and medical history.

Cutler also added that it is important to validate a person’s experience of fatigue and recognize that the symptom is real. Supportive treatment is important while the diagnostic process seeks to identify an underlying cause.

“Once the source is better understood, people can take intentional steps to address it, such as setting boundaries, improving sleep habits, seeking support, or making lifestyle changes. Awareness is important, but meaningful change often requires action,” Glowiak said.

#5049. Microdosing GLP-1s Like Wegovy, Zepbound May Still Lead to Weight Loss

📅 25 Oct 2026 | Category: other | Source
  • Recent research suggests that people may still lose weight while staying on the lowest starting doses of GLP-1 medications, such as Wegovy or Zepbound, for longer periods.
  • After one year, people taking sustained low dose tirzepatide lost about 5.5% of their body weight, while those taking sustained low dose semaglutide lost about 2.2%.
  • Lower doses were associated with more modest weight loss than higher doses, but sustained low dose semaglutide was also associated with lower rates of some side effects.

People may still be able to experience weight loss by staying on lower starting doses of GLP-1 medications, such as Wegovy or Zepbound, for longer, according to research published in Biology Methods & Protocols on August 31. 

Staying on lower doses of GLP-1 medications for longer may overlap with a practice commonly referred to as “microdosing,” which has become a trend among celebrities and on social media.

However, the study did not establish that participants were intentionally microdosing, and using these medications outside their approved dosing schedules is not FDA- or manufacturer-approved.

Microdosing GLP-1s may still aid in weight loss

The study included electronic health records from 814 individuals who met the criteria for sustained low dose semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy), defined as at least three 0.25 mg doses spanning at least six months. It also included 1,016 individuals who met the criteria for sustained low dose tirzepatide (Mounjaro, Zepbound), defined as 2.5 mg doses spanning at least six months.

They found that those taking sustained low dose tirzepatide for one year saw around a 5.5% weight loss, while those taking sustained low dose semaglutide for the same period saw around a 2.2% weight loss.

The researchers also compared weight loss trajectories between those taking low dose and high dose semaglutide. After matching participants based on age, sex, BMI, and type 2 diabetes status, they found that those on low dose semaglutide experienced 1.7% weight loss while those on high dose semaglutide had about 12% weight loss.

“These findings introduce an important treatment paradigm toward increasing the awareness around ‘microdosing’ for patients whose goal is cardiometabolic health with lesser side effects than typical dose escalation of these medicines and an associated modest sustained weight loss in the 2-7% range from baseline,” Venky Soundararajan, PhD, co-founder, chief scientific officer for nference, and co-author of the study, told Healthline. 

Mir Ali, MD, medical director of MemorialCare Surgical Weight Loss Center at Orange Coast Medical Center in Fountain Valley, CA, who wasn’t involved in the study, said it isn’t surprising to see that people taking GLP-1 medications can lose weight even at the lowest dose.

“I have seen this in my own practice. Although these starter doses are generally intended to help patients adjust to the medication, some people may experience meaningful weight loss even at these lower doses,” he added. 

Soundararajan noted that weight reductions remained evident through the 15-month follow-up. This suggests that the initiation dose may potentially serve as a sustained maintenance strategy over many months, rather than simply a brief stepping stone to a higher dose, particularly for a patient who has already achieved the weight loss and cardiometabolic benefit they are seeking.

However, Ali cautioned, “In my experience, many patients eventually reach a point where they stop losing weight on the lowest dose, although individual responses can vary considerably. Some patients may achieve satisfactory results on a low dose, but most will need some degree of dose escalation to continue losing weight and reach their goals.”

What is the recommended dosing schedule for GLP-1 drugs?

Wegovy (semaglutide) and Zepbound (tirzepatide) are GLP-1 medications approved for weight management as well as other conditions. Other GLP-1s are only approved for the management of certain conditions like type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, kidney disease, and obstructive sleep apnea.

Novo Nordisk, the manufacturer of Wegovy, recommends starting at a weekly dose of 0.25 mg and increasing the dose every 4 weeks. The recommended maintenance dose is 2.4 mg once weekly, although some adults with obesity may increase to 7.2 mg once weekly if additional weight loss is needed.

Eli Lilly, the manufacturer of Zepbound, recommends starting at a weekly dose of 2.5 mg. It then recommends increasing to the next higher dose every 4 weeks until you reach the recommended maintenance dose or the maximum dose of 15 mg. 

Your healthcare professional may adjust how quickly your dose is increased based on your response to the medication and any side effects you experience. Talk with them about the appropriate dosing schedule for you.

How does microdosing affect GLP-1 side effects? 

It is important to keep in mind that while some people experience fewer side effects,  microdosing may not reduce the side effects of GLP-1 medications for everyone. 

Common GLP-1 medication side effects include: 

  • nausea or vomiting
  • reaction at the injection site
  • diarrhea 
  • constipation 

Soundararajan told Healthline that low dose tirzepatide (Zepbound, Mounjaro) was associated with modestly more weight loss, while the overall adverse-event pattern (side effects) significantly favored low dose semaglutide (Wegovy), which may be particularly relevant for individuals prioritizing cardiometabolic health and tolerability over maximal weight reduction. 

“Compared with patients escalating to conventional higher doses, sustained lowest-dose semaglutide was associated with lower 24-month rates of constipation (23.3% vs 36.3%), nausea (39.7% vs 50.1%), and low blood pressure (1.7% vs 5.4%),” he said. 

“This suggests that precision obesity care may increasingly involve asking not only, ‘How much weight can this drug help someone lose?’ but also, ‘What is the lowest dose that achieves this patient’s cardiometabolic goals with the best tolerability?’” 

Ali also noted that these findings reinforce that GLP-1 medications can be effective for weight loss even at their lowest doses. 

“However, the amount of weight loss at these doses tends to be more modest than what is seen with higher maintenance doses. For many people, gradually increasing the dose may be necessary to achieve greater or continued weight loss, but the appropriate dose ultimately depends on the individual’s response, treatment goals, and ability to tolerate the medication,” he added.

#5048. Aerobic Exercise May Improve Brain Health in Postmenopausal Women

📅 25 Oct 2026 | Category: other | Source
  • Regular aerobic exercise can significantly improve thinking skills like planning and decision making in postmenopausal women, according to recent research. 
  • The greatest cognitive benefits were observed in those who engaged in 45 minutes of moderate aerobic activity four times per week.
  • With two-thirds of Alzheimer’s cases occurring in older women, exercising during and after menopause may influence cognitive resilience.

Regular aerobic exercise may help postmenopausal women strengthen executive function, according to new research.

The study, published in the Journal of Alzheimer’s Disease on August 1, suggests that cognitive benefits may appear within 3 to 6 months. This highlights that exercise can be a practical strategy for preserving brain health after menopause.

Brain health strategies are crucial during and after menopause, with two-thirds of cases of Alzheimer’s disease occurring in women. Menopause can influence cognitive decline, as well as resilience. 

Dung Trinh, MD, a physician at MemorialCare Medical Group and Chief Medical Officer of Healthy Brain Clinic in Irvine, CA, who wasn’t involved in the study, said the findings are encouraging, but noted some limitations. 

“This was a secondary analysis of a relatively small study, so I would view it as important evidence that deserves confirmation rather than as definitive proof,” he told Healthline.

Aerobic exercise improves executive function after menopause

The study included 93 cognitively healthy females ages 20 to 67, 43% of whom were postmenopausal. Each participant had below-average cardiorespiratory fitness and didn’t exercise regularly. 

The participants were randomly assigned to groups for either a 6-month aerobic exercise program or a stretching-and-toning program. 

The greatest cognitive benefits were seen in individuals who engaged in 45 minutes of aerobic activity four times a week, who saw improvements in executive function. These cognitive gains were first observed at 3 months and strengthened through the remainder of the program. 

Executive function involves working memory, cognitive flexibility, and inhibitory control. In daily life, this includes managing tasks, making decisions, staying organized, and maintaining independence. 

“Stretching and mobility work have important benefits — namely, flexibility, comfort, range of motion, balance, and stress reduction, to name a few. However, aerobic activity produces a more sustained cardiorespiratory and metabolic stimulus,” said Kecia Gaither, MD, a double board certified OB-GYN and maternal fetal medicine specialist, and associate professor of Clinical Obstetrics and Gynecology at Weill Cornell Medicine. Gaither wasn’t involved in the study.

What counts as moderate aerobic activity? 

Aerobic activity is rhythmic and continuous, increasing your breathing and heart rate. Moderate intensity means you can talk but not sing while participating in the activity. 

Examples of aerobic activities include: 

  • brisk walking
  • cycling
  • water aerobics
  • dancing
  • active gardening, like mowing, raking, or digging
  • doubles tennis or pickleball

How exercise benefits brain health after menopause

Aerobic exercise can affect the brain in various ways.

“Exercise may help by improving cardiovascular fitness, reducing vascular risk factors, and supporting brain plasticity, all of which are relevant because vascular and metabolic health are closely tied to later cognitive decline,” Trinh said.

While aerobic exercise doesn’t prevent Alzheimer’s disease or slow the progression of dementia, it can help improve overall cognitive function as you age.

One of the ways aerobic exercise can affect the brain is in its structure. It can help protect brain regions such as the hippocampus, which is important for memory.

Aerobic exercise can also benefit the brain’s white matter. Regular aerobic activity may support connections in the brain that help with focus and processing information.

Short-term workouts can also have immediate cognitive benefits, like boosting thinking skills right after you work out. 

Gaither told Healthline that aerobic exercise can be extremely helpful in multiple areas, such as:

  • Cerebrovascular function: Aerobic exercise increases cardiac output and challenges vascular regulation. It may improve endothelial function, cerebral perfusion, and increase the brain’s capacity to deliver oxygen and glucose where neural activity is greatest.
  • Metabolic health: Aerobic exercise can improve blood pressure, glucose handling, and lipid profiles — these same cardiometabolic factors are strongly entwined with small vessel brain disease and later cognitive risk. 
  • Inflammation and oxidative stress: Aerobic exercise reduces mechanisms plausibly relevant to brain aging.
  • Brain pathways: Aerobic exercise can help to improve pathways involved in synaptic remodeling, neuronal survival, and learning. 

How menopause affects cognitive function

Cognitive decline appears to occur more frequently in women than in men. This suggests that hormonal factors likely contribute to this decline. 

Cognitive complaints are more common among postmenopausal women than premenopausal women. This may be due in part to reduced ovarian function and decreased serum estrogen levels. 

This leads to menopausal symptoms that have an emphasis on vasomotor symptoms. Vasomotor symptoms include hot flashes, sweating, and dizziness. 

It’s possible that estrogen has a neuroprotective role in the body, which may also help explain why cognitive decline is more common in women, especially those who are postmenopausal. 

“The researchers tried to account for age statistically and still found a menopause-related effect, suggesting the result may not be explained by age alone,” Trinh said. “Menopause and aging are tightly linked, so the study cannot completely separate those influences.” 

Trinh noted that the study highlights a gap in research: Menopause status is often overlooked in exercise and brain-health trials, even though women have a higher lifetime burden of dementia. 

“Future studies with larger samples, better characterization of perimenopause and postmenopause, and Alzheimer’s biomarkers such as amyloid and tau would help determine whether these cognitive improvements translate into lower long-term dementia risk,” he said.

#5047. Think You’re Too Young to Worry About Alzheimer’s? Maria Shriver Says Think Again

📅 25 Oct 2026 | Category: other | Source
  • Author and journalist Maria Shriver is raising awareness about brain health.
  • When her father was diagnosed with Alzheimer’s disease, she made it her mission to inform others about what they can do to lower their risk of cognitive decline.
  • As a longtime advocate for Alzheimer’s, Shriver wants people to take agency over their brain health.

Maria Shriver wants women to know that they’re nearly twice as likely as men to develop Alzheimer’s disease.

As an author, journalist, and founder of the Women’s Alzheimer’s Movement at the Cleveland Clinic, Shriver has been spreading awareness about Alzheimer’s since her father was diagnosed with the disease in 2003.

Now, she’s teamed up with Eli Lilly’s Brain Health Matters campaign to raise awareness about the signs and symptoms of Alzheimer’s disease, and how early detection and intervention can make a big difference.

For Shriver, spreading awareness is a gift. “[Particularly] to women who say that once they find out that they’re vulnerable, they’ll change how they’re walking through their life, but they need the information,” she told Healthline.

She wants everyone to know there are proactive strategies to help support brain health.

“I’m really hopeful that people will actually take away from this, that their brain health matters, that there are things that they can do, and go from… feeling like there’s nothing I can do to feeling there are things I can do,” Shriver said.

Since she first started advocating for Alzheimer’s, the research on brain health has come a long way.

“[There] was no hope, and there was nothing you could do, and you just felt powerless, and you felt it was something that just happened to you as you aged,” said Shriver. “There’s been a sea change in that.”

The science, Shriver said, will only continue to advance. “I do believe that in my lifetime, there will be a cure, but I’m excited about what we know today. I’m excited that the conversation includes brain health,” she said.

Genetic factors that may influence Alzheimer’s risk

Genetics plays a complicated role in Alzheimer’s disease, said Heather Snyder, PhD, senior vice president of Medical & Scientific Operations at the Alzheimer’s Association.

“[There’s] still a lot that we don’t know and a lot that we’re understanding about risk and what may make somebody’s risk higher related to their genes, or lower,” Snyder told Healthline.

For example, having a first-degree relative with Alzheimer’s, such as a parent or sibling, can increase your risk of developing the disease, but does not guarantee it.

“Most people with a family history will not necessarily develop Alzheimer’s,” said Snyder.

Researchers have identified over 100 genetic variants linked to a greater chance of late-onset Alzheimer’s (age 65 and older), the most common form of the disease. The known genetic factors for Alzheimer’s have started influencing treatment and research.

The most well-studied risk gene is ApoE4 (apolipoprotein E4). Snyder shared how the gene may influence risk:

  • You inherit one copy of the gene from each parent.
  • One copy of ApoE4 raises risk; two copies raise it further (with some variation across populations).
  • DNA surrounding the gene may also modify a person’s risk level.
  • Having ApoE4 variants increases risk but doesn’t determine outcome one way or the other.

Another genetic mutation, autosomal dominant, accounts for fewer than 1% of Alzheimer’s cases. People with this gene:

  • are likely to develop Alzheimer’s
  • may develop Alzheimer’s at a similar age to when their affected parent developed it
  • may experience Alzheimer’s onset much younger

If you’re concerned about your own genetic risk, your doctor can connect you with a genetic counselor who can help guide you through the genetic testing process and explain the results.

You can also find a genetic counselor in your area by visiting the National Society of Genetic Counselors.

Can you lower your risk of Alzheimer’s?

While genetic factors are outside of your control, up to 45% of dementia risk could be prevented or delayed with modifiable risk factors.

Most studies on lifestyle modifications and how they affect brain health are about cognitive decline and dementia. “So all causes, not necessarily just Alzheimer’s disease, but other causes of cognitive decline in dementia,” said Snyder.

Conditions like cardiovascular disease and diabetes are linked to increased risk of cognitive decline and dementia, but not everyone with these conditions develops Alzheimer’s.

As with genetics, Snyder said this is a risk factor, not a guarantee, and there is variability in why one person progresses to cognitive decline and dementia while another with similar conditions doesn’t.

However, she noted that research has shown that getting good sleep, knowing and managing your heart and metabolic numbers, and eating a nutritious, balanced diet are beneficial to brain health.

“What we’re seeing now is more translating from those associations into specific recommendations or specific steps that we can take,” Snyder said.

Snyder cited the Latin American Initiative for Lifestyle Intervention to Prevent Cognitive Decline (LatAm-FINGERS) study, which builds on the results of the U.S. Study to Protect Brain Health Through Lifestyle Intervention to Reduce Risk (U.S. POINTER) study.

The findings indicate that lifestyle interventions improved memory, thinking, and overall cognitive function in older adults at risk of dementia across various Latin American countries. The strongest gains were seen in participants receiving structured support and coaching. These interventions included:

  • physical activity
  • healthy eating
  • cognitive training
  • social engagement

Shriver said when it comes to lifestyle factors, she takes what she can control to heart.

“I meditate every morning. I pray; that’s my go-to meditation, and I exercise kind of first thing every morning,” she said. “Those are my three kinds of non-negotiables — and coffee.”

She also prioritizes challenging her brain by learning new things at work and playing games.

“I’m now kind of deep in Mahjong,” Shriver said. “[That] brings me to social engagement. I’m in a Mahjong group with younger people, so that helps me keep and make new friends, which is also an important thing.”

Shriver continues her father’s mission to find a cure

Shriver believes her father would be amazed at how far science has come, but she said he would be more focused on pushing her to keep making a difference.

“[He’d] tell me to get on it and, you know, find the cure,” Shriver said. “So I think that’s kind of how I am, as well…let’s fund the research that will get us to the finish line.”

She feels both hope and urgency as she advocates for more answers and new treatments.

“That’s why I think it’s really important to continually talk about [the] information available to us today that did not exist five years ago,” said Shriver.

Informing adults about the importance of brain health during midlife, she said, is her top priority.

“Alzheimer’s is a disease of midlife,” she said. “Alzheimer’s, or other dementias, are in your brain for more than 20 years before you’re symptomatic, so if you’re in your 30s, or your 40s, or your 50s, or your 60s, it’s important to be focused on your brain health now, not sit there and say, Oh, I can focus on my brain health when I’m 70 or when I’m 80.”

Snyder agreed. She encourages people to prioritize a healthy lifestyle to help lower their risk of dementia.

As researchers explore the disease’s pathology, new screening options have become available.

In the last several years, four new blood tests were cleared by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to improve the diagnosis of Alzheimer’s. Three new treatments were also FDA-approved. Additionally, the number of clinical trials studying Alzheimer’s increases yearly.

“It’s incredibly exciting when you think about that landscape,” said Snyder. “Then you [add in] behavioral interventions and the idea that we might be able to do things to protect our brains before we see disease or before we see the cognitive changes, and you can see incredible momentum for [change].”
