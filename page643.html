#36121. A fungus is threatening the world’s food supply. Scientists just found a new defense

📅 15 Oct 2026 | Category: technology | Source

Scientists have uncovered a promising new way to protect some of the world's most important food crops from a destructive fungus that threatens global food security. By engineering the natural immune defenses of plants, researchers have developed an experimental approach that could eventually help wheat, rice, and barley resist fungal infections more effectively.

The international research team, led by scientists at the John Innes Centre in the United Kingdom, investigated a recently discovered group of plant immune receptors. They identified how these proteins recognize invading pathogens and demonstrated that they can be modified to detect multiple fungal threats. The findings could open the door to crops with stronger, more durable disease resistance.

"We've discovered something that could lead to a potential intervention on what is the worst fungal disease of cereals," said Professor Mark Banfield, a research group leader at the John Innes Centre and an author of the study, which involved collaborators at Kobe University in Japan.

A Devastating Fungus Threatens the Global Food Supply

The target of the research is Magnaporthe oryzae, the fungus responsible for blast disease. It causes the most serious fungal disease affecting cultivated rice and has also become a major threat to wheat and barley in parts of Asia and Africa.

The damage is enormous. Each year, rice blast destroys enough rice to feed approximately 60 million people. As the fungus spreads into additional agricultural regions, developing effective ways to protect crops has become increasingly important for global food security.

The fungus infects plants through a sophisticated biological strategy. It delivers specialized molecules known as effectors into cells in the leaves and stems. These molecules interfere with normal plant functions, allowing the pathogen to establish an infection and promote disease.

Plants, however, have evolved their own defense systems. Specialized immune receptors can recognize these foreign molecules and trigger a protective response. One important mechanism involves causing infected cells to die in a controlled, localized area, helping prevent the pathogen from spreading farther through the plant.

Scientists Discover a Hidden Plant Immune Defense

For more than three decades, much of the research into plant immunity has centered on proteins called Nucleotide-binding and leucine-rich receptors (NLRs). These receptors have played a central role in understanding how crops recognize and defend themselves against blast disease and other infections.

More recently, scientists have begun investigating another group of immune proteins found in cereal crops, known as tandem kinase proteins (TKPs). Although researchers are still learning how these receptors operate, they appear to offer additional opportunities for strengthening plant defenses.

Earlier studies revealed that some TKPs contain a specialized region called a Heavy Metal Associated (HMA) domain. Similar regions are also found in certain NLR receptors, where they help recognize pathogen effectors and initiate immune responses.

This shared feature attracted the attention of Banfield and his colleagues because it suggested that scientists might be able to redesign the receptors to recognize additional fungal threats.

"This piqued our interest," said Professor Banfield. "If we can engineer these integrated HMA domains for new properties by making amino acid protein changes where effectors bind, we can gain what we call novel recognition specificities - and potentially a new frontline for defense of plants against disease."

Turning Fungal Attack Molecules Into Targets

In the study, published in Science Advances, researchers examined precisely how HMA domains interact with the molecules released by the blast fungus.

Using biophysical techniques and crystallography, a method that allows scientists to determine the detailed three-dimensional structure of molecules, the team obtained a high-resolution view of how these plant receptors bind to fungal effectors.

The results revealed that HMA domains can function as biological bait. By binding to molecules the fungus uses during infection, these regions help plants recognize the presence of an invading pathogen and activate their defenses.

Armed with this structural information, the researchers modified TKP immune receptors so they could recognize two different fungal effectors associated with infections in wheat and barley.

This ability to detect multiple targets, known as dual specificity, is unusual in naturally occurring immune receptors. Demonstrating that it can be introduced through protein engineering suggests a promising way to expand the range of threats that crop immune systems can recognize.

The findings also provide a foundation for investigating other recently identified TKPs in different cereal crops. Scientists may eventually be able to design customized immune receptors that detect several fungal effectors simultaneously, potentially making plants more difficult for pathogens to infect.

Engineering Crops With Stronger Disease Resistance

Although the findings are encouraging, the technology remains at an early experimental stage.

The researchers conducted their initial tests using protoplasts, individual wheat cells that serve as a simplified model for studying how plant tissues respond. The next step is to determine whether the engineered receptors can provide effective protection in whole plants grown under controlled greenhouse conditions.

"Our study indicates that this new class of resistance proteins is amenable for engineering purposes. We now know that the previous tools we used in engineering NLR receptors may also work with this new class," said Dr. Daniel Yu, the first author of the study.

Understanding exactly how fungal effectors attach to plant immune receptors could also help researchers take advantage of other technologies. Precision breeding and artificial intelligence may provide additional ways to redesign HMA domains, allowing them to recognize pathogen molecules that previously escaped detection.

One particularly promising possibility involves combining different types of immune receptors within the same crop.

Because NLRs and TKPs appear to activate plant defenses through different mechanisms, using them together could create several layers of protection. This might make it harder for fungi to evolve ways around a plant's immune system.

"We think these two classes of immune receptors, NLRs and TKPs, are probably activated in different ways," said Professor Banfield. "That gives the potential for more robust immunity in the field because you are not putting all your eggs in one basket. Further down the road, we may be able to use techniques to stack genes encoding TKPs and NLRs together against the same disease, making a super-resistant crop. They will have different ways of activating immunity, so it is likely to be more difficult for a pathogen to evolve away from," he added.

A Growing Threat to Wheat, Rice, and Barley

The need for better protection is becoming more urgent as blast disease continues to threaten major food crops.

Wheat blast was first identified in Brazil during the 1980s and has since spread to parts of Asia and Africa. Although the disease has not been detected in the United Kingdom, climate change could eventually create conditions that allow it to threaten cereal production in northern Europe.

For farmers, the emergence of blast disease in new regions presents a serious challenge, particularly because the fungus can threaten crops that are essential to feeding large populations.

By revealing how a previously underexplored class of plant immune receptors can be engineered to recognize multiple fungal threats, the researchers have identified a possible new strategy for protecting future harvests.

Further testing will be necessary to establish whether these modified defenses work effectively in whole plants and under agricultural conditions. If successful, combining different immune receptors could eventually help scientists develop crops with more resilient protection against one of agriculture's most destructive fungal diseases.

#36111. Veterinarios coinciden sobre el apego seguro de los gatos: «Muchas veces se nos olvidan cuáles son los pasos que tenemos que dar para aproximarnos a un gato»

📅 15 Oct 2026 | Category: business | Source

El veterinario y divulgador Carlos Gutiérrez recuerda que el vínculo con un gato no depende de gestos inmediatos ni de ideas romantizadas, sino de respeto, paciencia y una buena aproximación

No todos los gatos muestran afecto de la misma manera ni se acercan igual a las personas. De hecho, el veterinario y divulgador Carlos Gutiérrez insiste en una idea clave que a menudo se pasa por alto: para que un gato confíe, primero hay que saber cómo aproximarse a él. De ahí que recuerde que muchas veces se olvidan los pasos básicos necesarios para construir un vínculo sano con un felino.

Frente a la imagen del gato como un animal completamente independiente, Gutiérrez explica que los felinos sí desarrollan lazos con sus cuidadores, lo que se conoce como apego seguro. Este tipo de vínculo implica que el gato percibe a la persona como una figura estable y predecible, alguien que no supone una amenaza y que le ofrece seguridad. No se trata solo de alimento o rutina, sino de una relación basada en la confianza.

Ese apego, sin embargo, no se forma igual en todos los casos. Influyen factores como la socialización en las primeras semanas de vida, el carácter del animal o sus experiencias previas. Hay gatos que se muestran más abiertos desde el primer momento, mientras que otros necesitan más tiempo para observar y adaptarse. Esto se ve con claridad en refugios, donde los animales más tímidos suelen recibir menos atención, pese a tener el mismo potencial para generar un vínculo fuerte.

En este sentido, el especialista también cuestiona la idea extendida de que “el gato elige a su humano”. Aunque puede haber una conexión inicial, reducir la relación a ese momento puede llevar a ignorar a muchos gatos que no se acercan de forma inmediata. Según explica, estos animales no son menos sociables, sino que requieren una aproximación más pausada.

La llegada al hogar es otro punto clave. Incluso un gato que parecía confiado en el refugio puede mostrarse reservado en un entorno nuevo. Por eso, Gutiérrez recomienda no forzar el contacto y permitir que el animal explore a su ritmo. Lo más adecuado es ofrecerle un espacio tranquilo, con acceso fácil a comida, agua y refugio, y dejar que sea él quien dé el primer paso.

A partir de ahí, el vínculo se construye con pequeñas interacciones positivas: responder cuando el gato se acerca, ofrecer caricias suaves en zonas que le resulten agradables o fomentar el juego sin invadir su espacio. Evitar perseguirlo o insistir cuando se retira es fundamental para no generar rechazo.

El experto recuerda además que el afecto felino no siempre se expresa de forma evidente. Un gato puede confiar plenamente en su cuidador sin necesidad de buscar contacto constante. Entender esta forma de relacionarse ayuda a evitar frustraciones y a construir una convivencia más equilibrada.

Respeto, paciencia y atención a las señales del animal son, según Carlos Gutiérrez, los pilares para desarrollar un apego seguro. Más que esperar una respuesta inmediata, se trata de crear las condiciones para que el gato, a su ritmo, encuentre en su entorno y en su humano un espacio de confianza.
