#29812. Judge curbs Trump administration's noncitizen voting probes

📅 22 Oct 2026 | Category: other | Source

A federal judge restricted Department of Homeland Security investigations into suspected noncitizen voting in nine states, citing concerns that the agency's tactics could intimidate eligible voters.

Why it matters: With the midterms less than two weeks away, the ruling curtails a central part of the Trump administration's efforts to investigate suspected noncitizen voting.

Driving the news: The ruling, issued late Wednesday, blocks election-related field operations and interviews under the government's Unlawful Voter Initiative that involve or are likely to involve U.S. citizens.

  • California-based U.S. District Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín also blocked the administration from conducting operations against likely citizens in states relying on records generated by its voter-matching policy.

Zoom in: Martínez-Olguín wrote in her order that the groups that sued provided "substantial evidence" that the government's "intimidating conduct" was "directly interfering with their members' right to encourage voters to participate in the 2026 midterm elections."

  • Her order stated that DHS had compiled a "target list" of individuals from a variety of sources, including state voter rolls and commercial data.
  • But Martínez-Olguín cast doubt on the accuracy of the administration's data, warning of a "high likelihood of error in review because there is no federal database containing citizenship status of U.S. residents."
  • Court filings indicated that officers were ordered to prioritize probes in California, Connecticut, Georgia, Missouri, Nevada, New York, Pennsylvania, Washington and Wisconsin.

Catch up quick: Democratic Sens. Chuck Schumer (N.Y.) and Alex Padilla (Calif.) released a whistleblower report last month alleging that officials had diverted fraud and national security personnel to the Unlawful Voter Initiative using "highly questionable data" and methods.

  • The whistleblower disclosure alleged that officers were directed, in violation of state law, to access sensitive voter information under minimal training and significant pressure.

The other side: "DHS is laser focused on identifying, prosecuting, and removing aliens who vote and dilute the votes of U.S. citizens," a spokesperson told Axios in a statement, adding that the department has "no plans to question or investigate people we believe to be U.S. citizens."

  • Responding to the nonprofits and local government entities that sued, the administration argued in court that DHS "reasonably decided to make use of the best data it had available to it to perform its law enforcement role, notwithstanding that the information may not be perfect in all cases."
  • The administration also contended that the allegations of voter intimidation were too vague.
  • The White House referred Axios to DHS for comment.

Zoom out: Courts have repeatedly blocked the Trump administration's attempts to obtain state voter data, but the administration has pursued suspected noncitizen voting — which is rare — through other means.

  • Last week, the administration sued California and election officials over claims that suspected ineligible voters received mail-in ballots for the election, asking a court to delay about 20,000 voters from having their ballots counted.
  • But as Axios' Brittany Gibson notes, administration-created lists of potential noncitizens on voter rolls have been found less than accurate before, including in Nevada.

What we're watching: The administration faces continuing legal challenges to its election-related initiatives ahead of the Nov. 3 midterms.

  • On Tuesday, Democratic groups sued the government, accusing it of using unfounded fraud claims as a cudgel to insert itself into election administration.

#29813. Trump ordered to sit for deposition in $10 billion BBC lawsuit

📅 22 Oct 2026 | Category: other | Source

A federal judge on Thursday ordered President Trump to sit for a deposition by Dec. 11 in his $10 billion defamation lawsuit against the BBC over an edit of one of his speeches that the president's lawyers say was misleading.

Why it matters: The order will likely force Trump to answer questions under oath in a legal battle he started.

Driving the news: Judge Enjoliqué Lett ordered Trump to appear by Dec. 11, after lawyers for the BBC argued that Trump is improperly delaying his appearance.

  • In a filing published Wednesday, Trump's counsel said the president could appear between Jan. 11 and Jan. 22, 2027, and argued that his presidential duties prevent him from sitting for the deposition before then.
  • They also allege the BBC is "wrong to claim that President Trump is avoiding his deposition in this action."
  • The president was originally scheduled to appear for the deposition on Oct. 16. However, his lawyers said, that appearance was canceled because Trump had to attend to "matters that have significantly impacted the United States' national security and economy," including the wars in Iran and Ukraine and the White House Accord on Super Intelligence.

The other side: BBC attorneys say the president was campaigning in Georgia on Oct. 16, as reflected in a White House press pool report that includes his travel plans.

  • The BBC argues the president chose political activity over appearing for the original deposition date.
  • According to Trump's daily schedule, he attended an intelligence briefing and had executive time before departing for a rally in Georgia and then attended an RNC event in Palm Beach, Florida, that evening.

The White House referred Axios to Trump's personal counsel.

  • A spokesperson for Trump's legal team told Axios in an emailed statement that the BBC "defamed President Trump by intentionally, maliciously, and deceptively doctoring his speech in a brazen attempt to interfere in the 2024 Presidential Election."

The BBC's legal team did not immediately respond to Axios' request for comment on the accusations Trump's legal team made in its statement.

  • When Axios perviously asked for comment, the BBC's attorneys told Axios they "have nothing to add."

Catch up quick: The defamation suit stems from a documentary the BBC aired that spliced together clips from Trump's speech on Jan. 6, 2021, the day of the riots at the U.S. Capitol.

  • The president claimed the edit was defamatory and demanded the BBC make amends by providing financial compensation, in addition to an apology and a retraction.
  • The BBC later said in a correction note that the clips "unintentionally created the impression that we were showing a single continuous section of the speech, rather than excerpts from different points in the speech, and that this gave the mistaken impression that President Trump had made a direct call for violent action."
  • Two top leaders at BBC later resigned over the backlash.
