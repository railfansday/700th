#5006. ADHD Medications May Help Ease Menopausal Brain Fog

📅 25 Oct 2026 | Category: other | Source
  • A recent review found that ADHD medications like lisdexamfetamine (Vyvanse) and atomoxetine (Strattera) may help improve self-reported symptoms of brain fog in perimenopausal and postmenopausal women.
  • Menopausal brain fog — including forgetfulness, slow thinking, and difficulty concentrating — may be linked to hormonal fluctuations that affect brain function during perimenopause and menopause.
  • Experts caution it’s too early to recommend ADHD medications for menopausal brain fog due to limited research, potential risks like cardiovascular effects, and lack of FDA approval for this use.

Medications commonly used to treat attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) may help reduce certain symptoms of menopausal brain fog, according to a review presented at the 2026 Annual Meeting of the Menopause Society. The findings have not yet been published in a peer-reviewed journal.

The research suggests that both lisdexamfetamine (Vyvanse) and atomoxetine (Strattera) may improve self-reported executive function in perimenopausal and postmenopausal people. However, they showed little to no effect on objective cognitive tests.

While this scoping review showed promising results, experts caution that it may be too early to begin recommending these medications to females experiencing menopause. 

Sara Vaughn, MD, Chief Medical Officer at Clair Health, who wasn’t involved in the study, told Healthline that these findings are interesting and hypothesis-generating, but they do not yet establish ADHD medications as a treatment for menopausal brain fog.

Connection between ADHD-like symptoms and menopause

Many middle-aged females report experiencing forgetfulness, slow thinking, reduced concentration, and difficulty finding words. This is often referred to as “brain fog,” and is a common occurrence during various stages of menopause. 

These symptoms of brain fog also mirror the symptoms of ADHD.  

Brain fog may be due to declining or fluctuating levels of estrogen during perimenopause, which affects dopamine and norepinephrine signaling in the prefrontal cortex and the hippocampus. This can impact memory, focus, and executive functioning. 

Despite how disruptive these symptoms can be to quality of life, there is currently no medication approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to manage them. This often leaves clinicians in a position of trying to differentiate between brain fog from menopause and undiagnosed ADHD. 

The review analyzed five studies involving females ages 45–60 with their last menstrual period within the past 10 years. The studies all focused on how ADHD medications affect outcome measures of cognition and ADHD severity. 

The researchers found that with lisdexamfetamine, scores on the Brown Attention Deficit Disorder Scale (BADDS), a self-report rating scale that measures ADHD symptoms, dropped from 35.7 to 21.2, compared with 29.8 with a placebo. With atomoxetine, BADDS scores decreased from 38.6 to 25.5, compared with 30.1 with a placebo.

There was no significant benefit observed on objective cognitive tests (ex., memory and processing speed). 

These findings suggest that certain ADHD medications may improve perceived cognitive symptoms during menopause, although benefits were not demonstrated on objective cognitive tests.

Jennifer Tat, a medical student at the University of Tennessee Health Sciences Center and lead author of the study, noted, “In the population data, symptom severity, not a formal ADHD diagnosis, predicted menopausal burden. Many women were never diagnosed, so relying on a chart diagnosis misses the women most at risk.”

“We have evidence that the menopause transition is associated with measurable brain changes,” said Vaughn. “But no specific brain signature of menopausal brain fog has been established. To study it rigorously, we first need to define what the term means and measure it consistently.”

Why experts say it may be too soon to recommend these medications

Limitations of this review include the small number of studies and the small sample sizes. Most of the measures were also self-reported, which leaves room for error. 

Also, the FDA has not approved these types of medications for menopausal brain fog, meaning using them for these purposes would be off-label. 

“ADHD medications are not without risks, and I think it is too early to use them routinely for menopausal ‘brain fog,’” said Vaughn. 

ADHD medications can carry risks, including cardiovascular side effects. Stimulants such as lisdexamfetamine also have the potential for misuse and dependence. These medications should only be used with medical supervision.

“I am a strong proponent of treating the underlying disorder rather than treating isolated symptoms. ADHD and menopausal cognitive symptoms may look alike, but that does not mean they share the same underlying mechanism—or require the same treatment,” noted Vaughn.

Further research, including additional long-term prospective studies, is needed before clinical guidance can be changed

“We hope this opens up the conversations with patients and providers. We recommend clinicians to screen patients for ADHD and, for those who screen positive, refer them to our psychiatric colleagues who have the knowledge and resources to perform a diagnostic test so that those women who have gone undiagnosed with ADHD can get the treatment they need and deserve,” said Tat.  

Tips to help manage menopausal brain fog 

According to the National Council on Aging (NCOA), your brain requires exercise to function optimally, just like your muscles do. 

Ways to help keep your brain sharp and manage menopausal brain fog include: 

  • eating a nutrient-rich diet, such as the MIND diet 
  • exercising regularly
  • quitting or avoiding smoking
  • limiting your alcohol intake

The NCOA also suggests keeping your brain well-exercised by: 

  • doing a mental exercise each day, such as a crossword, sudoku, or a word search 
  • taking up a new activity, such as gardening, knitting, or anything else involving physical coordination
  • memorizing your shopping list before going to the store
  • engaging in games that stretch your brain, such as chess or bridge

You can also speak with your healthcare professional about how menopause, estrogen levels, hormone therapy, and modifiable risk factors like a sedentary lifestyle or high blood pressure can affect cognitive health. 

“I want clinicians to know that they must take brain fog seriously. Ask whether the symptoms are new or a lifelong pattern that menopause has worsened. ADHD in women often goes unrecognized for decades,” said Tat.

#5005. Letter from the Editor-in-Chief: The Good and the Hard of Life on GLP-1s

📅 25 Oct 2026 | Category: other | Source
  • GLP-1 medications like semaglutide and tirzepatide can lead to significant weight loss and may help reduce inflammation, improve mobility, and quiet persistent thoughts about food. Many people report life changing results after years of unsuccessful diets.
  • A Healthline story on Mayim Bialik’s GLP-1 side effects became our most engaged Facebook post of the year, as thousands of commenters shared hard side effects and life changing results. Research also increasingly links GLP-1s to benefits beyond weight loss.
  • Healthline’s Editor-in-Chief Robin Hough talks with his mom, Kitty, who lost 56 pounds on tirzepatide, and a registered nurse who has lost over 50 pounds, about the benefits, side effects, and cost of long-term treatment.

“Years of diets that worked for a couple of months, then gave all the weight back and more. Now the food noise has gone quiet,” a GLP-1 user shared.

In June, our brilliant News team published a piece about actor Mayim Bialik and her challenging experience with GLP-1 side effects. It did well, like many of the celebrity + health condition + human-interest angle features we create tend to do with our audience (that’s you all reading this!). Not a massive viral hit by any measure, but a strong performer on the traffic front.

Life in a busy newsroom went on. 

Jump forward nearly 4 months; our equally brilliant social media team posts the piece on Facebook, leading with one of Bialik’s quotes from the article: “GLP-1s have helped people in serious need. Of that I am certain. But nobody talks much about what happens when it goes wrong.” 

The fuse was lit. Nearly 12k likes, 1k reposts, and 3.5k comments later, the team had their most engaged post of the year by some margin. The irony is not lost on me that Bialik’s quote, about how rarely people talk about side effects, sparked such a deep conversation about that very thing. 

GLP-1s are a big topic for Healthline. They drive traffic, they drive revenue, and they offer a rich seam of human-centric storytelling for our teams. Even so, no one predicted the post would catch fire the way it did. Our social team’s read is that a few things stacked up: Mayim Bialik is a name our Facebook audience knows, the topic is one where nearly everyone has a stake, and a busy thread tends to get shown to more people, who then add more comments. That’s the hypothesis. What we’ve consistently seen is that posts that lead with a quote get more people talking than those that lead with a headline. You may have noticed this article takes the same approach.

What the GLP-1 conversation on Facebook revealed

We also know that the comment sections on social media aren’t always the most hospitable of environments, particularly in the health space. What stood out about the lion’s share of the comments we read was how constructive the discourse was. People were leading with deeply personal and often moving perspectives about the life changing impact (for good and bad) of GLP-1s. In turn, they were met with equally balanced and thoughtful responses. 

Many of the comments expanded on Bialik’s experience, detailing long and difficult battles with a range of side effects. But plenty told the other side of the story: years of failed diets and struggles with chronic conditions leading to despondency, side effects that went on to ease with time, and life changing results that were ultimately worth the cost.

My mom’s experience with tirzepatide

The line that opened this piece could have come from any of those commenters. It didn’t. It came from Kitty Hough, who is 75 and lives in London. Eagle-eyed readers will have spotted that she has the same surname as me – that’s because she’s my mom. She’s one of two people closer to the Healthline home who spoke to me for this piece and whose stories echo the Facebook post thread, with the good and the hard bits side by side.

Kitty was a hockey player and distance runner as a girl, the kind who could eat what she liked without it showing. Then came children, and a weight that crept up year after year, with decades of diets that “worked for a couple of months only to be back to my former weight and then some.” In late 2024, after a lot of research, she decided to try tirzepatide.

Since then, Kitty has lost 56 pounds. She says her joints hurt less, which she puts down to reduced inflammation. She is more mobile. The constant chatter about food has faded. She thinks harder before she eats now. 

“I am a strong believer that obesity is a disease and in my case certainly not the result of overindulgence or laziness,” she says. “GLP-1s have greatly improved my health and self-esteem. After years of struggling with my weight, I have finally found a way to solve the problem.”

Kitty will also tell you what it cost. Nausea. Constipation. Sulfur-tasting burps and rough nights of indigestion. Initial hair loss that has since grown back, and loose skin. Muscle loss. And she misses some of the true joy of cooking and eating food. 

But despite bumps in her journey, Kitty remains convinced that the benefits of GLP-1s for her life and health outweigh any drawbacks she’s experienced. “I am conscious that I will probably be on this drug for a long time if not for life. It is truly a miracle drug.” 

A healthcare professional’s view on the cost of GLP-1s

I also spoke to Ann Marie Maguire, AGNP-C, RN, TCTCN, a nurse practitioner specializing in transplantation and cellular therapy, the sister of Healthline’s Chief Business Officer Christine Macguire, and someone who has lost over 50lbs since starting GLP-1s in 2022. 

“I struggled with my weight since my teens and always believed I should be able to manage it on my own. But in my 30s, my knees were hurting just getting out of bed or walking to dinner. I realized how much my weight was affecting my quality of life. Starting a GLP-1 in 2022, alongside healthier eating and exercise, made a tremendous difference. My only regret is not starting sooner,” said Ann Marie. 

As a healthcare professional, Ann Marie also identified cost as a major factor that can’t be ignored regarding GLP-1s and their impact. 

“There’s understandable debate about the long-term use and cost of these medications,” she says. “But we also need to consider the health and financial burden of obesity, including cardiovascular complications, hospitalizations, surgeries and ongoing medications. The cost conversation should account for the potential benefits of preventing or reducing those complications.”

Ann Marie points to a frustration many patients will recognize: reaching a healthier weight can become the reason coverage gets cut off. “We don’t automatically stop blood pressure medication because a patient’s readings improve,” she says. The treatment may be what’s keeping the condition controlled. Obesity, she argues, deserves the same approach. “Reaching a goal weight can mean treatment is working, and decisions about continuing it should reflect the patient’s ongoing needs, benefits and side effects.”

The bigger picture on GLP-1s

It’s important to note that this article looks at the experiences of only a handful of people out of the millions who now take GLP-1s. Like all drugs, these medications are not right for everyone. Side effects are common; some of them can be serious, and both the long-term impact and what happens when people stop are still being worked out. Ultimately, your care team, with knowledge of your medical history, is a far better guide than a comment thread.

Yet the evidence now reaches well beyond the scales: large trials have linked semaglutide to fewer heart attacks and strokes in people with heart disease and slower kidney decline in people with diabetes and kidney disease, and tirzepatide became the first FDA-approved drug for moderate to severe sleep apnea in adults with obesity.

Stories like Kitty’s, Ann Marie’s, Mayim Bialik’s, and those of the 3.5k commenters on the Facebook post aren’t proof of what will happen to you one way or another. But for a lot of people, my mom included, these drugs have changed what an ordinary day looks like, and in many cases changed what their future can look like. 

One of the most significant drug developments of the century so far? Hard to argue with. 

Robin Hough is the Editor-in-Chief of Healthline, and in addition to this regular Letter from the Editor column, he can be found every other Wednesday in our Healthline Wellness Wire newsletter.

#5004. Getting More Physical Activity May Help With Your Perimenopause Symptoms

📅 25 Oct 2026 | Category: other | Source
  • A new study reports that exercising more frequently may help alleviate perimenopause symptoms. 
  • While increased physical activity was linked to improved perimenopause symptoms overall, the strongest association was with psychological symptoms like anxiety.
  • Finding exercises you enjoy and creating accountability can help increase your physical activity level.

A new study found that exercising more frequently may help alleviate perimenopause symptoms like hot flashes, anxiety, and urinary issues. 

The study results were recently presented at The Menopause Society’s 2026 Annual Conference. 

Perimenopause is the transitional period leading up to menopause. 

Due to hormonal changes that mark the end of a person’s reproductive years, symptoms like hot flashes, mood swings, brain fog, insomnia, and urinary incontinence are common.

Past studies show that lifestyle modifications, such as eating a healthy diet, managing stress, and getting enough physical activity, can help reduce some perimenopause symptoms. 

“Physical activity is low cost, accessible, and already known to benefit sleep, mood, and heart and bone health — all areas that matter a great deal during perimenopause,” said study lead and co-author Liudmila Zhaunova, PhD, director of science at Flo Health — the developer of a women’s health tracking app, which was used to collect health data for this study. “But when we looked at the research, there was surprisingly little evidence specific to perimenopause.”

“Hormone therapy isn’t suitable or desired for everyone, so women deserve a broader set of evidence-based options,” Zhaunova told Healthline. “The more we understand about accessible, lifestyle-based approaches, the better equipped women and their healthcare providers are to build a plan that works for them.”

Higher rates of exercise lower perimenopause symptoms overall

Study researchers recruited more than 1,800 women from around the globe who were using the Flo app. 

Study participants were asked to complete online surveys about their physical activity, from not being physically active to exercising more than 5 days a week. 

Participants were also asked to report on their perimenopause symptoms in three categories — somatic (body-based issues like hot flashes), psychological (mental health issues like mood changes), and urogenital (issues regarding the urinary or vaginal areas). Researchers rated the severity of these symptoms using the Menopause Rating Scale (MRS).

The researchers found that higher rates of physical activity were associated with fewer perimenopausal symptoms across all three categories. 

“Perimenopause symptoms are wide-ranging, and women often experience several at once,” Zhaunova said. “Seeing a lower symptom burden across all three domains suggests that being more active may be relevant to women’s overall experience, not just one symptom.” 

Mental health benefits of exercise were most pronounced

Additionally, the scientists discovered that the greatest exercise benefit was observed in the reduction of perimenopausal psychological symptoms. 

“Psychological symptoms, such as low mood, irritability, anxiety, and exhaustion, are among the most disruptive parts of perimenopause, and they’re often overlooked or not recognized as related to this life stage,” Zhaunova explained.  

“Our findings suggest that physical activity may matter most for mood and anxiety-related symptoms,” she continued.

“This could help women and clinicians have more informed conversations about how to support mental well-being during perimenopause. That said, women with more symptoms may find it harder to exercise, so we need further research to understand the direction of the relationship.”

How exercise may improve perimenopause symptoms

Sherry Ross, MD, a board certified OB-GYN and Women’s Health Expert at Providence Saint John’s Health Center in Santa Monica, CA, said that regular exercise could potentially help relieve perimenopause symptoms like hot flashes and mood changes. Ross wasn’t involved in this study, but noted other benefits of exercising during this life stage:

  • body flexibility and strength
  • body image and self-esteem
  • bone health
  • cardiovascular health
  • energy levels
  • immune system strength
  • mental health
  • sleep quality
  • weight management

“Exercise releases endorphins, improves blood vessel health, lowers inflammation, helps reset circadian rhythm, and improves the body’s internal cooling system,” Ross told Healthline. “Collectively, these changes ease anxiety, irritability, mood changes, sleep, and fatigue.”

“There were mixed reviews on how exercise benefits hot flashes, but exercise does help with weight control, which may benefit hot flashes,” she said. “Strengthening the pelvic floor and core may help with the urinary symptoms women many experience in perimenopause.”

Exercise tips for perimenopausal women

Prudence Hall, MD, an OB-GYN in private practice in Santa Monica, CA, and author of “Radiant Again & Forever,” said that building a sustainable exercise routine requires consistency, joy, and community. Hall wasn’t involved in the study, but shared some exercise tips for perimenopausal women.

  • Create built-in accountability: Schedule recurring exercise sessions with a friend, whether meeting in person or syncing up virtually.
  • Prioritize enjoyment over punishment: Choose activities that genuinely make you happy, whether that is Pilates, tennis, racquetball, dance, or short high intensity interval training (HIIT) circuits. “When exercise feels like play rather than a chore, consistency follows naturally,” Hall said. 
  • Remove logistical friction: Work with a trainer if possible, or build an accessible library of on-demand online classes that fit your schedule.
  • Connect movement to cellular health: Remember that exercise is not just about weight management; it literally rebuilds your body’s cellular engine. “Reconnecting with physical strength and seeing a vibrant, energized body emerge is one of the most empowering experiences a woman can have during midlife,” Hall said. 

“Too often in midlife, previously stable women suddenly plummet into deep depression and anxiety, only to be prescribed multiple medications with debilitating side effects that never address the underlying biology,” Hall said.

“While hormone optimization remains essential to correct these endocrine deficiencies, exercise serves as a powerful, non-pharmaceutical therapy that directly targets the true root causes by revitalizing our cellular health and restoring overall vitality.”
