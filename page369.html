#4365. The Next Mass Effect Sounded Like John Wick In Space

📅 23 Oct 2026 | Category: other | Source

After news broke that EA would be shutting down BioWare, more details on the studio's in-development Mass Effect have begun to trickle out. A trailer for Mass Effect Universe hinted that the next game in the series would have a completely different tone, moving away from the galactic warfare that defined the first three games and toward covert ops, espionage, and the occasional barroom shootout. Gaming content creator and YouTuber Dantics has shed more light on the game, describing it as a Mass Effect game for the fans.

Known for his lore videos, including several on Mass Effect, Dantics says he was part of a private "Mass Effect Council," a community feedback group of content creators who helped advise BioWare's small team.

Dantics says that Mass Effect Universe was set over 600 years after the Reaper War, and players would step into the boots of a customizable N7 agent. Players would also have a much smaller arsenal of weapons that would make them feel like a "cross between John Wick and James Bond," and they'd be working to take down a sinister force that was operating in the shadows of a galactic cold war. While Commander Shepard was a soldier, your N7 character was going to be an "agent" who would rely on cunning to get through encounters, but could apply pressure when needed--ballistic pressure, that is. 

Following the destruction of the relays at the end of the Reaper War, the fast-travel hubs had been rebuilt, and technology had advanced at a rapid pace. Shepard's legacy had been preserved centuries after their death; three new powerful alliances had arisen: Council races had formed their own faction, while humanity had aligned itself with Krogans, Batarians, and other superpowers. The Geth--a synthetic, network-based artificial intelligence species--had become a third power, and Dantics said there would be connections between the Milky Way and Andromeda galaxies, thanks to the Geth building superstructure relays.

So what would the gameplay have been like? Dantics says that your N7 agent would be implanted with experimental technology to give them an edge in battle, allowing you to hack and manipulate the environment to your advantage, similar to hacking in Cyberpunk 2077. Missions would have a handcrafted feel to them, but with "Hitman-style freedom" in how you complete them. Squadmates would also feature in the game, and this time, they could clash with each other. Dantics also mentioned that romance "wasn't being sanitized," something the leaked Mass Effect trailer alluded to with some saucy scenes. A familiar face from the past would also return, as Liara T'Soni was still alive and had grown into a powerful Asari Matriarch.

Mass Effect universe has been shelved, but it has been reported that the TV show based on the sci-fi series is still in development. Experts have also spoken about EA's decision, saying that it sadly does make business sense, while Former BioWare executive producer Mark Darrah has suggested that EA could sell the Mass Effect IP in the future. It might have been bad timing, but this week also saw Archetype Entertainment release a new trailer for its Mass Effect spiritual successor, Exodus.

#28239. Bluetooth backlash and nostalgia helped Bose launch wired headphones that keep selling out

📅 23 Oct 2026 | Category: other | Source

Last fall, Raza Haider, president of premium consumer audio at Bose, noticed something peculiar when dropping his daughter off at college: more students were wearing wired headphones than he’d seen in recent years. “Even [my daughter] made a comment like, ‘this is nuts. When I was in high school, we would have made fun of kids who had wired headphones, and now it feels like all the cool kids have them,’” Haider says. 

There’s a rift from Bluetooth headphones underway that isn’t just anecdotal. Sales increased 15% year-over-year from 2025 to 2026, totaling $354 million in 2026 according to data from market research company Circana. That might not seem very high, but in the context of the broader, usually mostly flat-growth headphone market, it’s pretty noteworthy, says Paul Gagnon, VP and industry advisor for consumer technology at Circana.

“If you look at the last six or seven years, wired headphones had been declining. Consumers had shown preference for wireless, especially earbud style, like Apple’s AirPods,” Gagnon says. “We started to see the wired headphone category return to growth last year.”

When Bose released its noise-canceling wired earbuds—its first wired audio product in 11 years—in late September, pre-orders for the $99 headphones sold out two months’ worth of stock in less than 24 hours. The company says this “overwhelming demand” has caused subsequent stock availabilities to quickly sell out as well. Excitement for the new headphones, which shipped Oct. 15, underscores a renaissance happening in headphones.

Bluetooth may have dominated for years as the hands-free way to listen to music, with active noise cancellation becoming standard for many. But it brought with it a suite of things to worry about, including charging the headphones and locating them if they aren’t in their case. As nostalgic cords come back into the limelight, Bose found a way to combine the best of both worlds.

Why wired?

According to Gagnon, wired headphone sales stopped dropping in August 2025. Since then, wired headphone sales have grown every month. The shift happened against the backdrop of a particularly tough economy. 

“Some of it may be cost related. The average price of a pair is $12,” Gagnon says. Though the interest in Bose’s relatively pricey pair of Noise Cancelling Wired Earbud headphones may speak to a desire for premium wired products, the top five brands—Apple, Sony, JLab Audio, Sennheiser, and Skullcandy—mostly come in under $20. 

But there’s also a convenience factor in a world where people own an average of 3.6 devices per person—data from the UN Conference on Trade and Development that didn’t even include headphones. 

“People are starting to realize the downsides of a lot of our modern technology systems. Generally, they’re getting fatigued,” Taylor Lorenz, independent technology journalist and User Magazine founder, tells Fast Company. “Wired headphones don’t die. They’re easy to find. You can’t lose one; they’re attached to each other. There are a lot of upsides to them.”

One of those upsides: an inline mic. When Lorenz isn’t talking into a podcast mic, there’s often a headphone wire dangling from ear with the mic visible, a common sight among content creators on TikTok and Instagram.

Wired headphones have also become a common sight on celebrities going about their day, or as part of interviews (Dua Lipa for Vogue), music videos (Role Model and Addison Rae), and ad campaigns (Sabrina Carpenter for Skims).

Haider says the pop culture moment showed that “Wired earbuds were becoming part of culture again.” Carpenter in particular was an inspiration for the Bose project. Internally, Haider says, the company “adopted ‘Sabrina’ as its codename,” the president says.

Once again a statement piece

At the height of iPod mania, the portability of music became synonymous with the company’s wired headphones. The early ads with dancing silhouettes that emphasized the white headphones turned them into iconic accessories. 

Twenty-five years since the introduction of Apple’s earbuds (branded these days as EarPods), wired headphones are now an aesthetic preference rather than a necessity, especially as people increasingly wear early-2000s tech as an embellishment. 

“Tech accessories are very popular, whether they’re bag charms or barrettes or jewelry,” Lorenz says. “People are getting creative with retro technology. It reminds me of them using the iPod Nano as a hair clip.” Some folks are even decorating their headphone cords.

Given the retro appeal, it might seem fair to think Gen Z, which has been visibly driving the trend, is leading it, but it’s actually millennials who lead wired headphone purchases. “They also overindex in households with children,” says Gagnon. “If you think about a 35- to 44-year-old, that’s the typical age of a young parent or a parent of young kids, so maybe they’re buying them for their kids.”

Nostalgia, with an upgrade

The shift to corded headphones—happening concurrently with the resurgence of “retro-tech” trends, like point-and-shoot cameras—underscores nostalgia as a powerful influence on consumers right now. “Newer isn’t necessarily better. A lot of our technology is actually making our lives worse for no reason,” Lorenz says, pointing to people going back to analog, be it iPod Nanos or film cameras. 

But like newer digital point-and-shoots, which are using flash memory to improve on the original product, the strong performance of Bose’s new product shows interest in upgrades that borrow from now-standard features of high-tech products. 

The newly released headphones are noise-cancelling, which is a pretty unique feature for corded headphones, and represent an upgrade on an older model of Bose’s wired earbuds. 

On its original noise-cancelling earbuds—which cost three times as much as the new ones—noise cancelling could “only be delivered through wires because it required that much battery life,” Haider says. “There was actually a big battery pack on the product itself because it needed to be charged and then plugged into the jack of your phone,” Haider says. 

With the new product, Haider says Bose rethought how it would bring noise cancellation, a fairly standard feature today, into a “form factor that people are really yearning for.” So, rather than a battery pack, Bose’s 2026 wired headphones draw power from the phone itself to fuel the noise cancelling feature. 

In the old days, technology was the star of the show when it came to product design, Haider says. Now, since many technologies are pretty advanced, consumer satisfaction comes more from how things are configured and built. “We had to quickly unlearn that wireless is not the only answer, and wired is not just something of the past,” he says.
