#7806. SDK Engineer - Python at Supabase

📅 16 Oct 2026 | Category: other | Source

About Supabase

Supabase is the Postgres development platform, built by developers for developers. We provide a complete backend solution including Database, Auth, Storage, Edge Functions, Realtime, and Vector Search. All services are deeply integrated and designed for growth.

About the Role

We're looking for a SDK Engineer - Python to join our SDK team and own how developers talk to Supabase from Python. You'll work closely with the rest of the SDK team, our Auth, Realtime, Storage, PostgREST, Functions, CLI and Docs teams, and the open-source community, helping us make the Python SDK a library Python developers genuinely enjoy using.

Python developers reach Supabase from backend services, data pipelines, notebooks, and a growing number of AI agents and agent frameworks. The Python SDK is how all of them talk to the platform: database queries, auth, storage, realtime and edge functions, in both sync and async flavors.

This is a library-authoring role, and the work happens in the open: public repos, public issue trackers, and API decisions whose consequences last for years. If you get satisfaction from a type checker that catches the bug before your users do, from an async client that cleans up after itself, and from an issue tracker that isn't a graveyard, you'll like it here.

This role is ideal for someone who thrives in async, fast-paced environments and is excited about building developer tools that scale to millions.

What You'll Be Responsible For

  • Build and evolve our Python SDK across database, auth, storage, realtime and functions, with both sync and async APIs that feel natural in Python.

  • Start new things. Alongside the maintenance work, find what's missing, make the case for it, and build it. Nobody will hand you the list.

  • Work in the open. Triage inbound issues, review and shepherd outside contributions, keep CI and release automation healthy, and hold a clear, kind line on scope.

  • Own the typing experience. Make the SDK fully typed and checker-friendly, so autocomplete and static analysis stay correct in real codebases.

  • Keep us honest across environments. Multiple Python versions, sync and async frameworks, workers, serverless and notebooks — with dependency ranges that don't fight our users' projects. Test it, don't assume it.

  • Work on realtime. Long-lived websocket connections, reconnection, and subscription and presence semantics.

  • Track the platform. When auth, realtime, storage, postgrest or edge functions ship a change, make sure the SDK reflects it, and that we say so in the changelog.

  • Design in public. Write the RFCs, migration guides, release notes and docs. Treat a breaking change as a deprecation path plus a migration story, not a version bump.

  • Make our SDK legible to AI coding agents, which are now a major consumer of it.

You Might Be a Good Fit If You

  • Know modern Python typing deeply. You use generics, protocols, overloads and TypedDict as design tools, run strict type checking, and fix type errors without reaching for Any.

  • Have authored or maintained a published library with a public API and users outside your own company. You know what it costs to change something people depend on.

  • Know the maintainer side of open source. Issue triage at volume, reviewing outside PRs, release automation, changelogs and semver, security reports, and saying no to a feature request without losing the person who asked.

  • Are fluent in asyncio and have opinions on how a library should serve both sync and async callers.

  • Know Python packaging for libraries: pyproject.toml, wheels, modern tooling, version support policy, and why libraries shouldn't pin their dependencies tightly.

  • Test as a habit: unit, type-level and end-to-end against a real stack. Every fix comes with a regression test.

  • Write well. Design docs, changelogs and issue replies are a large part of the output here, much of it public, and we're async by default.

  • Have real autonomy: you find the important work, scope it, ship it, and say what you didn't do.

  • Communicate clearly across both technical and non-technical audiences

  • Have experience in async or globally distributed teams

  • Are energized by solving real-world problems for developers

  • Are comfortable navigating ambiguity and moving quickly

Bonus points if you: have run a major-version migration for a public package, know your way around JWT/OAuth, have built integrations for AI agent frameworks, or have generated idiomatic clients from a schema.

What We Offer

  • Fully Remote

    We hire globally. We believe you can do your best work from anywhere. There are no Supabase offices, but we provide a WeWork membership or co-working allowance you can use anywhere in the world.

  • ESOP

    Every team member receives ESOP (equity ownership) in the company. We want everyone to share in the upside of what we’re building together.

  • Tech Allowance

    Use this budget to set up your ideal work environment—laptop, monitor, headphones, or whatever helps you do your best work.

  • Health Benefits

    Supabase covers 100% of health insurance for employees and 80% for dependents, wherever you are. Your wellbeing and your family’s health are important to us.

  • Annual Off-Sites

    Once a year, the entire company gathers in a new city for a week of connection, collaboration, and fun. It’s a highlight of our year.

  • Flexible Work

    We operate asynchronously and trust you to manage your own time. You know what needs to be done and when.

  • Professional Development

    Every team member receives an annual education allowance to spend on learning—courses, books, conferences, or anything that supports your growth.

About the Team

Supabase was born-remote and open-source-first. We believe our globally distributed team is our secret weapon in building tools developers love.

  • ~400 team members

  • 60+ countries

  • 20+ languages spoken

  • Over $1B raised (including our $500M Series F)

  • 540,000+ community members

We move fast, build in public, and use what we ship. If it’s in your project, we probably use it in ours too. We believe deeply in the open-source ecosystem and strive to support—not replace—existing tools and communities.

#8042. France’s Riots Echo 1968. But Macron Is No de Gaulle

📅 16 Oct 2026 | Category: other | Source

On March 29, 1968, at the height of leftist protests crippling France, President Charles de Gaulle boarded a secret flight to a French military base in Germany. France was teetering on chaos, and the iconic French leader wanted to know whether the army would remain loyal.

There are strong similarities but also differences with the present situation in France, whose streets are now engulfed daily in violent riots that are spreading to other European countries.

Clement Liebard, president of La Cocarde Etudiante, a conservative student group fighting the Left to keep schools open, emailed me one difference with ’68: Trade unions are less engaged now than they were back then. That reflects the trend of industrial workers being less and less interested in pursuing societal change.

But some elements of today’s protests are integral to France. The French character can be explosive, which means that France periodically succumbs to massive street violence, and this violence can topple not just governments but entire societal systems.

>>> France’s Education Collapse Is a Warning for America

De Gaulle was despondent when he landed at the base in Baden-Baden. According to Gen. Jacques Massu, the commander of French forces in Germany, de Gaulle said to him, “Everything is ruined, the communists have caused a total paralysis of the country. I command nothing anymore. I’m going to quit.”

Massu, who revealed this exchange years later, well after de Gaulle’s death from a heart attack in 1970, said he reassured his president that the military was behind him in case he needed to use it to quell the unrest. He urged him to return to Paris.

That de Gaulle did. The next day, back in Paris, de Gaulle resolutely told his countrymen: “I will not resign.” He dissolved the National Assembly and called for snap elections. A massive demonstration in his favor took place on the Champs-Elysees, and weeks later the Gaullists triumphed in the elections, re-establishing de Gaulle’s authority.

President Emmanuel Macron, however, is no Charles de Gaulle. The former is a weak man leaving behind him a failed economy and a country gripped by riots; the latter led the Free French during World War II.

Another difference in today’s riots is the participation of Islamic demonstrators, who can be seen waving Palestinian and Algerian flags and who have forged a “red-green alliance” with communists, also coordinating the riots. We see this alliance here in the U.S. as well.

France in the 1960s had a Muslim population of just over 1%, nowhere near the 10% of today. And today they are highly concentrated in the urban areas where the riots are taking place. In Paris it is close to 20%, and in Marseille, just under 30%.

But when you hear today’s rioters chant slogans from ’68, you realize that the similarities are numerous, too.

De Gaulle was well aware back then that street violence could lead to change in France, having himself returned to power in 1958 after demonstrations over the war in Algeria shook France, and he was asked to form a government.

The turmoil proved fatal for the Fourth Republic, with its unstable coalitions, and led to today’s Fifth Republic with its strong presidential system, initially under de Gaulle.

The ’58 crisis is not called “the Algiers putsch” for nothing; constitutional niceties were kept as the National Assembly invested de Gaulle as president and authorized him to write a new constitution. But it was street violence that delivered the death blow. Massu himself seized government buildings in Algiers and formed a “Committee of Public Safety.”

And then there was that other massive street uprising, the French Revolution, which ended Europe’s old order and rewrote borders, politics, and society, and the street rebellions of the 19th century. Baron Georges-Eugene Haussmann, Napoleon III’s prefect, had parts of Paris demolished and ordered the construction of the wide, straight boulevards we admire today—to make it easier for troops to shoot protesters.

>>> Why This Legacy Newspaper Is Siding With Illegal Aliens Over American Citizens

Today, street violence is helping the Right electorally, just like the Revolution led to Napoleon and ’58 to de Gaulle. Marine Le Pen, leader of Rassemblement National, is poised to win the upcoming presidential elections, defeating by a 67-33 margin Jean-Luc Melenchon, the communist leader of La France Insoumise. Melenchon is expected to face Le Pen in the second round of voting in May.

RN is described in the media as “far right,” but this is wrong. It is a strong supporter of French identity, and Le Pen has responded to the riots by saying, “I will immediately conduct mass deportations. Mass immigration has been a disaster to France.” Liebard tells me that the riots and LFI’s involvement give Le Pen “the perfect arguments.”

However, Liebard adds that the riots help Melenchon mobilize his electorate and train his activists. “He will have the largest activist striking force to occupy the field during the weeks leading up to the election,” he wrote to me.

Melenchon has already promised to install a Sixth Republic with a new Marxist constitution if he is elected. Let’s hope France finds a way to save herself and not export her revolution to the rest of Europe—as it did during the period known as “the Terror” in the 1790s.
