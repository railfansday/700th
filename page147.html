Senior officials from Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Turkey opened talks with Iranian authorities in Tehran on Monday, following increased Iran-backed Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia and renewed warnings of military action between Tehran and Washington.

Saudi Defense Minister Khalid bin Salman, Pakistani army chief Asim Munir and Turkish intelligence chief Ibrahim Kalin will travel to Tehran, Reuters reported on Monday, citing Saudi and Iranian officials.

Ali Bagheri Kani, deputy secretary of Iran's Supreme National Security Council, confirmed the visit.

The initiative was proposed during Munir's visit to Tehran last week, when he met President Masoud Pezeshkian and other senior officials, according to a senior Iranian official.

Iranian media reported that officials told Munir they would not negotiate with Washington until Tehran's demands were met.

The visit marks the first high-level meeting between Iran and all three members of the Mecca defense pact since the escalation of hostilities.

Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Turkey signed the agreement in August, establishing that a military attack on one member would be considered an attack on all three.

Pakistan has ratified the pact, while ratification remains pending in Turkey's parliament.

Earlier this month, the three countries agreed to implement their defense commitments and rapidly deploy military personnel and capabilities to Saudi Arabia.

The agreement faces its first significant test following increased Houthi attacks on Saudi territory.

Saudi Arabia announced on Sunday that it had intercepted another wave of missiles and drones.

The visit is intended to reduce hostilities and support mediation efforts by Qatar and Pakistan, Saudi Deputy Foreign Minister for Public Diplomacy Raed bin Khalid Qarmali said earlier.

Qatari officials also visited Tehran on Sunday for talks with Iranian authorities.

Previous mediation by Qatar and Pakistan helped secure a temporary halt to hostilities, but the agreement collapsed when fighting resumed.

Indirect contacts between Tehran and Washington have continued through both mediators, a process also referenced by Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi.

US President Donald Trump raised the possibility of renewed military operations against Iran on Friday.

Iranian officials have warned that any further attack would trigger a stronger response.

The diplomatic meetings come as Saudi Arabia seeks to prevent the conflict from spreading to its critical infrastructure, putting the Mecca defense pact and regional mediation efforts under growing pressure.

A renewed diplomatic push to avert another round of the US-Iran war gathered pace on Sunday, with Qatari mediators in Tehran as President Donald Trump publicly weighed reaching an agreement with the Islamic Republic against taking further military action.

The Qatari team was in the Iranian capital as part of an effort coordinated with the United States to reduce regional tensions, an official briefed on the matter told Reuters.

The visit puts Doha at the center of an attempt to break a diplomatic deadlock even as the risk of renewed fighting has grown.

The urgency behind that effort was underscored by Trump himself. In an interview with Fox News on Sunday, the US president framed his options in stark terms.

“Do I make a deal with them or do I finish them off? That's the only question,” Trump said.

Iran, for its part, is also keeping the diplomatic channel alive without signaling that it is prepared to accept Washington's terms.

Foreign Ministry spokesperson Esmaeil Baghaei said on Sunday that messages were continuing to pass between Iran and the United States through intermediaries and that Tehran had not closed the window on diplomacy.

At the same time, Iranian officials have accused Washington of demanding what Tehran characterizes as its “complete surrender.”

The two sides also remain divided over the terms of a broader settlement. Washington has continued to make Iran's nuclear capabilities central to any settlement. Trump told FOX 2 Detroit on Sunday that the war would not end until the United States was satisfied Tehran did not have nuclear weapons capabilities, while saying he was prepared to take time to reach an agreement.

“I'm going to make a great deal. I'm a longterm person; I'm not a short term person,” he said.

The Qatari intervention follows a broader diplomatic push by regional and European governments concerned that another attack on Iran could expand the war.

Foreign ministers from Turkey, Saudi Arabia, Egypt, Pakistan, Britain, France, Germany and Italy met in Istanbul on Friday, where Turkish Foreign Minister Hakan Fidan warned that renewed attacks on Iran would widen the conflict and said diplomacy remained the only way to end it.

Iran may be approaching a mental-health emergency as prolonged uncertainty over war, economic pressure and the future takes a growing toll on the public, the head of the country’s Psychology and Counseling Organization said on Sunday.

Asked by ILNA how far Iranian society was from a mental-health emergency, Alireza Agha-Yousefi said: “I don’t think we are very far from it.”

He pointed to marriage, divorce and birth rates as areas of concern and said authorities needed to act before psychological and social problems became more severe.

Agha-Yousefi said the prolonged state of neither full-scale war nor peace was itself becoming a major source of pressure.

“The overall condition of society in a situation of neither war nor peace can create significant psychological strain,” he said.

He said people normally try to meet their needs while also responding to threats, but when both are present at the same time, threats tend to dominate.

“Uncertainty about the future is one of the most difficult situations,” he said, adding that it deprives people of the ability to plan their lives.

Agha-Yousefi said prolonged uncertainty could produce severe anxiety and, over time, lead to both physical and psychological exhaustion.

He cautioned that the consequences of intense stress do not always appear immediately.

“The effects may show themselves one or two years later,” he said, citing depression, marital conflict and physical health problems as possible outcomes.

He also pointed to prolonged muscle tension, joint and spinal pain and gastrointestinal problems as conditions that can be associated with chronic stress.

President Masoud Pezeshkian last week also described the current “no war, no peace” situation as the worst position Iran could be in, as tensions with Washington remained unresolved.

Agha-Yousefi said the same economic conditions contributing to psychological strain were also making treatment less accessible.

He said demand for counselling had risen, but inflation and declining purchasing power meant many people could no longer afford psychological services.

“Psychological services are not covered by basic insurance,” he said, adding that official figures on the number of people seeking treatment could therefore give a misleading picture.

He said some people who need help simply do not seek it because of the cost.

A proposal to bring psychological services under insurance coverage had been submitted to the organization’s central council around four months ago but had yet to be approved, he said.

Pressure on families and social stability

Agha-Yousefi also said Iran appeared to be moving toward greater social dislocation.

“It seems our social situation is, to some extent, moving toward anomie or social normlessness,” he said.

He warned that as social stress rises, the effects can spread into family life and wider social behavior, including later marriage, addiction and greater instability within households.

“When problems enter the family, they can be a sign of deeper social damage,” he said.

He added that severe family pressures could, in some cases, contribute to extreme violence, including killings within families.

Agha-Yousefi said delaying preventive measures would make the psychological, physical and social consequences much harder and more expensive to address later.

His comments come as speculation grows over possible renewed US and Israeli military action against Iran.

Recent reports have said the US military has prepared options for further strikes after the November 3 midterm elections if President Donald Trump authorizes them.

That uncertainty has added another layer of pressure to an economy already strained by inflation, a weakening currency and declining purchasing power.

The war with Iran raises a familiar question for Israel: Can battlefield gains produce lasting peace? Its military history offers mixed answers, from unresolved victories to withdrawals and ceasefires that left adversaries armed.

Destroying Iran’s military capabilities and preventing their reconstruction are different objectives, requiring different measures of success.

Since 1948, Israel has fought conventional Arab armies and armed organizations such as Hezbollah and Hamas, and has increasingly confronted Iran directly through missile, drone and air attacks far beyond its borders. These forms of warfare have overlapped, posing different challenges for bringing the fighting to an end.

Their endings show how battlefield outcomes interact with diplomacy, outside pressure and arrangements for security after the fighting.

Fighting between Jewish and Arab communities followed the UN’s 1947 partition plan. After Israel declared independence in May 1948, neighboring Arab armies entered the conflict. Israel survived and gained territory beyond the boundaries proposed for the Jewish state.

In 1949, it signed separate armistice agreements with Egypt, Lebanon, Jordan and Syria. These established lines separating the forces, rather than permanent political settlements. The Egyptian agreement explicitly envisaged a subsequent peaceful settlement.

Israel’s survival did not resolve the territorial dispute or the displacement of Palestinians. The fighting ended without peace between Israel and its neighbors.

1956: Military gains, withdrawal under pressure

During the Suez crisis, Israel attacked Egypt in coordination with Britain and France, rapidly advancing across Sinai.

Pressure from the United States, the Soviet Union and the United Nations forced a withdrawal, completed in March 1957. Israel could not retain the territory it had captured, despite its battlefield success.

The outcome nevertheless brought security gains: Israel regained access to the Straits of Tiran, while a UN emergency force deployed in Sinai. The US State Department’s historical account distinguishes those gains from the territorial withdrawal.

The episode illustrates why withdrawal is not automatically evidence of military defeat, and why military superiority alone does not determine a war’s political outcome.

1967: Six days of fighting, decades of consequences

Israel’s opening airstrikes devastated Egypt’s air force. Within six days, Israeli forces had captured Sinai, Gaza, the West Bank, East Jerusalem and the Golan Heights.The war ended through ceasefires, without a formal surrender by the defeated Arab states. Israel’s decisive battlefield victory transformed the territorial balance but did not settle the conflict.

UN Security Council Resolution 242 subsequently established a framework linking Israeli withdrawal from occupied territory with an end to belligerency and recognition of states’ right to live within secure, recognized boundaries.

The military result was immediate. Disputes over occupation, borders and Palestinian rights lasted far beyond the fighting.

1973: From surprise attack to a peace treaty

Egypt and Syria attacked Israeli forces in Sinai and the Golan Heights in October 1973, initially making substantial gains. Israel counterattacked and crossed the Suez Canal before US-Soviet diplomacy and UN ceasefire resolutions helped stop the fighting.

The US State Department’s history describes the war as opening the way for Henry Kissinger’s shuttle diplomacy and, ultimately, the Egyptian-Israeli peace treaty. Egypt did not recover all Sinai through military action, but the war helped break the diplomatic stalemate.

The 1978 Camp David accords provided the framework for the 1979 treaty. Israel agreed to withdraw from Sinai in exchange for peace with Egypt. The main withdrawal was completed in 1982; the disputed Taba area was returned in 1989 following arbitration.

Egypt recovered territory through a combination of war and diplomacy. Israel exchanged territorial control for a political agreement with a major adversary.

Lebanon, 1982–2000: Initial success, prolonged conflict

Israel’s 1982 invasion drove into Beirut, where US-mediated arrangements secured the departure of Palestine Liberation Organization leaders and fighters. This was an evacuation from Beirut, not the elimination of every Palestinian armed presence in Lebanon.

Israel withdrew much of its force in 1985 but retained a southern security zone alongside the allied South Lebanon Army. Its remaining troops left in May 2000, following years of conflict with Hezbollah. UNIFIL’s historical account records both withdrawals.

The withdrawal was unilateral, without a peace treaty or formal Israeli surrender. Hezbollah portrayed it as a victory; Israel ended a prolonged deployment without resolving the underlying confrontation.

The departure of one armed organization’s leadership in 1982 had not produced a lasting end to fighting.

The 2006 war followed Hezbollah’s cross-border attack and capture of two Israeli soldiers. Israel launched extensive air and ground operations, but Hezbollah remained armed and continued firing rockets during the conflict.

Neither side’s survival or military performance alone explains the settlement. The operative mechanism was UN Security Council Resolution 1701, adopted on August 11, followed by a cessation of hostilities on August 14.

It called for Hezbollah to stop attacks, Israel to stop offensive operations, and Israeli forces to withdraw as Lebanese troops and an expanded UNIFIL deployed. It envisaged an area between the Blue Line and Litani River free of armed personnel and weapons other than those of the Lebanese government and UNIFIL.

Hezbollah was not disarmed. Nevertheless, UNIFIL says the arrangements helped sustain almost 17 years of relative calm before the escalation beginning in 2023. An incomplete settlement still had significant practical effects.

The Gaza wars of 2008–09, 2012, 2014 and 2021 ended without Hamas surrendering or losing all its military capabilities.

Scholars Efraim Inbar and Eitan Shamir described Israel’s approach as “mowing the grass”: periodically degrading an adversary’s capabilities and restoring deterrence rather than expecting a permanent political resolution through force. Their study appeared online in 2013 and in the Journal of Strategic Studies in 2014.

After Hamas’s October 7, 2023 attack, Israel set broader objectives, including dismantling its military and governing capabilities and recovering the hostages. Shamir argued that the attack marked a departure from the previous limited-campaign approach. That was his strategic assessment, rather than proof that eliminating Hamas was achievable.

The ensuing war devastated Gaza. The October 2025 ceasefire did not settle its future: a year later, the UN human rights office was still reporting killings despite the agreement.

Lebanon after 2023: Military damage and state authority

Israel’s 2024 campaign killed Hezbollah leader Hassan Nasrallah and other senior commanders, severely damaging its leadership. The fighting nevertheless moved toward a negotiated cessation of hostilities rather than formal surrender.

The US-French-brokered arrangement took effect on November 27, 2024, and again rested on implementing Resolution 1701, Lebanese state authority and Israeli withdrawal.

Disarmament remained contested. In October 2026, The Wall Street Journal reported that Hezbollah demanded the return of explosives seized by Lebanese authorities, illustrating the continuing struggle over weapons outside state control.

Iran: Ending operations or preventing reconstruction?

The June 2025 war involved Israeli attacks on Iranian military and nuclear targets and direct US strikes on Fordow, Natanz and Isfahan. US involvement therefore preceded the broader war of 2026.

Israel’s Foreign Ministry described the nuclear threat as “existential and imminent” in its account of the operation. That characterization was Israel’s stated justification, not an independently established finding that Iran possessed nuclear weapons. The same document records Israel’s acceptance of a US-proposed ceasefire on June 24.The broader confrontation in 2026 extended to shipping and energy supplies. By October, the US naval blockade and the standoff over reopening the Strait of Hormuz remained unresolved.

Netanyahu has explicitly advocated moving beyond containment. Addressing military cadets in February 2026, he declared there would be “no more containment of threats” and emphasized preemptive action. This supports describing a shift in his stated approach, without assuming a uniform doctrine across every Israeli institution.

Destroying launchers or facilities has measurable immediate effects. Preventing their reconstruction depends on industrial capacity, supply networks, political decisions and whatever monitoring or enforcement follows.

Within the approach of seeking to prevent Iran from rebuilding its military threat, two scenarios can be considered. Neither is a prediction or an exhaustive account of how the conflict might end.

The first is a military defeat sufficiently extensive to destroy much of Iran’s offensive capability and substantially constrain its reconstruction. Lasting results would depend on measures that prevent replenishment, rather than destruction alone.The second combines military defeat with the end of the Islamic Republic and its replacement by a political system that abandons hostility toward Israel and support for armed proxies.

That scenario depends on more than a change of leadership. It assumes a successor government both chooses and can enforce a different policy, including ending financial, military and operational support for allied armed groups. A government’s collapse would not itself establish those conditions.

The scenarios may overlap, but neither guarantees stability. Their outcomes would depend on political authority, security arrangements and the capacity to enforce commitments after combat ends.

Other possible outcomes include a negotiated agreement that constrains Iran’s capabilities without changing its government, or a prolonged armed standoff sustained by deterrence. Neither would necessarily eliminate the underlying hostility, though either could bring the current fighting to an end.

Israel’s earlier wars provide different benchmarks: territorial withdrawal under international pressure in 1956, land exchanged for peace with Egypt, unilateral withdrawal from Lebanon, and ceasefire arrangements that reduced fighting without disarming Hezbollah. Each ended a particular phase of conflict; only some produced a broader political settlement.

Il menu è un condensato di sapori della Marmilla, in cui il “tempo dei ricordi” non è rievocato in chiave nostalgica, ma declinato da Francesco Vitale in una visione contemporanea. Che rende onore alla sfida di portare in tavola una tradizione che non tradisce la memoria, ma resta collegata al futuro per affermare la propria identità. Via dunque inutili esercizi di stile per dar voce ad ingredienti di indiscussa qualità e ad un pensiero di cucina “netta”, fatta di estrema pulizia, di equilibrio tra rigore filologico e precisione tecnica.

Arieddas sa creare una elegante liaison tra cibo e cultura, memoria e futuro, antichi saperi ed evoluzione. E lo fa in un ambiente avvolgente e coinvolgente con una cucina che rifugge dall’iper-inflazionato binomio tradizione /evoluzione. Preferisce trasformare le peculiarità agroalimentari dell’entroterra sardo in un’esperienza d’alta cucina rigorosa e alla portata di molti.

La conferma arriva dal menù degustazione “Viaggio in Marmilla” (44 euro), un percorso di 7 assaggi (5 antipasti, un primo e un secondo) che permette di gustare la Sardegna autentica ed il pensiero dello chef ad un prezzo super accessibile.

Ma anche i piatti alla carta offrono un ottimo rapporto qualità – prezzo. Oltre a quelli assaggiati e di cui vi racconto ecco qualche esempio.

— Pane lentu e gallina, 13 €— Pan brioche, fegatini, agrumi e alloro, 12 €— Lorighittas e ragù di cortile, 16 €— Coniglio al Vermentino, 16 €— Sanluri-Brest, 9 €

Con questi prezzi, un pasto completo con 3 piatti salati vi costa in media 45 euro. Difficile fare meglio.

L’entré, pane di semola e farine macinate a pietra con testa in cassetta, è un goloso biglietto da visita che ben predispone all’inizio della degustazione.

Gli antipasti, pensati per essere condivisi, vengono serviti in contemporanea per favorire l’interazione a tavola e quel tanto rimpianto dialogo tra i commensali.

La tartare di pomodoro arrosto con olio all’origano, fichi, mandorla e agrumi (12 euro) è un atlante di colori, profumi e paesaggi di macchia mediterranea. È l’esempio concreto di come una cucina ragionata d’entroterra possa declinare l’ingrediente vegetale con rigore e originalità. Un vero e proprio manifesto di sostenibilità e intelligenza gastronomica che denota l’abilità dello chef nel nobilitare materie prime povere attraverso un’esecuzione tecnica d’autore. Nulla da aggiungere ad un boccone inclusivo e rassicurante al tempo stesso.

Medesimo costrutto per l’anguria al bbq con pesto di basilico ed erbe aromatiche (12 euro), che stravolge la percezione comune attraverso una cottura ancestrale, ma di grandissimo impatto emozionale e di “gola”. Lo chef sa impreziosire la dolcezza del frutto con una piacevole e inattesa tridimensionalità, giocando sui rimandi aromatici del pesto che allunga oltremodo la persistenza. Un bite che rende omaggio alla filosofia agricola, cui conferisce spessore culturale, senza risultare banale. Qui la mano di Parini è evidente anche per ricordo storico.

La Kofta – lo spiedo di pecora e maiale con semi misti, spezie e salsa allo yogurt (13 euro) – è un compendio di eleganza, tecnica e conoscenza della materia. Un piatto che, pur conservando un’anima sarda, strizza l’occhio alla cultura mediorientale, senza mai perdere di vista l’obiettivo. Francesco conferma dunque di saper giocare con dosate contaminazioni e dimostra maturità professionale nel rendere omaggio al genius loci, ampliandone l’orizzonte in maniera ponderata.

L’uovo della festa all’occhio di bue con pomodoro arrosto, pecorino e basilico (12 euro), tra i signature del ristorante, è un inno alla cucina golosa ingentilita dal know-how dello chef. Un boccone rotondo, capace di regalare la purezza strutturale dei sapori “di casa” attraverso consistenze millimetriche e una studiata freschezza.

La zucchina brasata con fondo di carne, riduzione di vino, erbe aromatiche e pecorino (12 euro) è la combinazione tra grandi classici e nuove vibes. Qui da Arieddas i colori vivi e intensi raccontano le colline, la Marmilla, i frutti della terra. Un antipasto che dà continuità alla alla filosofia no-waste in modo leggibile e diretto.

I lorighittas fatti a mano con ragù di cortile e uovo di gallina marinato (15 euro) sono omaggio alla tradizione e esaltazione di un pensiero di cucina in cui la tecnica è a servizio dell’ingrediente. Un primo piatto che compie il miracolo di dar spessore a pochissimi ingredienti, divertendosi a stuzzicare il palato in un crescendo di sensazioni. La texture da manuale di una pasta dai ricami complessi, il ragù perfetto nella cottura e il tocco dell’uovo marinato, rendono questo piatto una felice, golosa versione POP di un classico senza tempo. Una forchettata che nasconde dietro un apparente “così com’è” il lavoro di ricerca dell’armonia.

I filindeu in brodo di pecora (15 euro) sono l’essenza di una cucina testarda, di quella resilenza sincera tra campi e fornelli a tutela della salvaguardia della biodiversità territoriale. Un gusto che va in crescendo, arricchendosi di un pot-pourri di sfumature gustolfattive, ad ogni cucchiaiata. Eclatante per maestria e studio maniacale di dosi e ingredienti. Un piatto che sa asciugare la tradizione portandola a livelli di finezza inattesa e che arriva dritto al cuore di molti nostalgici.

La pecora di Sanluri (12 euro) è in tre diverse cotture, ognuna riservata ad uno specifico taglio. Contemporaneità della mano e della tecnica per questa felice espressione di una cucina riduttivamente etichettata come “di casa”. Ma che è un profondo e atavico insieme di gesti innati della cultura gastronomica locale. Impossibile resistere a questo grande classico sardo che stupisce per cotture e lavorazione.

Il gelato di Arieddas alla crema, accompagnato da ciò che la stagione e il territorio offrono (9 euro) è un piacevole gioco di gusto che incuriosisce l’ospite, invitandolo a comporre il proprio abbinamento ideale, attingendo dal territorio. Un’idea fresca per ritoccare un tipico “fine pasto”.

La winelist dà giustamente spazio ai vini della Cantina Su’Entu, che ospita il locale, cui si affianca una corretta selezione di bottiglie sarde, nazionali ed internazionali. Una carta che sa parlare tanto all’appassionato quanto al tradizionalista, senza far sentire in difetto nessuno.

A rendere più piacevole l’esperienza è un servizio di sala, professionalmente informale, che sa accogliere l’avventore e il cliente con sorriso e savoir-faire.
