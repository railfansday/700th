#3864. Why AI Can’t Replace Engineering Teams

📅 22 Oct 2026 | Category: other | Source


This article is crossposted from IEEE Spectrum’s careers newsletter. Sign up now to get insider tips, expert advice, and practical strategies, written in partnership with tech career development company Parsity and delivered to your inbox for free!

If you’re an engineer, you may feel like your skills aren’t commanding the premium they once did. Many engineers have faced layoffs or restructuring. But even for those of us who still have jobs, something corrosive is happening. You may have heard some version of this:

“ChatGPT answered that in five seconds. Why can’t you?”

Or, “I made this app in a weekend. Why is our team taking so long?”

I worked on a team where leadership said things like this directly to engineers. This was in 2024 and 2025, when the AI craze was in full swing and Claude and ChatGPT adoption was rising.

Demo-Driven Development

Generative AI (and the marketing behind it) gives people the illusion that the work is simpler than it is. A working demo hides complexity, and once someone has a website that works, they may decide they no longer need engineers, or start to distrust the ones they do have.

Some CEOs want to believe they can replace an engineering team with a few sexy prompts. Many companies also want them to believe this.

That website works great in the demo—under specific conditions and without users with thumbs for fingers. Then it has to plug into a system with millions of lines of old code, satisfy legal requirements, meet accessibility standards, and be delivered in a complex deployment process.

Thinking that writing code was the only bottleneck for deploying new software is like the Titanic seeing the tip of the iceberg and assuming there was nothing underneath it.

What you can do about it

Engineers have a unique position in the AI space, because you have the capacity to understand how these models work much better than the average person. Use this to your advantage.

Learn how AI models work. If you can’t already explain that LLMs are probabilistic, what tokens are, or why training data matters, spend a weekend fixing that. You don’t need to become a full-blown AI researcher. A few fundamentals will put you ahead of most people making the AI decisions.

Teach upward. Turn that knowledge into teaching moments for the people who lead you. Explain the limits of these tools, and why a demo and a production system are different animals. It keeps people from truly believing that a notebook full of prompts can replace an engineering team.

Consider the criticism of today’s processes. Some of what we do has been treated as ceremony for too long. Be willing to say, “you know what, maybe you’re right, this process is too slow for the speed we can work at now.” Conceding that earns you the credibility to defend the steps that matter.

I still believe that speed at the cost of everything else is harmful—but that’s a topic for another time.

Find your voice. Engineers at the forefront of AI adoption need to push back and explain why we do things the way we do. Nobody else in the room is going to do it for us.

Don’t chase (too many) shiny objects

Your goal, in my opinion, is to save your organization from buying into the hype so much that they chase the wrong thing. Our job, before and after AI, is building safe and useful products for human beings, and engineers are still best suited for that.

—Brian

#29815. Black October: why bugs discovered by OpenAI tech are creating a cyber emergency

📅 22 Oct 2026 | Category: other | Source

The websites you use every day may have been vulnerable to hackers for years because of hidden flaws in their code that AI only recently discovered.

Why it matters: The code, called PHP, powers millions of websites, and the vulnerabilities could allow hackers to steal sensitive data like your passwords or take control of affected servers.

Driving the news: A potential attack exploiting flaws in PHP emerged on Wednesday, which prompted governments and companies to temporarily take their websites offline while they installed security patches.

  • Dozens of websites in the U.S., U.K., and around the world were briefly taken offline to make the fix.
  • The disruption put the National Security Agency and Europe's cybersecurity agency on alert.

State of play: Security patches were released to fix the issues on Oct. 21, 2026. Cybersecurity firms urged anyone using PHP to install the security updates immediately.

What they're saying: OpenAI's technology helped find the bugs, and the company called the discovery "one of the most impactful security issues in recent memory."

  • "To our knowledge, frontier AI found these vulnerabilities before attackers did," OpenAI said.
  • The cybersecurity firm Wiz wrote in a blog post: "These findings rank among the most severe vulnerabilities discovered in PHP, and arguably in software history."

Read more below.

What is PHP?

You've probably never heard of PHP, even though you've interacted with it for decades.

  • PHP, which stands for Hypertext Preprocessor, is a programming language used by roughly 70% of websites whose server-side language is known.
  • It has been around since the 1990s to help build websites, taking data and generating content sent to a browser.
  • It's still used today for a variety of websites, including Wikipedia, WordPress blogs and shopping websites.

How it works: HTML determines what a website displays. PHP works behind the scenes to collect information and generate content.

What was the PHP vulnerability, exactly?

AI researchers discovered two critical vulnerabilities in PHP that could allow an attacker to take control of a website's server without having legitimate access to it.

  • Hackers could exploit the flaws, dubbed "php2shell" by cybersecurity firms, to steal information from those websites.

Between the lines: The flaws went undetected for more than a decade because exploiting them required complex technical work that AI helped researchers discover, per Wiz.

Why cybersecurity incidents are a big deal

Thought bubble via Axios' Bradley Olson: The PHP vulnerabilities are so serious that the NSA warned website operators to take their sites down if they didn't have enough time to patch the bug.

  • But the cybersecurity problems in recent days have extended far beyond that and include disruptions to developer platforms, data leaks on public schools, break-ins at public companies and more.
  • A major Axios scoop also revealed that banks are detecting unauthorized third-party agent access inside customer accounts, a problem they are racing to contain.

Cyber pros are calling the series of attacks "Black October" due to the sheer volume and the level of risk, although so far many organizations have mitigated the risk and avoided massive real-world harm.

  • While defenders and AI companies have warned for months that a season of cyber emergencies would imminently arrive, they have also been working to secure some of the obscure software that still underpins much of the modern internet ecosystem.

The bottom line: As attacks continue, experts have urged users to secure passwords, use a password manager and also to avoid panic.

  • As with any cyber emergency, the true barometer of harm is the extent to which damage seeps into the real world, as it has done with ransomware that took hospital software offline or a pipeline system that disrupted fuel supplies briefly on the East Coast.
