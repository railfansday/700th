KYIV – Russia and Ukraine have started a localised ceasefire to allow for a humanitarian operation to begin in the Russian-occupied town of Oleshky, both sides said on Oct 27.

Russia’s Defence Ministry said on Telegram that it stopped shelling in the direction of the Ukrainian city of Kherson from 4am Moscow time (9am Singapore time) to prepare for the humanitarian operation in Oleshky, just across the Dnipro River from Kherson.

Dmytro Lubinets, Ukraine’s Parliament commissioner for human rights, confirmed in a Facebook post that the ceasefire has started at the same time.

“As head of the Ukrainian negotiating team, I confirm that the Ukrainian side is honouring its commitments,” he wrote.

Russia and Ukraine have agreed to a localised ceasefire around the Russian-occupied town of Oleshky in southern Ukraine, an arrangement that will enable aid deliveries and allow evacuations from a front-line town suffering acute shortages of food and medicine.

Oleshky lies on the eastern bank of the Dnipro in Ukraine’s southern region of Kherson, opposite the city of Kherson, which is held by Ukrainian forces.

Russian troops seized Oleshky during the first days of their February 2022 invasion and have controlled it ever since. The White House said earlier in October that US First Lady Melania Trump helped to secure the Oleshky ceasefire.

Elsewhere in Ukraine, a Russian missile attack on the Ukrainian capital Kyiv on Oct 26 killed at least one person and injured 10, including students at a dormitory, with the attacks also killing and injuring people in the cities of Kharkiv and Pavlohrad.

#35646. 'Iraq-first' message from Iran ally sparks calls for retaliation

📅 27 Oct 2026 | Category: technology | Source

Ammar al-Hakim’s warning that Baghdad must protect Iraq from US sanctions despite its solidarity with Iran has sparked a sharp backlash on Persian-language social media, with some hardliners calling for retaliation against Iraq.

An excerpt from an interview al-Hakim gave to Iraqiya TV on September 29 has gone viral in recent days.

Al-Hakim, head of Iraq’s National Wisdom Movement and a former head of the Supreme Islamic Council of Iraq, said Washington had explicitly warned Baghdad to comply with US sanctions against Iran or risk sanctions itself.

He said Iran was justified in feeling it had been treated unfairly under what he described as a US siege, but argued that countries could not be governed by emotions and aspirations alone.

Iraq, he said, stood in solidarity with the Iranian people while also having a responsibility to shield its own population from the consequences of sanctions.

Hardline Iranian social media users accused al-Hakim of betraying Iran and showing ingratitude for its support, with some calling for retaliatory measures against Iraq.

Ehsan Salehi, an adviser to former Iranian President Ebrahim Raisi, accused al-Hakim on X of bowing to Washington, justifying that stance and moving closer to Arab Gulf rulers.

Hardline commentator Alireza Taghavinia interpreted al-Hakim’s comments as an admission that Iraq had little choice but to comply with Washington.

“When Ammar al-Hakim, a Shiite whose position he owes to Iran, says this, what can we expect from [Pakistan’s Prime Minister] Shehbaz Sharif?” he wrote.

Filmmaker Ali Zarnegar wrote: “This is precisely the line between honor and dishonor, and unfortunately, you chose to be dishonorable.”

Another user sarcastically called for Iran to prevent Iraqi oil from passing through the Strait of Hormuz.

“Our feelings are with Iraq, too, but what can we do? We have to attack tankers associated with them,” the user wrote. “Since they are so afraid that America will freeze their oil money, we will make things easier for them and prevent even a drop of their oil from being exported through the Strait of Hormuz.”

Other users defended al-Hakim, arguing that his remarks reflected the Iraqi government's responsibility to protect its national interests and criticizing Iran’s leaders for putting ideology ahead of Iran's own welfare.

Mohsen Salehikhah, a journalist and documentary filmmaker associated with reformists, praised the Iraqi politician.

“Ammar al-Hakim said something absolutely right,” he wrote. “If the Islamic Republic also understood that it should prioritize Iran’s interests rather than sacrifice our national interests at the altar of ideological delusions and aspirations, perhaps the situation would be different today.”

Another user pointed out that al-Hakim leads one of the Iraqi political movements closest to Iran, suggesting that other Iraqi groups could hardly be expected to put Iranian interests first.

“Some people spent years selling the public the illusion that sanctions did not matter because we could maintain relations with our neighbors instead of America,” the user wrote. “Well, this is our closest neighbor.”

The backlash comes amid a separate dispute over Iraq’s closure of the al-Shaib/Chazabeh border crossing with Iran.

An adviser to Iraqi Prime Minister Ali Faleh al-Zaidi has said the crossing was closed to prevent the smuggling of narcotics and counterfeit money from Iran, while Iran’s Interior Ministry complained that Baghdad had not informed Tehran in advance.

The closure has prompted calls from some Iranian hardliners for retaliation against Iraq.

Iranian businessman Babak Zanjani called for greater restrictions on Iraq’s access through the Strait of Hormuz, saying Baghdad should be made to understand “the real meaning of a blockade and its consequences.”

He went further, arguing that Iraq should be brought closer to collapse over what he described as a strategic mistake “so that others learn a lesson.”

Hardliners in Tehran are hailing a surge in global oil prices as evidence that pressure on shipping is paying off, even as moderates warn that the same confrontation is inflicting mounting damage on Iran's economy and strengthening the case for a deal with Washington.

Crude prices rose above $116 a barrel on October 24 following reports of two attacks involving supertankers near Oman, prompting hardline newspaper Kayhan to portray the increase as vindication of the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps Navy's strategy of asymmetric pressure around the Strait of Hormuz.

Under the headline “Minefields near Musandam Prove Diplomacy Is Obsolete,” Kayhan argued that disruption to shipping and the resulting pressure on global energy markets had strengthened Iran's bargaining position rather than demonstrated the need for compromise.

The newspaper accused moderates and more pragmatic conservatives of “begging for an agreement,” singling out the centrist Sazandegi newspaper, which supports reaching a deal with the United States.

But the same confrontation is taking a growing toll on Iran's economy.

Moderate daily Shargh warned that higher crude prices offer little relief to private-sector manufacturers facing bankruptcy and argued that keeping negotiations with Washington alive is essential to Iran's economic survival.

The economic daily Donya-ye Eghtesad has also highlighted pressure on factories in industrial centers including Isfahan, Tabriz and Arak, reporting an unprecedented wave of corporate bankruptcies as the maritime blockade continues.

The competing assessments expose an increasingly important argument in Tehran: whether the economic disruption caused by the confrontation is leverage to be exploited or damage Iran can no longer afford.

The debate comes as signs of diplomatic movement feed cautious optimism among some Iranian political figures that an agreement with the United States may be becoming more likely than another round of war.

Former deputy parliamentary speaker Ali Motahari gave voice to that view on Monday, saying that for the first time in several weeks, the prospects of an agreement with Washington appeared greater than the likelihood of renewed conflict.

Speaking to reporters in Tehran, Motahari argued that war was becoming less likely because the United States was facing multiple problems and might therefore be prepared to accept some of Iran’s conditions.

Monday’s high-level talks with regional powers have added to that sense. Saudi Defense Minister Khalid bin Salman, Pakistani army chief Asim Munir and Turkish intelligence chief Ibrahim Kalin are in Tehran to meet national security chief Mohsen Rezaei and Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi.

According to Iranian state outlets, Tehran has reiterated its proposal for reducing tensions in the Strait of Hormuz. Following technical discussions in Doha, it resubmitted a plan to restore shipping through the waterway in stages over seven days.

Foreign Ministry spokesman Esmaeil Baghaei said on Monday that Iranian technical negotiators were examining US counterproposals on procedures for lifting the naval blockade.

Tehran insists the seven-day window will not begin until $24 billion in foreign reserves becomes fully accessible and restrictions on container clearance at Bandar Abbas are lifted.

Iran has also insisted that its seven-point de-escalation plan, based on the Islamabad Memorandum of Understanding, remain separate from negotiations over uranium enrichment, International Atomic Energy Agency inspections and regional missile defense arrangements.

It remains unclear whether Washington accepts that separation. Tehran's position suggests it is seeking immediate economic relief while avoiding concessions on broader strategic issues.

The divide leaves Tehran confronting an awkward calculation. The disruption around Hormuz may be imposing higher costs on Iran's adversaries just as hardliners intended, but it is also increasing the economic pressure driving others in Tehran toward a deal.

The comments, however, are not a representative measure of Iranian public opinion, and Persian-language posts alone do not establish whether their authors live inside Iran or abroad.

Netanyahu, born in Tel Aviv on October 21, 1949, grew up in an academic family and spent part of his adolescence in the United States.

After serving in an Israeli military special forces unit, he studied at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology before entering politics. He first became prime minister in 1996, aged 46.

The leader of the right-wing Likud party is Israel's longest-serving prime minister, and confronting the Islamic Republic has been a central feature of his political career.

In September 2012, Netanyahu displayed a cartoon drawing of a bomb at the United Nations, drawing a red line across it to warn about Iran's nuclear program.

The confrontation later extended beyond nuclear warnings.

During the 12-day war in June 2025, Israel struck Iranian military and nuclear facilities. Netanyahu told Iranians that the military campaign could pave the way for them to achieve freedom from the Islamic Republic.

Joint Israeli-US strikes on February 28, 2026, which killed Iran's former Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei, marked another stage in the confrontation.

“The people of Iran deserve to live in freedom, security and dignity,” Netanyahu wrote in a Persian-language Instagram post on Thursday, October 22, five days before Israel's election.

The message came as Netanyahu faced significant political competition at home, even as Persian-speaking users expressed enthusiasm for his continued leadership.

His messages have resonated with some opponents of the Islamic Republic who hope Israeli pressure will contribute to political change in Iran.

Netanyahu's familiar nickname, Bibi, has taken on new forms among Persian-speaking admirers.

They call him Bibi Joon, adding a Persian term of affection, or Bibi Gol, a reference to a popular song by Iranian singer Moein.

The phenomenon has also attracted Netanyahu's attention.

Netanyahu himself referred to the nickname during a CBS News interview in May 2026, saying Iranians called him “Bibi-joon,” meaning beloved Bibi.

Saudi Defense Minister Khalid bin Salman, Pakistani army chief Asim Munir and Turkish intelligence chief Ibrahim Kalin will travel to Tehran, Reuters reported on Monday, citing Saudi and Iranian officials.

Ali Bagheri Kani, deputy secretary of Iran's Supreme National Security Council, confirmed the visit.

The initiative was proposed during Munir's visit to Tehran last week, when he met President Masoud Pezeshkian and other senior officials, according to a senior Iranian official.

Iranian media reported that officials told Munir they would not negotiate with Washington until Tehran's demands were met.

The visit marks the first high-level meeting between Iran and all three members of the Mecca defense pact since the escalation of hostilities.

Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Turkey signed the agreement in August, establishing that a military attack on one member would be considered an attack on all three.

Pakistan has ratified the pact, while ratification remains pending in Turkey's parliament.

Earlier this month, the three countries agreed to implement their defense commitments and rapidly deploy military personnel and capabilities to Saudi Arabia.

The agreement faces its first significant test following increased Houthi attacks on Saudi territory.

Saudi Arabia announced on Sunday that it had intercepted another wave of missiles and drones.

Qatari officials also visited Tehran on Sunday for talks with Iranian authorities.

Previous mediation by Qatar and Pakistan helped secure a temporary halt to hostilities, but the agreement collapsed when fighting resumed.

Indirect contacts between Tehran and Washington have continued through both mediators, a process also referenced by Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi.

US President Donald Trump raised the possibility of renewed military operations against Iran on Friday.

Iranian officials have warned that any further attack would trigger a stronger response.

The Qatari team was in the Iranian capital as part of an effort coordinated with the United States to reduce regional tensions, an official briefed on the matter told Reuters.

“Do I make a deal with them or do I finish them off? That's the only question,” Trump said.

Iran, for its part, is also keeping the diplomatic channel alive without signaling that it is prepared to accept Washington's terms.

Foreign Ministry spokesperson Esmaeil Baghaei said on Sunday that messages were continuing to pass between Iran and the United States through intermediaries and that Tehran had not closed the window on diplomacy.

At the same time, Iranian officials have accused Washington of demanding what Tehran characterizes as its “complete surrender.”

The two sides also remain divided over the terms of a broader settlement. Washington has continued to make Iran's nuclear capabilities central to any settlement. Trump told FOX 2 Detroit on Sunday that the war would not end until the United States was satisfied Tehran did not have nuclear weapons capabilities, while saying he was prepared to take time to reach an agreement.

“I'm going to make a great deal. I'm a longterm person; I'm not a short term person,” he said.

Iran's ambassador to Pakistan said on Saturday that Tehran remained willing to resume negotiations under a Pakistan-brokered interim agreement reached in June. He said Iran had offered through Islamabad to reopen the Strait of Hormuz if Washington lifted its naval blockade.

The June agreement, brokered with Qatar's help, was intended to restore shipping, end the blockade and begin negotiations toward a broader agreement addressing Iran's nuclear program.

The Iranian ambassador accused Trump of undermining the latest diplomatic effort by insisting that the blockade would remain in place.

The Qatari intervention follows a broader diplomatic push by regional and European governments concerned that another attack on Iran could expand the war.

Foreign ministers from Turkey, Saudi Arabia, Egypt, Pakistan, Britain, France, Germany and Italy met in Istanbul on Friday, where Turkish Foreign Minister Hakan Fidan warned that renewed attacks on Iran would widen the conflict and said diplomacy remained the only way to end it.

The meetings brought together governments with different relationships with Washington and Tehran but a shared interest in preventing another round of fighting from spreading across the region.

That concern has grown as Iran has threatened retaliation against US and regional interests and attacks on commercial shipping have continued around the Strait of Hormuz.

Seven oil tankers were reported attacked or damaged by Iranian forces or naval mines in and around the strait on Friday. Despite the continuing risks, tankers have been moving oil through a US-protected corridor.

CENTCOM commander Adm. Brad Cooper flew over the Strait of Hormuz on Sunday as the US military said it had supported more than 3,000 commercial vessel transits through the waterway over the past six months and helped Persian Gulf partners export more than 1.5 billion barrels of crude oil since summer.

Cooper said the main shipping routes remained clear of mines laid by Iran's Revolutionary Guards. CENTCOM said more than 95% of vessels had passed safely in recent months despite attacks by the Guards.

The confrontation has also spread to Yemen, where renewed Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia have added to concerns over regional energy infrastructure and shipping routes.

The diplomatic push is therefore taking place while both sides retain significant means of pressure.

Washington continues its naval blockade of Iran while keeping open the possibility of renewed military action. Tehran, meanwhile, retains its ability to threaten shipping through the Strait of Hormuz and US and regional interests.

Those pressures also form part of the bargaining over a possible settlement. Tehran has linked reopening the Strait of Hormuz to an end to the US blockade, while Washington has continued to press Iran over its nuclear program, with the sequencing of concessions remaining a key obstacle.

The result is a diplomatic effort taking place under the threat of escalation rather than after that threat has receded.

For now, both sides continue to signal that an agreement remains possible. But neither has publicly indicated that it is prepared to bridge the differences that have stalled negotiations, leaving Qatar and other mediators seeking an opening while Washington and Tehran prepare for the consequences of another breakdown.

Iran may be approaching a mental-health emergency as prolonged uncertainty over war, economic pressure and the future takes a growing toll on the public, the head of the country’s Psychology and Counseling Organization said on Sunday.

Asked by ILNA how far Iranian society was from a mental-health emergency, Alireza Agha-Yousefi said: “I don’t think we are very far from it.”

He pointed to marriage, divorce and birth rates as areas of concern and said authorities needed to act before psychological and social problems became more severe.

Agha-Yousefi said the prolonged state of neither full-scale war nor peace was itself becoming a major source of pressure.

“The overall condition of society in a situation of neither war nor peace can create significant psychological strain,” he said.

He said people normally try to meet their needs while also responding to threats, but when both are present at the same time, threats tend to dominate.

“Uncertainty about the future is one of the most difficult situations,” he said, adding that it deprives people of the ability to plan their lives.

Agha-Yousefi said prolonged uncertainty could produce severe anxiety and, over time, lead to both physical and psychological exhaustion.

He cautioned that the consequences of intense stress do not always appear immediately.

“The effects may show themselves one or two years later,” he said, citing depression, marital conflict and physical health problems as possible outcomes.

He also pointed to prolonged muscle tension, joint and spinal pain and gastrointestinal problems as conditions that can be associated with chronic stress.

President Masoud Pezeshkian last week also described the current “no war, no peace” situation as the worst position Iran could be in, as tensions with Washington remained unresolved.

Agha-Yousefi said the same economic conditions contributing to psychological strain were also making treatment less accessible.

He said demand for counselling had risen, but inflation and declining purchasing power meant many people could no longer afford psychological services.

“Psychological services are not covered by basic insurance,” he said, adding that official figures on the number of people seeking treatment could therefore give a misleading picture.

He said some people who need help simply do not seek it because of the cost.

A proposal to bring psychological services under insurance coverage had been submitted to the organization’s central council around four months ago but had yet to be approved, he said.

Pressure on families and social stability

Agha-Yousefi also said Iran appeared to be moving toward greater social dislocation.

“It seems our social situation is, to some extent, moving toward anomie or social normlessness,” he said.

He warned that as social stress rises, the effects can spread into family life and wider social behavior, including later marriage, addiction and greater instability within households.

“When problems enter the family, they can be a sign of deeper social damage,” he said.

He added that severe family pressures could, in some cases, contribute to extreme violence, including killings within families.

Agha-Yousefi said delaying preventive measures would make the psychological, physical and social consequences much harder and more expensive to address later.

His comments come as speculation grows over possible renewed US and Israeli military action against Iran.

Recent reports have said the US military has prepared options for further strikes after the November 3 midterm elections if President Donald Trump authorizes them.

That uncertainty has added another layer of pressure to an economy already strained by inflation, a weakening currency and declining purchasing power.

Durante la jornada de manifestaciones pacíficas convocadas a nivel nacional por la Cámara Nacional del Autotransporte de Carga (CANACAR), las vialidades de Coahuila se mantuvieron operativas y sin afectaciones a la movilidad, informó el gobernador Manolo Jiménez Salinas.
