#28236. Your AI story has an expiration date

📅 23 Oct 2026 | Category: other | Source

A biotech or SaaS company wants to be seen as an AI company. A health company wants to be seen as a tech company. Some have earned the label. Many are chasing a valuation multiple, knowing capital flows to whoever carries the tag. We’ve seen this script before. During the dot-com boom, adding “.com” to a corporate name could trigger a speculative surge. A decade later, companies rushed to rebrand around blockchain.

I run a communications firm. Narrative can change how people see a business, but it can’t fix the fundamentals.

STORY MATTERS—TO A POINT

Investors, employees, and reporters invest in stories, especially concerning frontier technology. When a company is building something that won’t exist for years, it can’t point to a finished product. It has to give people reasons to believe. Jensen Huang positioned NVIDIA not as a graphics card maker for gamers, but as an AI computing company years before ChatGPT made AI a household word. NVIDIA’s story ran years ahead of the market, and the company built fast enough to catch it.

Your story can run ahead of your business. That is what vision is for. But it can’t run ahead forever. A good story sets expectations. If the business doesn’t hit the expectation, however, the market responds in many ways: a fall-from-grace headline, a disengaged investor, or a stalled deal.

THE GAP HAS A SHELF LIFE

I call the distance between your vision and your execution “the gap.” Opportunity and disaster both live there. How long you can stay in the gap depends on how many people are watching.

A young company in stealth mode gets plenty of room to maneuver. A well-known brand gets very little, because reporters, analysts, and competitors track every claim. Early on, the story is nearly all you have. Later, especially once you’re public, people start reading the receipts, such as partnerships that were really one-off pilots or R&D spending that never became a product. Wins extend your runway. Big claims with no wins shorten it, and eventually you’re in a crisis.

Right now, that room is smaller than usual. Reporters are tired of AI hype, and the public is uneasy as well. Even as of mid-2023, Pew Research found that 52% of Americans were more concerned than excited about AI, while only 10% felt genuinely excited. As Fast Company reported during the spring AI backlash, public trust is wavering while regulatory scrutiny grows. The SEC chair warned against “AI washing” in late 2023, and the agency brought its first two AI-washing cases forward in 2024. Since then, a steady stream of related cases has followed.

When the gap gets too wide, external forces close it for you. WeWork pitched a community movement while making its money renting office space; in 2018, Fast Company asked whether WeWork could be worth $40 billion. It peaked at $47 billion in 2019 and filed for bankruptcy in 2023. More recently, Apple agreed to a $250 million settlement over how it marketed delayed features. The more attention you have, the faster someone else closes the gap for you.

REPOSITIONING OR COSTUME?

Repositioning is grounded in proof. Without that proof, it is just a costume. Before you claim a new category, ask your team a few questions:

  • Who on our leadership team or advisory board has built this kind of business before?
  • Which customers use the product today, and will they say so on the record?
  • What would a skeptical reporter find after 10 minutes of searching your company or execs in Google or an LLM?
  • Could we let someone see how the product works without a human guiding the demo behind the scenes?
  • If we took the word “AI” out of our pitch deck, would there still be a business worth writing about?
  • What is our next proof point, and when does it land?

The answers make up your paper trail: people who validate you, customer data that backs you up, and a clear account of the problem you’re solving. A costume has none of that.

The gap can also run the other way, with the technology ahead of the story. Then the job is helping the narrative catch up.

DO THE WORK FIRST

Bill Walsh, who won three Super Bowls as the coach of the San Francisco 49ers, wrote a book called The Score Takes Care of Itself. He argues that if you focus maniacally on preparation, details, and quality, the desired result will follow. The same goes for your story. If you do the work, category leadership and a higher valuation usually come next.

If you’re a CEO or a communications lead, check your own paper trail before a reporter or a regulator does it for you. Ask your team for proof and verify it yourself. Storytelling is my business, so I’m telling you the story only goes so far. Mind the gap.

Tyler Perry is co-CEO of Mission North.

#35797. Origin Range Requests: Serving large files without range-request tuning

📅 23 Oct 2026 | Category: technology | Source

A video player seeking to the middle of a movie asks for a few megabytes. Under our old range caching, that one request could make Cloudflare pull 128 MiB from the origin and move it between data centers, sometimes after the viewer had already left. Range requests are how large files get delivered, and caching them naively easily leads to wasted network bandwidth and wasted disk operations when running a CDN at scale.

So we rebuilt how our cache handles them. For R2, Cloudflare's object storage, p99 time to first byte dropped by about half, and a follow-up improvement cut tiered cacheÂ traffic for R2Â and similar workloads by about 6%.

Today, we're making that new implementation available as Origin Range Requests, a self-service feature on all plans. There's no range size to tune and no need to contact support. Turn it on with a Cache Rule, and Cloudflare handles the rest.

An HTTP range request asks for part of a resource instead of the whole resource. The client sends a normal GETÂ with a RangeÂ header:

If the server supports the request, it returns 206 Partial ContentÂ and identifies the returned bytes with Content-Range:

Range requests are common for large objects. A video player can request bytes around a seek position instead of the whole file. A download manager can resume after an interruption. A package manager can fetch part of an artifact. A client can inspect the end of a file without downloading everything before it.

The client decides whether to request a range, and Cloudflare does not change that request. Instead, Origin Range Requests changes how Cloudflare satisfies it.

Cloudflare has supported range-request caching for years via static zone configuration.

The previous implementation resembled the cache-slicing described in an nginx blog post: each client request was mapped to fixed-size ranges of the file, with the range size and file extensions configured per zone.

The size of each range created a tradeoff: small ranges led to many small origin requests and memory pressure on Cloudflare from coalescing all of the requests in-memory, while large ranges caused Cloudflare to download much more data than the client requested. Updates to the range size itself would invalidate cache entries, since the size was part of the cache key.

This design also meant people requesting content through our network could have unpleasant experiences accessing these files. Concurrent requests that fell inside the same range, no matter how far apart each clientâs requested range was, could block each other. Our nginx-based cache also couldnât combine range caching with concurrent streaming, a feature we built to let multiple clients read a file from cache while it's still being written. Turning both on together cut responses short, so each range had to be fully downloaded before anyone could read it, and popular files needed a separate origin fetch for every range.

The extra bytes could also cross several parts of our distributed cache: from the origin to one data center, between cache tiers, and then toward the client. Every hop multiplied the waste. No value works equally well for a small seek, a sequential download, a large media file, and every origin and cache topology.

Our internal cache limitations were directly exposed to customers, who should not need to understand our capabilities in-depth to have files served efficiently.

Fortunately, our transition from nginx to a Pingora-based caching server created an opportunity to address the implementation problems that had forced a hard-to-use setting onto customers.

The fix came from something Pingora was already doing. To make it easy to resume interrupted downloads, it stores every cached file on disk in fixed-size chunks. If the cache already thinks in chunks, the range size doesn't need to be a setting at all, it can just be the chunk!

For a range request, Cloudflare checks which requested bytes are already cached. It serves those bytes directly. For missing bytes, it fetches origin ranges aligned to the cache's fixed-sized-chunk boundaries, stores the result in the same chunks used for ordinary cache storage, and returns only the bytes requested by the client.

For example, a request for bytes 150-250Â can cause Cloudflare to fetch the aligned chunk containing those bytes. The downstream response still contains only 150-250.

This does not eliminate extra work for requests smaller than our chunk size â such a request can still require fetching the whole chunk containing it. We also are taking on the cost of more work for non-aligned ranges. The difference is that the extra work is bounded and predictable, and no longer determined by a customer-selected range size that can cause unpredictable effects on us and origins.

Parsing the RangeÂ header is straightforward. The harder problem is producing one correct response when the requested bytes are split across cached data, an origin response, and downloads already in progress.

This is a coordinator, not a single cache lookup. One request can use a cached prefix, an in-flight download, and a newly fetched suffix. The internal origin response can be larger than the downstream response, but the downstream status and headers describe only the requested range.

If an existing download covers another request's bytes, the second request can attach to that download instead of starting another origin fetch. This shares work during bursts for popular large files.

The design uses the existing cache data model. Headers identify the asset version, the body is stored in bounded chunks, and metadata records which ranges were written successfully.

Composed range responses are valid only when all bytes belong to the same representation. The coordinator checks that each origin response matches the requested range, that the total asset size is stable, and that the entity tag remains consistent across subrequests.

The If-RangeÂ header lets a client request a range only if the representation still matches a known validator. If it does not match, Cloudflare returns the full representation instead of combining bytes from different versions.

Cloudflare also avoids composing ranges for a representation it is about to decompress because the range over a set of compressed bytes would be different from those selected by the range in a decompressed response. In these cases, Cloudflare serves the transformed full response rather than advertising a range it cannot honor correctly.

Before opening Origin Range Requests to everyone, we moved existing users of the old range-caching feature onto the new implementation, starting with the workloads that hurt us most.

Those workloads had a lot of concurrent range requests against very large files, often on very spiky traffic. Configured range sizes on the old system ran from tens of kilobytes to tens of gigabytes. With a large range size, a request that wouldn't fill a floppy disk could make us download a few DVDs' worth of data from the origin and move it across several data centers, sometimes after the client had already given up and gone elsewhere. With a small range size, one request turned into a long line of tiny origin requests, each holding memory while it waited. Both patterns caused real operational incidents for us.

We intend to make this the default way certain file types are served on our network, modifiable by cache rules.

This feature does not change normal cache eligibility. Enabling it does not make an otherwise uncacheable response cacheable. The origin also needs to support byte ranges for partial origin fetches to be useful.

To turn it on, create a Cache RuleÂ that matches your large-file traffic and set Origin Range Requests to On. You can do this in the dashboard under Caching > Cache Rules, or through the API with origin_range_requests: { "mode": "on" }. Customers choose the traffic; the edge handles the cache coordination and correctness requirements.

#28237. Healthcare AI is moving at warp speed

📅 23 Oct 2026 | Category: other | Source

It’s 2 a.m. in the ER, and a physician is asking the hospital’s new AI assistant to summarize a patient’s chart. The cursor spins for minutes while the AI tool waits on the decades-old database underneath to answer, something it was never designed to do.

The electronic health record (EHR) system is too old and was not built to keep up with AI’s demands. It’s like asking a 50-year-old runner to sprint with a 25-year-old; a pulled muscle or torn ligament is coming. Hospitals everywhere are operating this way as they onboard AI tools, not knowing whether their underlying systems can withstand the warp-speed demands of these technologies.

In another industry, a slow or sometimes-crashing system may create headaches for the sales team. In healthcare, patients are at risk. The doctor may have forgotten how to find the information “the old way” as the cursor spins and spins. The intersection of overloaded and aging systems, outsized patient needs, and today’s desire for speed and emergent technologies has the makings of a crisis. And it is a crisis nobody in healthcare is enthused to talk about or proactively solve.

HEALTHCARE IS DAZZLED BY AI PROMISES

Healthcare AI comes with massive upside; insights are frequently published touting how AI is making healthcare more prescriptive, efficient, and cheaper. It can help with workforce shortages and faster documentation, while providing speedy answers and shrinking time spent navigating prior authorizations. OpenAI is now teaming up with Epic, the largest EHR company in the U.S., which also has its own roadmap of AI features. Major national insurers are embedding solutions from those same AI companies onto their platforms. Health systems are stacking chat overlays and AI layers on top of all of it.

The AI genie is out of the bottle, and nobody wants to put it back. The problem, however, is that few leaders are asking whether the underlying EHR systems can handle the newfound pressure. If no, many large health systems and insurers could find themselves in predicaments with their EHRs. When something gives, it will give out without warning.

The problem is not unique to healthcare. Reporting from 2023 cited Dell estimating that 70% of software used by Fortune 5000 companies was developed at least 20 years ago. In a 2026 survey, 97% of U.S. database professionals said their organizations run at least some versions of outdated programs.

Every piece of healthcare software sits on a data architecture built for the structured, intermittent access of one clinician clicking through one screen at a time. An AI chatbot changes that entirely, as it queries the data in real time and then decides what output the doctor sees. The data architecture was never designed to be asked that quickly or that often. Old or misread data could easily become a confident chatbot response to medical staff.

Multiply that by thousands of users, as the AI queries and normal human staff on deck simultaneously hit the EHR day and night. A slowdown of the EHR under this duress doesn’t stay inside the new AI tool. It will drag down every workflow across the entire health system. The healthcare industry is essentially testing the bridge’s weight limit by driving more trucks across it instead of checking the bridge’s rating.

WHAT’S THE PLUMBING LIKE?

Rebuilding data architecture is expensive, complicated, and genuinely risky. Upgrading a production database can carry operational risk, including compatibility problems and service disruption, while running unsupported software creates security and reliability risks.

It’s like painting a house every few years, but not fixing what’s behind the walls, like aging plumbing or electrical wiring, until something breaks. Companies and governments struggle to fund work like modernizing infrastructure when it has no direct line to revenue, especially when the only return is avoiding a failure or crisis. It seems easier just to repaint.

The fast, unsupervised access by AI has made this old problem urgent in a way it wasn’t even six months ago. If one hospital runs a small AI pilot and it goes wrong, the damage remains small. If every hospital in a health system across a state moves at once, it could trigger a crisis of its own making without ever knowing it was possible. Leaders worry about cybersecurity and hacking, but few are looking underneath the hood.

Stakeholders should share responsibility to avoid fallout. That means health system technologists confirming that infrastructure can support a new tool in production before they deploy it. Vendors adding AI to existing healthcare platforms must know their own systems’ capabilities, because that creates risk for customers. AI builders offering products that bolt onto someone else’s underlying system will push that system’s limits. What’s the plan when it freezes up?

ASK THE HARD QUESTIONS NOW

It’s time to focus on risk mitigation. Healthcare is already in the AI race, driven by momentum and a desire to keep up with changing technologies. But as a sector where patient safety is on the line, we cannot afford to ignore what AI is being bolted onto and what could happen when it fails.

A 50-year-old runner who pulls a muscle keeping pace with a 25-year-old won’t have just one injury if he keeps running at the same speed; he will have them over and over again. Decades-old architecture will break down the same way when it’s asked to keep up indefinitely.

Three steps are needed, the first being to audit reality. Health systems and insurers must understand what their infrastructure can handle when AI comes calling. That includes how old systems are and how things perform when both AI and staff query at the same time. Second, invest. Leaders should proactively fund infrastructure upgrades with the same urgency they are bringing to AI rollouts by supporting or replacing underpinning technologies before it’s too late. Third, commit to a roadmap, as modernization endeavors are a multiyear effort and the long-term return will be AI that works.

The value of AI cannot be realized until the industry addresses what nobody wants to talk about—that AI adoption is moving faster than anyone’s understanding of the risk beneath it.

Effie Carlson is CEO of Watershed Health.
