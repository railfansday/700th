#36009. Will anyone end Spain's domination of women's Ballon d'Or?

📅 26 Oct 2026 | Category: technology | Source

Alessia Russo (left) and Khadija Shaw (right) are among the contenders seeking to deny Alexia Putellas (centre) a third Ballon d'Or

When the Ballon d'Or for best female footballer is dished out on Monday night, the chances are it will be a familiar figure that lifts the coveted trophy.

Spanish icons Aitana Bonmati and Alexia Putellas have hoovered up the past five awards - and Putellas is favourite to win it for a third time.

In May, the now London City Lionesses forward captained Barcelona to a quadruple, including a third Champions League trophy in four years and their seventh straight Liga F title.

Arguably, the only reason Bonmati is not in the running this time is because of a serious leg injury suffered on international duty in November 2025.

So will Putellas clean up once again? Is this trophy yet another example of Spain's increasing dominance of the women's game?

Or does anyone else on the 30-strong shortlist stand a chance of wrenching it from her grasp?

In Barcelona, Spain boast the world's most dominant club side.

The Catalans have contested seven of the past eight Champions League finals (winning four) and have been domestic champions every year since 2019-20, clinching the title by an average of 15 points above second place in that time.

Then, there is the national team. World champions in 2023, losing finalists at the Euros to England in 2025 and then winners of the Nations League last December, beating Germany in the final.

At the heart of all this success has been Putellas, who scored 21 goals and produced 13 assists during Barcelona's sensational season.

And it is not just her output that makes her case. Her all-round influence captaining an already stellar side cannot be overstated.

So, with 38 trophies in her possession already, the 2021 and 2022 Ballon d'Or winner appears poised to take it once again.

"Give it to her!" Spanish football expert Guillem Balague told BBC Sport. "Along with Marta, she is the best player in history. No doubt she was the best player in this campaign â and she won everything!"

Unsurprisingly, Spain have more players on the shortlist than any other nation (six) and eight of the starting line-up from the Champions League final are in the running for this award.

Among them are star striker Claudia Pina, who recently scored four against Real Madrid, goalkeeper Cata Coll and midfielder Patri Guijarro.

While Putellas is now tormenting Women's Super League defences in the blue of London City, it is another forward in this country that perhaps has the best case for consideration.

Khadija Shaw scored 21 goals in the league last season to fire Manchester City to their first title in a decade, adding another five in the FA Cup as they completed the double.

The Jamaica striker has been the WSL's top scorer for the past three seasons, scored 90 goals since her arrival in summer 2021 and was rewarded with a lucrative four-year deal in June.

"We haven't had a major tournament but there are some amazing players on the list," said former England international Ellen White. "I would love to see Shaw up there - brilliant goalscoring."

Shaw is part of a five-strong City contingent â they had none last season â but they all may fall short because they did not play in last season's Champions League.

Arsenal's Alessia Russo was second to Shaw for goals last season and impressed in the Champions League, picking up nine goals.

Last year, she came third in the Ballon d'Or voting last year behind Bonmati and club team-mate Mariona Caldentey.

That came off the back of the Gunners' Champions League victory in May 2025. Exiting in the semi-finals to Lyon this year may cost her a shot at winning the award this year.

One final candidate to break the Spanish stranglehold is, perhaps unsurprisingly, a Barcelona player. Poland's Ewa Pajor scored 11 goals in just 10 Champions League games last season, including a hat-trick in the final against Lyon.

The 29-year-old won the Women's Gerd Muller trophy for top scorer last season and finished eighth in the overall rankings.

Maybe it is her turn to take home the top prize.

The award covers the period from 3 August 2025 until 19 July 2026 and is based on individual performances, collective performances and fair play.

Selected international journalists â one from each nation in the top 50 of the Fifa world rankings - rank their top 10 candidates, with points distributed on a descending scale.

The Ballon d'Or is then awarded to the player with the highest total number of points.

In previous seasons, the ceremony has come under fire for being too Euro-centric and for its scheduling during international breaks in the women's game.

This year, the event is taking place during the domestic calendar, solving the date issue. However, the lack of US-based players from the National Women's Super League (NWSL) remains a fair criticism.

One reason for this may be that the NWSL season runs from February to November and therefore does not marry with the dates this award covers.

It means the likes of Washington Spirit's Trinity Rodman are notable absentees from a list purported to chart the world's best players.

#29790. Meta's $42B in penalties is a warning to AI

📅 26 Oct 2026 | Category: other | Source

A tidal wave of litigation is catching up to Meta, leading to $42 billion in recent penalties and settlements, a powerful reminder to AI companies that they could face a court reckoning even when regulation lags.

Why it matters: AI companies are racing to build better models and autonomous agents, but they don't enjoy the same legal protections that shielded web platforms for decades. The scale of the penalties Meta is facing offers a preview of potential legal exposure.

The big picture: For years, Facebook's parent company navigated a seemingly endless string of scandals — weathering allegations of election interference, privacy lapses and platform harms — and was largely protected from severe litigation penalties due to Section 230.

  • The provision — from a historic 1996 law passed as the internet was just beginning to change modern life — protects tech platforms from being sued over content created by their users.
  • Legal experts say that law doesn't apply to AI. Models create their own content, so companies could face traditional product liability and negligence claims.

Catch up quick: The two massive rulings highlight how legal risk for tech giants can extend by a decade, or proceed much faster.

  • Meta was just hit with a $25 billion penalty over the Cambridge Analytica scandal after a jury found the company misled users about Facebook's privacy practices. Many details about that event surfaced nearly 10 years ago. The company said it believes it will win an appeal, and legal experts have said the penalty will likely be reduced.
  • In August, the company agreed to a $17 billion settlement with states related to the impact of Meta products such as Instagram on teen mental health.

State of play: A key cog in the Trump administration's regulatory efforts is the threat of litigation.

  • Liability is sure to feature prominently in the risks AI giants such as Anthropic and OpenAI spell out to shareholders as they prepare for massive public listings. Anthropic's IPO is expected before Thanksgiving.
  • The companies face an array of legal threats, including harms caused directly by their models, the potential damage from bad actors who use them and copyright matters that remain far from settled.
  • The potential to be hauled into court isn't limited to AI companies; it could also extend to firms that use AI.

Yes, but: Big legal penalties take years to arrive, meaning a product can take off and revolutionize the world long before a court determines whether safeguards are adequate.

  • As recent cases have shown, the AI paper trail is extensive, and records of internal warnings, testing and product decisions will shape the findings by judges and juries.
  • Warnings about catastrophe from AI executives will also be ready at hand, and variations on "I told you so" aren't likely to indemnify CEOs.

The bottom line: Meta's recent penalty offers a warning to AI companies: legal exposure can build slowly, then become enormous.
