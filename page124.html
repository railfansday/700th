#35996. 'Cutting doubles would destroy careers' - angry players urge ATP rethink

📅 26 Oct 2026 | Category: technology | Source

Finland's Harri Heliovaara (left) and Britain's Henry Patten - the world number ones and reigning Wimbledon champions - have signed the open letter

More than 150 men's doubles players - including British world number one Henry Patten and the legendary Bryan brothers - have urged the ATP Tour to rethink "drastic" changes which they believe could kill the format.

In a show of collective action, the players have written an open letter to the men's governing body as they fight proposals which would cut draw sizes and reduce prize money from 2028.

"We're at risk of losing a discipline of tennis which is historic - but has a big future," Dutch player Sem Verbeek, one of the architects of the letter, told BBC Sport.

"These proposals would destroy careers, livelihoods and dreams."

The ATP proposed halving the doubles draws at its flagship Masters 1000 events to 16 teams, with smaller tournaments reduced to eight teams.

The percentage of prize money devoted to doubles could fall from 20% to 10% - with the difference redistributed to singles players.

'You're taking our jobs' - doubles players angry at proposed cuts

On Monday, a group of 137 current players, 28 legends and 20 national federations signed a letter which they hope will pressure the ATP into rethinking the proposals.

The tour's board - comprised of chairman Andrea Gaudenzi, four tournament representatives and four player representatives - will vote on the plans at next month's ATP Finals in Turin.

The doubles reform is part of a wider review by the ATP, which it says is designed to "strengthen" the tour from 2028.

The ATP aims to create "a more sustainable long-term model", insisting it wants to "maintain the important role" of doubles.

"Commercial success matters. Fairness, player welfare and the long-term health of tennis matter just as much," the players' letter says.

"Let us grow tennis together, without leaving doubles players behind."

The proposals provoked an angry response from the men's doubles cohort when they were revealed in July.

British pair Lloyd Glasspool and Julian Cash, who won Wimbledon in 2025 and became world number ones, accused the ATP of trying to "kill part of the sport".

Others within tennis believe reform is needed. They suggest a 20% share of the prize money for doubles is disproportionate to the interest shown by fans.

The doubles players argue the ATP should do more to promote their discipline and attract greater audiences, pointing out it is the format played by most club members and therefore more relatable.

The ATP says the proposals are based on data-led analysis centred on costs, revenue and viewership.

With fewer singles players now competing in doubles, it believes the current size of the standalone doubles events puts severe strain on tournaments.

"We support progress. We also understand the need to grow the sport, strengthen tournaments and increase commercial value," the players' letter says.

"Still, the proposed reforms go much too far."

Reigning Wimbledon champion Patten recently told BBC Sport the players were facing "an uphill battle", but remained "optimistic" the tour would consider counter-proposals. Since Wimbledon there have been several meetings between both sides, including recently at the Shanghai Masters.

The players still believe there is little appetite for negotiation from the ATP. But ATP sources insist a compromise is likely to be found.

Mike and Bob Bryan - the identical twins considered the greatest doubles pair of all-time - said they would support the group considering legal action to protect their discipline.

In 2005, the American brothers led players in a lawsuit against the ATP, when it planned radical changes to doubles.

"The tour's in the healthiest financial state it's ever been - in our opinion it should be creating more opportunities," Bob Bryan told BBC Sport in an interview at Wimbledon in July.

Mike Bryan added: "These proposed changes are anti-competitive. That's the stance we had to take in 2005. We still believe in doubles and we believe in the players fighting for this."

#35697. Two Iran Air retirees died after forgoing treatment over cost

📅 26 Oct 2026 | Category: technology | Source

Two retired employees of Iran's national airline died after being forced to abandon medical treatment they could not afford, the semi-official ILNA news agency reported on Monday, citing the head of the airline's retirees' association.

Jalal Hashemi, chairman of the Iran Air Retirees Association, said the deaths occurred over the past two to three weeks amid worsening financial and healthcare problems affecting around 13,000 retirees and pensioners of the airline, also known as Homa.

"The situation has become so catastrophic that in just the past two or three weeks, two of our retired colleagues were forced to abandon treatment because they could not afford hospital expenses, and unfortunately lost their lives," Hashemi told ILNA.

Iranian households face mounting economic hardship after years of inflation, sanctions and economic mismanagement, compounded by two rounds of war with the United States and Israel.

Iran Air retirees have staged repeated protests over pension payments, healthcare coverage and the management of their retirement fund.

In August, retirees gathered outside the airline's headquarters in Tehran to demand improvements to healthcare services and the reinstatement of suspended welfare payments.

Hashemi said Iran Air had paid only two of 12 installments owed to Iran Insurance Company under its insurance contract for the Iranian year ending in March 2026, prompting the insurer to suspend inpatient hospital coverage for retirees.

According to Hashemi, Iran Air retirees receive an average monthly pension of around 270 million rials (about $100), while approximately 5,600 receive minimum pensions of just over 180 million rials (about $66).

He estimated the shortfall in the airline's healthcare insurance fund at 4 trillion rials (about $1.5 million), saying no institution had accepted responsibility for covering the deficit.

Recent reductions in government-subsidized foreign currency for medicine imports, drug price increases and rising out-of-pocket healthcare costs have further strained the fund.

Hashemi also said Iran Air management had suspended a monthly welfare allowance of 60 million rials (about $22), citing financial losses from the war and reduced flights.

Flour shortages have forced bakeries to close in several Iranian cities and pushed up bread prices, with residents and bakers reporting disrupted supplies, falling purchasing power and growing uncertainty over their businesses.

Residents in Tabriz, Karaj, Kerman and Hamedan told Iran International that interruptions and delays in flour deliveries had disrupted independent bakeries and producers of various types of bread.

Several bakeries on a street in Tabriz have shut down, a resident said.

“I asked why, and they told me they weren't receiving flour. They keep promising that the flour quotas will be replenished today or tomorrow, but nothing happens.”

Previous reports have also documented flour shortages and bakery closures, with residents describing bread shortages and interruptions to production caused by electricity outages.

Flour supply disruptions shut bakeries and sandwich shops

Independent bakers have reported that difficulties obtaining flour have forced businesses to suspend operations.

A citizen of Hamedan in western Iran said flour shortages and rising prices had affected not only bakeries producing specialty bread but also sandwich shops.

Flour prices have risen, and suppliers are now saying there is no flour available at all, the resident said.

“Our bakery is closed because we have no flour, and nobody is taking responsibility,” a independent baker told Iran International.

The shortages have coincided with declining purchasing power, leaving bakeries struggling to secure supplies as customers struggle to afford bread.

“They don't have flour to give us, and I swear to God, people don't have money to buy bread either. Our economic situation has become critical,” another baker said.

Iran's flour distribution system distinguishes between subsidized bakeries and independent bakeries, which face different purchasing prices and supply arrangements.

Flour allocations to certain independent bakeries had been reduced, Bagher Khodadadi, head of supervision and inspection at the Khorasan Razavi provincial agriculture department, confirmed on October 21.

Khodadadi said the shortages in flour distribution had been addressed.

Bakeries reducing their operating hours because of flour shortages would be committing a violation and could face enforcement measures, he added.

The flour supply problems have also affected food retailers that depend on bakeries for their products, according to messages.

Two bakeries supplying bread to a supermarket in Kerman said they no longer had enough flour to continue production, the store's owner said.

“Two of the bakers who supply me called and said there was no flour left and they couldn't bake and deliver bread tomorrow. A famine has officially begun.”

A resident of Karaj also described a sharp increase in bread prices.

“I bought two baguettes and they cost 800,000 rials. The price of flour has also tripled.”

Iran International previously reported on October 8 that residents had described unannounced increases in bread prices and flour shortages in several areas.

Bread prices in Tehran province had risen by as much as 100% for certain varieties by June 23.

The increases have added to the financial pressure on households already struggling with declining purchasing power.

Uncertainty over flour quotas and pricing

The disruption has raised further questions about how flour quotas are allocated and priced for independent bakeries and producers of larger and semi-industrial bread varieties.

Flour allocations to these businesses were suspended about two weeks ago, according to another message received by Iran International.

The sender said suppliers had attributed the suspension to the government's failure to announce new flour prices, leaving bakeries uncertain about when deliveries would resume.

The resident also said certain flour mills were selling supplies on the black market without issuing invoices specifying prices.

Comments by provincial grain officials about flour allocations have added to questions over the distribution system and the availability of supplies.

Mizan said the case had been referred to court for further proceedings.

Marzban was sentenced in August 2019 to 23 years and nine months in prison on charges including insulting the supreme leader, propaganda against the Islamic Republic and cooperating with a hostile state, according to HRANA.

"Cartoonists should not be persecuted for their creative expression," said Julie Trébault, then director of PEN America's Artists at Risk Connection.

PEN America said the arrest reflected broader efforts by Iranian authorities to intimidate artists, journalists and activists.

Iran may be approaching a mental-health emergency as prolonged uncertainty over war, economic pressure and the future takes a growing toll on the public, the head of the country’s Psychology and Counseling Organization said on Sunday.

Asked by ILNA how far Iranian society was from a mental-health emergency, Alireza Agha-Yousefi said: “I don’t think we are very far from it.”

He pointed to marriage, divorce and birth rates as areas of concern and said authorities needed to act before psychological and social problems became more severe.

Agha-Yousefi said the prolonged state of neither full-scale war nor peace was itself becoming a major source of pressure.

“The overall condition of society in a situation of neither war nor peace can create significant psychological strain,” he said.

He said people normally try to meet their needs while also responding to threats, but when both are present at the same time, threats tend to dominate.

“Uncertainty about the future is one of the most difficult situations,” he said, adding that it deprives people of the ability to plan their lives.

Agha-Yousefi said prolonged uncertainty could produce severe anxiety and, over time, lead to both physical and psychological exhaustion.

He cautioned that the consequences of intense stress do not always appear immediately.

“The effects may show themselves one or two years later,” he said, citing depression, marital conflict and physical health problems as possible outcomes.

He also pointed to prolonged muscle tension, joint and spinal pain and gastrointestinal problems as conditions that can be associated with chronic stress.

President Masoud Pezeshkian last week also described the current “no war, no peace” situation as the worst position Iran could be in, as tensions with Washington remained unresolved.

Agha-Yousefi said the same economic conditions contributing to psychological strain were also making treatment less accessible.

He said demand for counselling had risen, but inflation and declining purchasing power meant many people could no longer afford psychological services.

“Psychological services are not covered by basic insurance,” he said, adding that official figures on the number of people seeking treatment could therefore give a misleading picture.

He said some people who need help simply do not seek it because of the cost.

A proposal to bring psychological services under insurance coverage had been submitted to the organization’s central council around four months ago but had yet to be approved, he said.

Pressure on families and social stability

Agha-Yousefi also said Iran appeared to be moving toward greater social dislocation.

“It seems our social situation is, to some extent, moving toward anomie or social normlessness,” he said.

He warned that as social stress rises, the effects can spread into family life and wider social behavior, including later marriage, addiction and greater instability within households.

“When problems enter the family, they can be a sign of deeper social damage,” he said.

He added that severe family pressures could, in some cases, contribute to extreme violence, including killings within families.

Agha-Yousefi said delaying preventive measures would make the psychological, physical and social consequences much harder and more expensive to address later.

His comments come as speculation grows over possible renewed US and Israeli military action against Iran.

Recent reports have said the US military has prepared options for further strikes after the November 3 midterm elections if President Donald Trump authorizes them.

That uncertainty has added another layer of pressure to an economy already strained by inflation, a weakening currency and declining purchasing power.

The war with Iran raises a familiar question for Israel: Can battlefield gains produce lasting peace? Its military history offers mixed answers, from unresolved victories to withdrawals and ceasefires that left adversaries armed.

Destroying Iran’s military capabilities and preventing their reconstruction are different objectives, requiring different measures of success.

Since 1948, Israel has fought conventional Arab armies and armed organizations such as Hezbollah and Hamas, and has increasingly confronted Iran directly through missile, drone and air attacks far beyond its borders. These forms of warfare have overlapped, posing different challenges for bringing the fighting to an end.

Their endings show how battlefield outcomes interact with diplomacy, outside pressure and arrangements for security after the fighting.

Fighting between Jewish and Arab communities followed the UN’s 1947 partition plan. After Israel declared independence in May 1948, neighboring Arab armies entered the conflict. Israel survived and gained territory beyond the boundaries proposed for the Jewish state.

In 1949, it signed separate armistice agreements with Egypt, Lebanon, Jordan and Syria. These established lines separating the forces, rather than permanent political settlements. The Egyptian agreement explicitly envisaged a subsequent peaceful settlement.

Israel’s survival did not resolve the territorial dispute or the displacement of Palestinians. The fighting ended without peace between Israel and its neighbors.

1956: Military gains, withdrawal under pressure

During the Suez crisis, Israel attacked Egypt in coordination with Britain and France, rapidly advancing across Sinai.

Pressure from the United States, the Soviet Union and the United Nations forced a withdrawal, completed in March 1957. Israel could not retain the territory it had captured, despite its battlefield success.

The outcome nevertheless brought security gains: Israel regained access to the Straits of Tiran, while a UN emergency force deployed in Sinai. The US State Department’s historical account distinguishes those gains from the territorial withdrawal.

The episode illustrates why withdrawal is not automatically evidence of military defeat, and why military superiority alone does not determine a war’s political outcome.

1967: Six days of fighting, decades of consequences

Israel’s opening airstrikes devastated Egypt’s air force. Within six days, Israeli forces had captured Sinai, Gaza, the West Bank, East Jerusalem and the Golan Heights.The war ended through ceasefires, without a formal surrender by the defeated Arab states. Israel’s decisive battlefield victory transformed the territorial balance but did not settle the conflict.

UN Security Council Resolution 242 subsequently established a framework linking Israeli withdrawal from occupied territory with an end to belligerency and recognition of states’ right to live within secure, recognized boundaries.

The military result was immediate. Disputes over occupation, borders and Palestinian rights lasted far beyond the fighting.

1973: From surprise attack to a peace treaty

Egypt and Syria attacked Israeli forces in Sinai and the Golan Heights in October 1973, initially making substantial gains. Israel counterattacked and crossed the Suez Canal before US-Soviet diplomacy and UN ceasefire resolutions helped stop the fighting.

The US State Department’s history describes the war as opening the way for Henry Kissinger’s shuttle diplomacy and, ultimately, the Egyptian-Israeli peace treaty. Egypt did not recover all Sinai through military action, but the war helped break the diplomatic stalemate.

The 1978 Camp David accords provided the framework for the 1979 treaty. Israel agreed to withdraw from Sinai in exchange for peace with Egypt. The main withdrawal was completed in 1982; the disputed Taba area was returned in 1989 following arbitration.

Egypt recovered territory through a combination of war and diplomacy. Israel exchanged territorial control for a political agreement with a major adversary.

Lebanon, 1982–2000: Initial success, prolonged conflict

Israel’s 1982 invasion drove into Beirut, where US-mediated arrangements secured the departure of Palestine Liberation Organization leaders and fighters. This was an evacuation from Beirut, not the elimination of every Palestinian armed presence in Lebanon.

Israel withdrew much of its force in 1985 but retained a southern security zone alongside the allied South Lebanon Army. Its remaining troops left in May 2000, following years of conflict with Hezbollah. UNIFIL’s historical account records both withdrawals.

The withdrawal was unilateral, without a peace treaty or formal Israeli surrender. Hezbollah portrayed it as a victory; Israel ended a prolonged deployment without resolving the underlying confrontation.

The departure of one armed organization’s leadership in 1982 had not produced a lasting end to fighting.

The 2006 war followed Hezbollah’s cross-border attack and capture of two Israeli soldiers. Israel launched extensive air and ground operations, but Hezbollah remained armed and continued firing rockets during the conflict.

Neither side’s survival or military performance alone explains the settlement. The operative mechanism was UN Security Council Resolution 1701, adopted on August 11, followed by a cessation of hostilities on August 14.

It called for Hezbollah to stop attacks, Israel to stop offensive operations, and Israeli forces to withdraw as Lebanese troops and an expanded UNIFIL deployed. It envisaged an area between the Blue Line and Litani River free of armed personnel and weapons other than those of the Lebanese government and UNIFIL.

Hezbollah was not disarmed. Nevertheless, UNIFIL says the arrangements helped sustain almost 17 years of relative calm before the escalation beginning in 2023. An incomplete settlement still had significant practical effects.

The Gaza wars of 2008–09, 2012, 2014 and 2021 ended without Hamas surrendering or losing all its military capabilities.

Scholars Efraim Inbar and Eitan Shamir described Israel’s approach as “mowing the grass”: periodically degrading an adversary’s capabilities and restoring deterrence rather than expecting a permanent political resolution through force. Their study appeared online in 2013 and in the Journal of Strategic Studies in 2014.

After Hamas’s October 7, 2023 attack, Israel set broader objectives, including dismantling its military and governing capabilities and recovering the hostages. Shamir argued that the attack marked a departure from the previous limited-campaign approach. That was his strategic assessment, rather than proof that eliminating Hamas was achievable.

The ensuing war devastated Gaza. The October 2025 ceasefire did not settle its future: a year later, the UN human rights office was still reporting killings despite the agreement.

Lebanon after 2023: Military damage and state authority

Israel’s 2024 campaign killed Hezbollah leader Hassan Nasrallah and other senior commanders, severely damaging its leadership. The fighting nevertheless moved toward a negotiated cessation of hostilities rather than formal surrender.

The US-French-brokered arrangement took effect on November 27, 2024, and again rested on implementing Resolution 1701, Lebanese state authority and Israeli withdrawal.

Disarmament remained contested. In October 2026, The Wall Street Journal reported that Hezbollah demanded the return of explosives seized by Lebanese authorities, illustrating the continuing struggle over weapons outside state control.

Iran: Ending operations or preventing reconstruction?

The June 2025 war involved Israeli attacks on Iranian military and nuclear targets and direct US strikes on Fordow, Natanz and Isfahan. US involvement therefore preceded the broader war of 2026.

Israel’s Foreign Ministry described the nuclear threat as “existential and imminent” in its account of the operation. That characterization was Israel’s stated justification, not an independently established finding that Iran possessed nuclear weapons. The same document records Israel’s acceptance of a US-proposed ceasefire on June 24.The broader confrontation in 2026 extended to shipping and energy supplies. By October, the US naval blockade and the standoff over reopening the Strait of Hormuz remained unresolved.

Netanyahu has explicitly advocated moving beyond containment. Addressing military cadets in February 2026, he declared there would be “no more containment of threats” and emphasized preemptive action. This supports describing a shift in his stated approach, without assuming a uniform doctrine across every Israeli institution.

Destroying launchers or facilities has measurable immediate effects. Preventing their reconstruction depends on industrial capacity, supply networks, political decisions and whatever monitoring or enforcement follows.

Within the approach of seeking to prevent Iran from rebuilding its military threat, two scenarios can be considered. Neither is a prediction or an exhaustive account of how the conflict might end.

The first is a military defeat sufficiently extensive to destroy much of Iran’s offensive capability and substantially constrain its reconstruction. Lasting results would depend on measures that prevent replenishment, rather than destruction alone.The second combines military defeat with the end of the Islamic Republic and its replacement by a political system that abandons hostility toward Israel and support for armed proxies.

That scenario depends on more than a change of leadership. It assumes a successor government both chooses and can enforce a different policy, including ending financial, military and operational support for allied armed groups. A government’s collapse would not itself establish those conditions.

The scenarios may overlap, but neither guarantees stability. Their outcomes would depend on political authority, security arrangements and the capacity to enforce commitments after combat ends.

Other possible outcomes include a negotiated agreement that constrains Iran’s capabilities without changing its government, or a prolonged armed standoff sustained by deterrence. Neither would necessarily eliminate the underlying hostility, though either could bring the current fighting to an end.

Israel’s earlier wars provide different benchmarks: territorial withdrawal under international pressure in 1956, land exchanged for peace with Egypt, unilateral withdrawal from Lebanon, and ceasefire arrangements that reduced fighting without disarming Hezbollah. Each ended a particular phase of conflict; only some produced a broader political settlement.

Iranians say they are paying more for slow, unreliable and heavily restricted internet after mobile operators raised data prices by up to 40%, with some reporting that their allowances run out unusually quickly.

Messages sent to Iran International described mounting frustration over the cost of staying connected, from families worried about paying for online classes to users struggling to send even a text message.

“All we have left of life in Iran is passing the time on our phones, and they are taking that away too,” one reader from Baharestan said.

Mobile operators Hamrah-e Aval and Irancell increased their data package prices by up to 40% early on Friday, October 23, according to state media.

One reader said a weekly four-gigabyte package had risen from 330,000 rials ($0.12) to 450,000 rials ($0.17). Another said a monthly six-gigabyte package that cost 530,000 rials ($0.20) three days earlier now cost 740,000 rials ($0.28).

The pressure comes as Iran’s official minimum wage stands at about 166.3 million rials per month, equivalent to roughly $62 at the unofficial-market exchange rate.

Others questioned why prices were rising again after earlier increases before and during the war.

“Didn’t they raise internet prices two months before the 40-day war? Didn’t they raise package prices again during the war, taking advantage of the blackout?” one reader asked, referring to the US-led war that began on February 28. “So what is this latest increase for?”

The higher charges have brought no improvement in service, according to accounts from users across the country.

A reader in the northern province of Mazandaran described Hamrah-e Aval’s connection as moving “at a snail’s pace,” saying Wi-Fi connections in many areas also cut out, even when accessing domestic messaging apps.

In Iranshahr, in southeastern Iran, another reader reported severely degraded ADSL service followed by a complete outage from 8 p.m. on Friday. Another said sending a text message on Telegram was difficult, let alone a photograph or video.

Iran International reported on Friday that users in Semnan, Shiraz, Tabriz, Kerman, Tehran and Tonekabon had complained of sharply slower connections, malfunctioning tools used to bypass internet filtering and difficulty accessing social media.

The Telecommunications Company of Iran has previously attributed some disruptions to “upgrading and optimizing technical infrastructure.”

The complaints come against a backdrop of extensive restrictions on internet access. Freedom House’s 2026 report gave Iran an internet freedom score of nine out of 100, down from 13.

The organization said Iran’s tiered internet system had made access to the global internet difficult and expensive, leaving much of the population restricted to a domestic network controlled by the authorities.

All global social media platforms, from X and Telegram to Facebook, Instagram, TikTok and YouTube, are banned in Iran.

Several readers also accused operators of “stealing data,” saying their allowances were depleted far faster than their usage would explain.

“I topped up my data package two days ago. Today, only 600 megabytes of my six gigabytes are left, even though we don’t have a decent connection,” one reader said.

Another said a six-gigabyte package bought for 570,000 rials ($0.21) had run out within two days despite limited use, and now cost 730,000 rials ($0.27) to replace.

For families, the increases have also raised concerns about the cost of education if gas shortages force schools to move classes online.

One reader said a one-gigabyte Hamrah-e Aval package cost 330,000 rials ($0.12), including tax. “With things like this, how are we supposed to buy data for online classes?”

Another said repeated price increases had left them unable to afford internet access at all.

#35795. Multi‑Ratio Video Resizing for Mobile Feed and Marketing

📅 26 Oct 2026 | Category: technology | Source

Serdar Cellat, Yuchen Xie, Bernadette Marcu, Blaid Burgess, Cody Jarrett, Carlos Pereira, Qing Shi, Samuel Carvajal, Manuel Pamplona, Dorothy Jung, Feiko Ritsema, Benjamin Klein, Alex Woo, Eugene Lok, Anna Pulido, Rebecka Swe, Sanford Holsapple, Ranjith Kumar ShettyIntroductionClips in the Netflix mobile app.At Netflix, a crucial part of our mission is ensuring our content looks stunning on any device. Vertical formats are increasingly important for Netflix, as we have 1.2B followers across our social media accounts, and have recently rolled out Clips, our vertical video feed in the Netflix mobile app. Traditionally, creating vertical assets meant editors and producers manually reframed horizontal videos in editing tools, a process that doesn’t scale as our catalog and use cases grow. To support both our in‑app feed and external marketing campaigns at scale, we built an automated multi‑ratio video cropping system, tightly integrated into our internal production tools, that can generate high‑quality vertical and other aspect‑ratio variants with minimal manual effort.Our Video Resizing ApproachConverting cinematic 16:9 footage into mobile‑friendly aspect ratios takes more than a simple center crop. Filmmaking often involves dynamic character blocking and complex camera movement. We need an intelligent “virtual camera” that can follow the key action, respond to how each scene is composed, and produce crops that feel as intentional as the original shot.We address this challenge by framing video resizing as a tracking and temporal optimization problem. Our pipeline processes each asset in three main stages, designed to produce 9:16, 4:5, and 1:1 crops in a single pass, without incurring additional computational overhead.An overview of the three-stage process converting horizontal footage into mobile aspect ratios.Shot Detection and SplittingFirst, we segment the video into atomic shots using a neural‑network–based shot boundary detector. In many cases, shots contain multiple characters conversing on opposite sides of the frame. To better handle these scenarios, we use an Active Speaker Detection based approach to logically split a single continuous shot into multiple sub‑shots. This allows our virtual camera to cut and re-focus on different speakers as the conversation flows. Our Active Speaker Detection leverages an internal face detector and LipFlap to reliably identify active speakers in both live‑action and animated titles.Shot splitting via Active Speaker Detection. The magenta box represents the dynamic vertical crop.Focus Region DiscoveryOnce shots and sub‑shots are defined, we identify the most important region in each frame. We combine face detections with visual saliency maps to filter and prioritize the faces that should be in focus. In scenes with multiple characters, we further rank faces using signals such as active speaker status and relative size. Our model aggregates these cues with shot‑level content understanding to establish a continuous “focus region” for the virtual camera.Blue box shows the focus region around the primary character for reframing.Temporal ReframingOnce we’ve established the focus region, the next challenge is deciding how the crop should move over time. Tracking the focus region on a strict frame-by-frame basis would produce a shaky, distracting viewing experience, so we apply a set of heuristics that consider how the focus region moves over the course of a shot. When its displacement is small, we lock a static crop window that consistently covers the relevant area. When there is significant motion, we compute a dynamic tracking window and smooth its path so that any panning feels intentional and cinematic.The vertical crop (magenta box) follows the character’s movement with a smooth panning path.Tooling and Workflow IntegrationWe integrated the resizing algorithm directly into our internal Clips tool, so editors and producers can access it as part of their existing workflow. The interface enables them to automatically generate multiple aspect‑ratio versions of a clip with smart reframing. A unified in‑app module provides intuitive shot‑reframing controls, allowing users to review the algorithm’s crops, and quickly make targeted adjustments where needed.For more complex shots or custom campaigns, producers can export the resizing parameters as XML and load them directly into external timeline editors such as Adobe Premiere. This allows agencies and external partners to start from the algorithm’s framing decisions instead of recreating crops manually or relying on tools like Premiere’s Auto Reframe. In our internal test, our resizing algorithm outperforms Auto Reframe on a curated evaluation set, so the XML export effectively brings those higher‑quality crops into external workflows. By bringing automated resizing and manual refinement into a single place, this pipeline significantly reduces production time and manual effort while preserving creative control.Video resizing interface with reframing controlsConclusionOur work on multi-ratio video resizing is one step in a broader effort to make content creation more scalable, without compromising creative intent. By pairing algorithm-driven reframing with editor-friendly tools, we’ve reduced the time teams spend on repetitive resizing tasks and unlocked faster experimentation across mobile feeds and marketing campaigns. As we continue to broaden these capabilities to new formats and workflows, our goal remains the same: to give creatives more time to tell great stories, and to deliver a consistently high-quality viewing experience for our members and fans.Multi‑Ratio Video Resizing for Mobile Feed and Marketing was originally published in Netflix TechBlog on Medium, where people are continuing the conversation by highlighting and responding to this story.

#29785. Economic policy vets see risks in AI overreliance

📅 26 Oct 2026 | Category: other | Source

Harvard economist Jason Furman told Axios that if he were to return to his old job as chairman of the White House Council of Economic Advisers today, he would rely on AI tools over staff economists for much of the council's day-to-day analytical work.

The big picture: Unsurprisingly, this made some waves in the online discourse, particularly the loose-knit community formerly known as Econ Twitter.

  • Many veterans of the economic policy trenches see real risks in future policymakers becoming overly reliant on AI — or at a minimum think that Furman is underestimating the importance of human economists in advising the nation's leaders.

What they're saying: "CEA work, at least in my experience, is highly bespoke as you're explaining the data flow (and much else) to very busy people who don't want only market-shop analysis," Jared Bernstein, the CEA chair under President Biden, wrote on Substack.

  • "Yes, they want and need that — what will the markets make of this CPI, jobs, GDP, etc. report — but more so they want to know how this report fits into our narrative, our agenda, the president's trip today to Iowa."
  • He adds that within a White House, "the human skill of reading-the-room is paramount. I'm far from an AI expert, but my sense is it sucks at that and probably always will, a conclusion that derives in no small part from listening to its creators!"

Zoom in: Claudia Sahm, a former Federal Reserve economist, raised an issue that we've often heard from senior people in fields like consulting and investment banking: If you get rid of junior positions, where will future senior leaders come from?

  • Furman's staff "went on to found the Yale Budget Lab, run Treasury economic policy and a Fed institute, serve in later [administrations], and publish research on trade, environment, and taxes. That's what '20 staff' become," she wrote on X. "No model does that."
  • Bloomberg chief U.S. economist Anna Wong argues that the "ability to now not depend on anyone, as a senior person, is very, very empowering. Now, with just the idea, a senior person can go far."

Zoom out: "Entering into sci fi speculation," Wong wrote on X, "as humans [we're] not about to replace the aging experts due to low pipeline of young human experts, AI themselves, through the training over the years, became the one spawning the ideas."

  • "That seems like a disaster waiting to happen that current social policy needs to address."

Yes, but: Casey Mulligan, who served in President Trump's first-term CEA and now as chief economist at the Department of Health and Human Services, said that the implications of AI for human labor in policymaking are not so obvious.

  • "I built AI tools at two federal offices," Mulligan wrote on X. "Able to do economic research better and more quickly, we needed more economists rather than fewer due to the added demand for what we do. This pattern is also evident in the small business sector."
  • This concept — that labor-saving tools can actually increase demand for labor — is known as Jevons Paradox.

The bottom line: "Be the labor-augmenting technological change you want to see in the world," wrote Stanford economist Neale Mahoney.

  • "It's on us olders to build organizations where people and AI complement each other, and to prepare the youngers for a world with better and better AI," he adds.
