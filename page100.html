G Flip is speaking out after Chrishell Stause shared that the pair had gone their separate ways.

“After 4+ years Chrishell and I are no longer together. I will always love her, we will always share a special connection and I cherish our memories together deeply,” G Flip, 33, wrote via Instagram on Monday, October 26.

The “Bed on Fire” singer continued, “My head & heart are feeling very heavy right now and I’m not able to talk but I want to thank you all for your support and appreciate you respecting our privacy at this difficult time.”

Less than two hours earlier, Stause, 45, took to Instagram to share that she and G Flip had separated after four years together. The pair tied the knot in 2023 after meeting two years prior.

“I have a pretty humbling update to share with you guys,” Stause said in an Instagram video on Monday. “G and I are no longer together, and we have agreed to treat this public separation with love and respect for each other. That’s not hard for me because I am in love with this person.”

The former Selling Sunset star went on to note that she doesn’t “have all the information or the reasons” for their split yet.

“I’m sure, just like always, with time, you get the lessons you’re meant to get, and I know that will happen, and I’m gonna heal, and everything’s gonna be fine,” she continued. “I’m definitely never doing this again, but I just felt like you guys deserved a little bit more than a cold written statement because you have been so supportive and so loving, and we really appreciate that.”

From Stause’s perspective, she feels “like a little bit of a failure” after her second marriage has come to an end. (Stause was previously married to Justin Hartley from 2017 until their 2019 separation. The exes finalized their divorce in 2021.)

“There’s nothing you could say about me that would be worse than what I say to myself, so I just have to kind of heal and figure this out,” she said. “It’s hard. It’s painful. I’m a lover girl. I swing big. I miss, or maybe I don’t miss.”

Stause noted that she had an “amazing over four years with G,” adding, “So maybe that’s not a miss, you know? I mean, it’s just coming to terms with it being a different ending than you had imagined. So, with all that being said, I wanted to give you guys a life update. … I don’t know what I hope for myself. I just — it’s really hard and stressful to share this information because I’m kind of going through it.”

The reality TV star concluded her video by noting that she “will always love G” and doesn’t envision that “changing” in the future.

“And this sucks, but everyone deserves to be happy, and I wish that for both of us,” she said. “So thank you.”

#31203. Why Marissa Mayer’s AI parenting anecdote received such a strong reaction on social media

📅 26 Oct 2026 | Category: other | Source

Many people use AI for routine tasks like drafting emails or answering quick questions. But former Yahoo CEO Marissa Mayer used it for something that social media users aren’t necessarily thrilled about.

In a New York Times interview published over the weekend, Mayer revealed that when tasked with coming up with three bullet points to summarize what made her 10-year-old daughter special, she asked AI for help.

“It came back and was, like, your daughter is empathetic, creative, and an animal lover,” Mayer told the news outlet.

Mayer served as Yahoo’s CEO from 2012 to 2017 and was Google’s first female engineer. She now runs an AI startup called Dazzle, which launched in September after raising $8 million in seed funding last year.

“This is so bleak”

To source the bullet points, Mayer used Dazzle AI, an AI personal assistant that relies on one’s camera roll rather than on text.

While Mayer allegedly found the response perfect, users on social media are thinking otherwise, with most pointing out the bizarre or disappointing nature of offloading human emotions and parenting to an agent.

“This is so bleak,” political commentator Keith Edwards wrote on X, in a post with 1.9 million views and hundreds of comments.

Many were quick to speculate about the veracity of the anecdote, hoping it could have been an embellishment or completely made up as a way for Mayer to promote her new product.

“I hope this is an exaggeration to make for an interesting click-baity article,” one user said on X. Another added: “The best-case scenario is that this is a lie made up for the article.”

Others were simply confused at the need to turn to artificial intelligence for a simple task like providing three bullet points about a loved one.

One user wrote: “That’s the type of thing we expect elementary school children to be able to come up with on the fly.”

The idea of outsourcing tender parenting moments to AI has proved controversial for a while now. Two years ago, Google (where Mayer once worked) received backlash over a commercial that depicted a father using the tech to write a fan letter for his daughter. Google ultimately pulled the ad.

The continued discourse around Mayer’s anecdote on social media has since caught the attention of Mayer herself, who published a response to the backlash on X.

“To be clear, I didn’t use Dazzle because I was overwhelmed by making a slide. I used it because summing up your child in a few bullets feels laughably limiting. And, since our team had just gotten an early prototype running, I wanted to see what deeper threads it might find,” she shared on X.

Reached for comment, a Dazzle spokesperson referred Fast Company to Mayer’s post on X.

The post further explained how Mayer used Dazzle, which surfaced “forgotten kindergarten projects,” connected them with “who [her daughter] is today,” and informed the final project.

If nothing else, the anecdote and response have sparked a debate about what we risk losing when we turn over our thinking to machines.

“Bleak doesn’t begin to describe this,” one user wrote on X. “The future of our civilization depends on our continued ability to use our own brains, at least a little bit.”

#36132. Shipping cuts training when it needs it most

📅 26 Oct 2026 | Category: technology | Source

Irene Rosberg, who runs the Blue MBA at Copenhagen Business School, returns to Splash today.

When business conditions become difficult, training is often one of the easiest budgets to cut. In shipping, that is becoming an increasingly dangerous habit.

The industry is asking its people to understand decarbonisation, artificial intelligence, cyber risk, increasingly complex regulation and geopolitical disruption, often all at the same time. Yet education and professional development can still be regarded as discretionary expenditure rather than part of a company’s ability to operate successfully. There is a contradiction here because shipping wants better leaders, more skilled seafarers and managers capable of making increasingly complex decisions, but those capabilities do not simply appear when they are needed.

Today’s maritime leader must be commercially savvy and technologically literate. Executives need sufficient understanding of AI and digitalisation to question the information put in front of them rather than simply accept it. They need to understand cyber risk, regulation and decarbonisation while remaining capable of responding when geopolitical events suddenly alter routes, costs and commercial assumptions. Operational experience remains enormously valuable, but experience alone cannot prepare someone for every challenge now entering the industry.

This is why I find the tendency to reduce training when companies come under financial pressure so short-sighted. Sometimes the lack of the right skills, knowledge or leadership capability may be contributing to the difficulty a business is experiencing in the first place. Cutting the investment that could address those weaknesses can make the problem worse. The same applies at every level of the industry. We rightly talk about training seafarers to handle new fuels, equipment and technology, but professional development cannot stop once somebody reaches shore management or the boardroom. Seniority should not exempt anyone from continuing to learn. If anything, the consequences of outdated knowledge become greater as responsibility increases.

A chief executive does not need to become an AI engineer or a cyber specialist, just as a board member does not need to become an expert in every new fuel. They do, however, need enough understanding to challenge specialists, recognise risk and make informed decisions about issues that may determine the future of the company. That requires investment and shipping has historically placed considerable value on experience, and rightly so. But the speed of change means that what someone knew ten or even five years ago may no longer be sufficient for the decisions they face today. Lifelong learning cannot be a phrase we use in speeches while treating the training budget as expendable when margins tighten.

Companies should also look carefully at what they mean when they say they cannot find the right talent. If the perfect candidate is expected to arrive with operational experience, commercial acumen, digital literacy, regulatory knowledge, leadership capability and an understanding of emerging technologies, perhaps the answer is not simply to keep searching. Perhaps companies need to build more of that capability themselves. That means identifying people with potential, exposing them to different areas of the business and continuing to educate them throughout their careers. It means developing seafarers for shore positions and managers for executive responsibility. It also means recognising that the future leaders of shipping may come from broader professional backgrounds than they did previously.

Education should not simply prepare somebody for their next job. Done properly, it creates organisations that are better equipped to deal with the unexpected. That matters enormously in shipping because unpredictability has become part of everyday management. A geopolitical event can alter trading patterns overnight. A cyber incident can affect operations across a fleet. New regulation can change investment decisions, while AI can alter established ways of working faster than traditional training structures can respond.

No company can predict every disruption. It can, however, decide whether it has people capable of understanding what is happening and responding intelligently. Human capital should therefore not be regarded as a cost to be cut when times are difficult. It is part of the infrastructure of a maritime business, every bit as important to resilience as technology, vessels and financial capacity.

Shipping will spend enormous sums preparing its fleets for the future. It should be equally serious about preparing the people who will be expected to lead them.
