#31206. Microsoft wants thousands of employees to leave, and it’s willing to pay them to do it

📅 26 Oct 2026 | Category: other | Source

Microsoft is giving an enormous swath of its workforce an easy out—if they want it. 

On Monday Amy Coleman, Microsoft’s chief people officer, announced that the company would extend a voluntary exit offer to eligible employees, allowing them to transition out of the company with financial support. ​Coleman framed the buyout offer as a way for Microsoft workers “considering a future outside the company” to make that move now, helpfully reducing the tech giant’s costly head count in the process.

At Microsoft, 60,000 workers are expected to receive the offer—more than 25% of the company’s total workforce. “The Voluntary Exit Offer is new for us,” Coleman wrote in the memo to staff, also shared on the company’s blog. “We’ll learn from it.”

Employees based in the U.S., U.K., Canada, and India on participating teams received details about the buyout on Monday morning via email.

“Participation varies based on each business’s priorities, customer commitments and workforce needs,” Coleman wrote.

Employees who work in Microsoft units including AI experiences, Copilot, human resources, finance, Windows and Devices, and Xbox will be eligible for the buyout, according to a report from Reuters, while workers in security, marketing, and commercial business won’t. Fast Company has reached out to Microsoft for additional details on the buyout offer.

Microsoft is eager to reduce the size of its sprawling global workforce, which totaled 223,000 full-time employees this summer. Like its peers, the tech giant has been on an epic AI spending spree, splashing out big on artificial intelligence chips and data centers. In 2026 alone, Microsoft projects $175 billion in capital expenditures, a mind-boggling sum driven by its huge bets on AI. 

This is the second time the company has offered buyouts this year, after a much smaller round of offers went out to around 9,000 U.S. employees in April. Those buyouts, the first in its 50-plus-year history, were extended to eligible employees at the senior director level or above whose age and years of employment added up to 70 or more. Microsoft laid off around 4,800 workers over the summer, mostly eliminating positions in its commercial sales and Xbox divisions.

“We’ll keep helping people focus on the work that matters most, build new skills and, where it makes sense, find new opportunities across Microsoft,” Coleman wrote. “There is no one-size-fits-all approach for every team or every employee. These are a few of the ways we’re trying to meet different business needs while continuing to create opportunities for our own people to grow and contribute.”

#31207. Why Instagram is about to look a lot more like YouTube

📅 26 Oct 2026 | Category: other | Source

Instagram just announced a new feature that looks a whole lot like YouTube.

In a video posted on Monday, the company announced its latest product, “Features.” According to Instagram head Adam Mosseri and Tessa Lyons, VP of product, Features is Instagram’s first major push into long-form, horizontal video, which will allow creators to post up to 90 minutes of content at a time—quite a jump from the former 20-minute maximum for vertical videos on Instagram Reels. Mosseri expects Features to start rolling out in a limited capacity this month and expand to “more and more people” down the line.

“Instagram is all about connecting people over creativity, and creativity is so much more than just short-form video or posts and carousels,” Lyons says in the announcement. “If we look at how creators are telling stories today, it’s in so many different amazing formats.”

Mosseri, who held an 18-year tenure at Facebook before taking the helm at Instagram in 2018, has long been opposed to adding long-form video to Instagram. Now, though, he and Lyons believe that adding long videos to the app is the best way to meet creators where they are.

What is an Instagram Feature?

According to the announcement video, one of Mosseri’s key stipulations for bringing Features onto the app was ensuring that they work in a “symbiotic” relationship with Instagram’s existing short-form content. That criteria is baked into how the new long-form content will appear to users.

For example, Mosseri explains, one pattern his team noticed was creators using Reels as a place to promote shorter clips of long-form content posted elsewhere. With Features, those creators will be able to post the entire source video, as well as up to eight Reels that link directly to it—essentially creating a natural segue from short-form to long-form content. 

“One of the things that we’re already starting to see as we’re testing these different products is that people might discover a long-form video that they love by seeing a Reel that’s a clip of that video,” Lyons says. 

In an interview with the podcast The Colin and Samir Show, Mosseri explained that users will be able to find Features both by navigating through links on short-form videos and by discovering them on the Explore page. The videos will also be a new core offering on Instagram for TV, which was launched last year as a way for users to watch Reels on the big screen with their friends. 

Mosseri and Lyons are imagining this new offering as a way to make Instagram the go-to platform for users regardless of the occasion: Reels and posts are the “snackable” content that work as a quick bite, and, at home, Features are a full course that can be viewed on mobile or on TV.

Why Instagram is going to look more like YouTube

When users see Features show up on their feeds, their first thought will likely be that it looks like a YouTube-Instagram hybrid—probably because YouTube holds a near-monopoly on long-form content on the internet. 

In a July interview with Fast Company, YouTube shared that it already hosts more than 20 billion total videos and, shockingly, “billions” of logged-in monthly users. In 2025, YouTube’s parent company, Alphabet, reported that the platform had generated more than $60 billion in revenue across advertising and subscriptions—a sum that outpaced Netflix’s revenue by almost $15 billion. From April to June 2026 alone, YouTube ads netted Alphabet more than $11 billion, up 13% from the same period in 2025. 

Clearly, long-form YouTube content is a gold mine for advertisers—and it’s no surprise that Instagram might want its own piece of the pie. 

“Creators want to tell their stories where their communities already are,” an Instagram spokesperson wrote in an email to Fast Company. “People are also connecting over entertainment in new ways: sharing what they watch, watching together on the big screen, and creating in response to what inspires them. We’re bringing those experiences together.”
