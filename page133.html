#36129. World’s Largest Battery-Electric Ship Begins Commercial Service in South America

📅 26 Oct 2026 | Category: technology | Source

Essential news coupled with the finest maritime content sourced from across the globe.

Incat Tasmania-built China Zorrilla, the world’s largest battery-electric ship, makes its maiden commercial crossing between Buenos Aires, Argentina, and Colonia, Uruguay. Photo: Incat Tasmania

The world’s largest battery-electric ship has officially entered commercial service, marking a major milestone for the maritime industry and the growing use of electric propulsion in large passenger vessels.

The 130-meter China Zorrilla, built by Australian shipbuilder Incat Tasmania for ferry operator Buquebus, completed its first commercial crossing between Buenos Aires, Argentina, and Colonia, Uruguay, on October 22.

The high-speed catamaran can carry up to 2,100 passengers and 225 vehicles across the River Plate, operating entirely on battery power without the need for conventional marine engines.

At the heart of the vessel is an energy storage system exceeding 40 megawatt-hours, made up of more than 250 tonnes of batteries. The system powers eight electrically driven waterjets, making China Zorrilla the largest battery-electric vessel ever constructed.

The ship is supported by dedicated high-power charging infrastructure at terminals on both sides of the river, allowing its batteries to be recharged between crossings.

The start of commercial service follows an official ceremony in Buenos Aires on October 21, attended by Incat Managing Director Craig Clifford, Buquebus representatives, and maritime authorities and government officials from Argentina and Uruguay.

Incat Chairman Robert Clifford described the achievement as one of the proudest moments in the company’s history, and credited hundreds of workers at Incat’s Hobart shipyard with overcoming significant engineering challenges to deliver the vessel, which represents one of the most ambitious projects in the company’s history.

“This ship proves that large, high-capacity commercial vessels can operate on electric power,” Clifford said.

Beyond its size and technical capabilities, China Zorrilla also offers a different passenger experience. Without conventional diesel engines, the vessel operates with significantly less machinery noise and vibration, while producing no direct exhaust emissions during crossings.

Craig Clifford, who traveled aboard the vessel during its maiden commercial voyage, said the difference was immediately noticeable.

He noted that passengers aboard a vessel of this size would normally expect to hear and feel powerful engines operating beneath them, but that familiar noise and vibration are largely absent.

The ferry also features extensive passenger amenities, including approximately 2,300 square meters of duty-free retail space.

The project’s completion is particularly significant given its evolution during construction. Originally designed to use dual-fuel engines capable of burning diesel and liquefied natural gas, the vessel was ultimately redesigned for fully electric propulsion, requiring substantial engineering changes.

Its entry into service demonstrates the growing potential for battery-electric technology in high-capacity ferry operations, particularly on fixed routes where shore-based charging infrastructure can support regular crossings.

For Incat, the milestone is also part of a broader shift toward electric vessel construction.

The Tasmanian shipbuilder is currently building three 129-meter battery-electric ferries for Danish operator Molslinjen, along with two 78-meter hybrid-electric vessels at its Hobart facility.

With China Zorrilla now carrying paying passengers, the company has moved one of the maritime industry’s most ambitious electric vessel projects from the shipyard into everyday commercial operation.

#29786. Anthropic's IPO outlines AI's extraordinary risks

📅 26 Oct 2026 | Category: other | Source

Anthropic's IPO filing includes an extensive list of extreme warnings — including the chance its own technology could put humanity at risk.

Why it matters: These are the wildest IPO risk factors in history, a jarring collision of Wall Street legalese and what some see as Silicon Valley sci-fi, including catastrophic threats to humanity, blackmailing AI models, and $750 billion in computing commitments.

Driving the news: Anthropic's prospectus includes over 90 pages of risk factors, which make up about a third of the paperwork overall. Among the most eye-popping, the company warns:

  • 💥 AI could pose "catastrophic or existential risks to humanity," and could try to manipulate humans;
  • 🔎 Models are becoming harder to monitor as they're getting better at circumventing oversight, and the pace of autonomous work alone could exceed the ability of human reviewers to monitor actions;
  • 💰 AI models generally, including its own, have exhibited behavior resembling blackmail, including attempts to resist being shut down.

Zoom in: Anthropic also disclosed that four of its models gained unauthorized access to real third-party systems during cybersecurity evaluations.

  • In one incident, a model used a discovered password to gain administrator access, collected additional credentials, changed system settings and accessed an individual's personal information.
  • Anthropic said it found no evidence that the models pursued goals beyond their assigned tasks or attempted to evade oversight.

The big picture: Every company has to lay out risks for their investors to consider when they go public.

  • But Anthropic is the first in modern history to go to such existential extremes.

Friction point: The company also acknowledged that its success depends in large part on its ability to develop the best frontier intelligence.

  • It admits that could butt up against safety practices, adding that model capabilities may then "advance more rapidly than our ability to develop, validate, and implement" corresponding safety measures.
  • That underscores a fundamental tension critics have warned about: As Anthropic scales and faces pressure from public market investors to build better models than its competitors, its commercial ambitions could increasingly clash with its safety commitments.

By the numbers: The risk factors also include the more typical financial cautions.

  • Anthropic has committed hundreds of billions of dollars to cloud computing and equipment leases, even as customers can reduce their usage relatively quickly.
  • Some agreements require minimum payments regardless of demand, and the company warns that failing to meet certain obligations could trigger defaults elsewhere, potentially accelerating debt repayments and jeopardizing access to computing infrastructure.
  • Anthropic also acknowledges that it previously underestimated its compute needs. But overestimating future demand could leave it paying for expensive, underutilized infrastructure.

The bottom line: Anthropic's IPO paperwork is an unusually candid warning about the risks of building a business around technology that its creator acknowledges may become increasingly difficult to control.

#36002. Racecourse fined after jockey taken to A&E in horsebox

📅 26 Oct 2026 | Category: technology | Source

An inspection in April revealed 'significant progress' at the course

A racecourse has been fined Â£6,500 after a jockey was taken to hospital in a horsebox instead of an ambulance.

The incident took place in March at Sedgefield Racecourse in County Durham.

A disciplinary panel of the British Horseracing Authority (BHA) found that the racecourse had failed to comply with five "general instructions" within its Rules of Racing.

The injured jockey's identity was kept private but it was reported that he suffered a left clavicle and shoulder injury after a fall, and was examined by a doctor at the course.

He was cleared to race again but said he was still in pain, which resulted in a doctor advising him to go to A&E.

By then all the ambulance crews had left the venue, and he was taken in a horsebox with the medical staff following behind.

"The 'general instructions' require proper transfer to hospital with accompaniment from qualified medical staff not, as here, being followed in a car," the BHA panel said.

It also added that the jockey was in too much pain to get into the following car.

The other failings at the venue included a telephone line not working and "tired worn rooms not conveying a clean clinical area".

The racecourse has acknowledged the "errors and shortcomings" and an inspection took place in April which revealed "significant progress".
