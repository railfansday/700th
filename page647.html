Following reported strikes on Yandex data centers, claims circulated on the Internet that Russian bot farms and influence operations had been knocked offline. HTTP traffic charts showed steep drops, fueling speculation that the infrastructure damage had stopped these malicious actors. However, aggregate traffic metrics can easily mislead. #35811. Integrating Netflix’s Foundation Model into Personalization applications

📅 14 Oct 2026 | Category: technology | Source

Authors: Divya Gadde, Ko-Jen Hsiao, Dhaval Patel and Moumita BhattacharyaThe motivation for building the Netflix Foundation Model for personalization was laid out in this previous blog post. The Netflix homepage is powered by several specialized models and it requires significant time and resources to maintain and continue innovating on these individual models. Therefore, we decided to centralize member preference learning and have one powerful model learned from comprehensive user interaction histories and content data at a large scale and have this model distribute its learnings to other models.The previous two blog posts(1, 2) focus on the development of the Foundation Model and here we talk about how we integrate the Foundation Model into various applications. We found that there is extensive literature focused on the training and inference of large-scale transformer-based models. However, there remains a significant gap in practical guidance and research on how to effectively and efficiently integrate large models into existing production systems. At Netflix, we experimented with 3 distinct integration approaches — embeddings, subgraph and fine-tuning. The motivation for trying different approaches stems from varying application needs. Specifically, different teams have different latency requirements, tech stack constraints and different appetites for leveraging the full power of a large foundation model.In this blog, we go over the 3 approaches we experimented with and which are now used in production for different use cases. Each integration approach has its own set of motivations, tradeoffs, complexities and learnings. The following sections go into the details of each of these integration approaches.Embeddings from Foundation modelTransformer style foundation models naturally lend themselves to generating comprehensive user profile and item representations. Our approach for generating embeddings includes getting the hidden state of the last user event as the profile embedding and weights from the item tower as item embeddings.Netflix has an elaborate set up for refreshing the embeddings every day. First, the Foundation Model is pre-trained from scratch every month. Then, the monthly pre-trained model is fine-tuned everyday based on the latest data. The daily fine-tuning also expands the entity id space to include the newly launching titles. After the daily fine-tuned model is ready, we run batch inference to refresh the profile and item embeddings and publish them to the Embedding Store.An important part of this setup is the embedding stabilization technique (paper). When the pretraining job retrains the model from scratch, the embedding spaces between different runs are completely different due to the random initialization in the model. In addition, the embeddings start to drift when the daily job fine-tunes on the latest data, despite the daily fine-tuning job warm-starting from the previous day’s model. Therefore, it is important to map the embeddings generated everyday into the same embedding space, so that downstream models can consume pre-computed embeddings as features.This embedding approach relies heavily on the Embedding Store, which is a feature store specializing in embeddings built by the platform team at Netflix. The embedding store makes producing and consuming embeddings easy by taking care of versioning and timestamping the embeddings. The Embedding Store also provides various interfaces to access the embeddings offline and online.Embedding Store integrationThis approach is often the initial and most straightforward way for applications to interact with the Foundation Model. Embeddings can serve as powerful features for other models or for candidate generation, helping to retrieve appealing titles for a user or facilitate title-to-title recommendations. Integrating embeddings as features into existing pipelines is generally well-supported and offers a low barrier to entry. Compared to other integration approaches, using embeddings from the Embedding Store is expected to have a relatively smaller impact on training and inference costs.However, this approach also has some cons. The time gap between embedding computation and downstream model inference can lead to staleness, impacting the freshness of recommendations. This may prevent applications from fully unlocking the benefit of the Foundation Model, especially for use cases requiring real-time adaptability.Overall, the default starting point for leveraging the Foundation Model is through its profile and item embeddings via the Embedding Store, provided that the embeddings’ freshness and stability meet application-specific needs. While embeddings may not leverage the full power of the Foundation Model, they offer a good initial step.Our biggest learning from working on this approach is that embeddings are a low cost and high leverage way of using the Foundation Model. A resilient embedding generation framework and embedding store are essential for updating the embeddings daily and serving them to application models. We found this infrastructure to be so valuable that we stepped up our investment here to build a near-realtime embedding generation framework. This new framework also enables us to update the embeddings based on user actions during the session, making the embeddings and our downstream models more adaptive. Although the near-real time framework cannot be used for very large models, it represents an important direction for addressing embedding staleness and adaptiveness of recommendations.Foundation Model as a SubgraphThis approach involves using the Foundation Model as an input to the downstream model i.e. the Foundation Model decoder stack is a subgraph of the application model’s full model graph and hence the name “subgraph integration”. The Foundation Model subgraph processes raw user interaction sequences and outputs representations that are then fed into the downstream model graph.Foundation Model Subgraph integrationApplications can fine-tune the Foundation Model subgraph as part of their own training process, potentially leading to better performance than just using static embeddings. In this approach, there is no time gap or staleness between the Foundation Model inference and the application model’s inference, ensuring that the most up-to-date learnings are used. This approach also allows applications to leverage specific layers from the Foundation Model that may not be exposed through the Embedding Store, uncovering more application-specific value.However, the subgraph approach comes with some complexities and trade-offs. To use the Foundation Model as a subgraph, application models need to generate all the features necessary for the subgraph as part of their feature generation process. This can add time, compute and complexity to their feature gen jobs. Merging the Foundation Model as a subgraph also increases the application model size and inference time. To alleviate some of these complexities, the Foundation Model team provides some reusable code and jobs that make the feature generation more compute efficient. To optimize the inference performance, we split the subgraph and ensure it only runs once per profile per request and is shared by all items in the request.Overall, the subgraph approach allows for a deeper integration and enables the applications to harness the full power of the Foundation Model. But it can also make the application’s training code more complex, requiring a trade-off between metric improvement, compute cost, and time. Therefore, we would reserve this approach for high impact use cases where the increase in metric wins compensate for the cost and complexity.Fine-tuning Foundation Model and use directlyThis approach is similar to fine-tuning LLMs with domain specific data. In the previous blog post, we described how the Foundation Model is trained on a next-token prediction objective, with the tokens being different user interactions. Different interactions are of different importance to various surfaces on the Netflix website. For example, for the ‘Trending now’ row, recent interactions on trending titles might be more important than old interactions. Hence, the Foundation Model can be fine-tuned on more product specific data and then the fine-tuned model can be used to directly power the product. During fine-tuning, application teams can choose to do full parameter fine-tuning or freeze some layers. They can also choose to add a different output head with a different objective. We built a fine-tuning framework to make it easy for application teams to develop their own custom fine-tuned versions of the Foundation Model.Fine-tuned Foundation Model IntegrationThis approach offers the ability to adapt the Foundation Model to application’s specific data and objectives, making it optimized for their use case. A side benefit of this integration approach is that it provides a de facto baseline for new models/applications. Instead of coming up with a new model stack and spending months on feature engineering, new applications can directly utilize fine-tuned foundation models. However, this approach can lead to more models and pipelines to maintain across the organization.The latency and Service Level Agreements (SLAs) of the fine-tuned model must be carefully optimized for the specific application use case.ConclusionWe are continuing to innovate on these integration approaches over time. For example, the Machine Learning Platform team is working on near-real time embedding inference capabilities to address the staleness issues with the embedding approach. We are also working on a smaller distilled version of the Foundation Model to reduce the inference latency of the subgraph approach. We also provide a fine-tuning framework to make it easy to fine-tune and leverage the Foundation Model. We also continue to refine and standardize the APIs used in these approaches to make it easy for application teams to use them. Overall, these three integration patterns provide a comprehensive framework for how Netflix applications can interact with and extract value from the Foundation Model.AcknowledgementsWe thank our stunning colleagues in the Foundation Model team for developing and continuing to innovate on the Foundation Model. We thank our colleagues in the AIMS applications teams for driving the integrations and their valuable feedback and discussions. We also thank our Machine Learning Platform team for building and supporting the infra components to get these approaches up and running in production.Contributors to this work (name in alphabetical order): Davis Shepherd Inbar Naor Jin Wang Jonathan Indig Kevin Zielnicki Luke Wang Mario Garcia-Armas Maryam Esmaeli Michael Tu Nicholas Hortiguera Qiuling Xu Robbie Greuner Roger Menezes Sejoon Oh Trung Nguyen Vito Ostuni Yesu Feng.Integrating Netflix’s Foundation Model into Personalization applications was originally published in Netflix TechBlog on Medium, where people are continuing the conversation by highlighting and responding to this story.

#35813. DataJunction as Netflix’s answer to the missing piece of the modern data stack

📅 14 Oct 2026 | Category: technology | Source

by Olek Gorajek, Samuel Redai, Yian ShangIntroOur Netflix journey from data to dashboards, particularly within the space of the experimentation platform, always presented a unique set of challenges. This blog post aims to illuminate these hurdles and explain how an innovative open-source metric platform became instrumental in overcoming some of them. We’ll showcase the critical role that DataJunction played in streamlining our data workflows and enhancing our ability to derive meaningful insights from our vast datasets.Metrics are the foundation of data-driven decision-making, but at many organizations, they are scattered, inconsistently defined, and difficult to discover or trust. At Netflix, this challenge first became apparent in our experimentation platform, where reliable metrics are essential for interpreting test results. But the complexity of the original tooling made it very difficult to understand and introspect these metrics both in terms of their definitions as well as their values.Initial ProblemScattered metric definitions, redundant datasets, and pipelines are often a natural effect of a fast-moving company with a large and powerful analytical workforce. This is a common problem in big data-driven firms, and it is not easy to solve across multiple departments, which are often governed by somewhat different rules and regulations. An easy-to-use, well-governed, and centralized semantic layer can help with these issues. Our initial problem was most evident in our Experimentation Platform team, where the previous system required:extensive business knowledgefamiliarity with experimentation conventionsand complex engineering practicesThis led to metric authoring processes that would take weeks, which was a prohibitive requirement for Data Scientists and Analytics Engineers who wanted to onboard new metrics to the Experimentation Platform.Enter DataJunctionTo address this problem and to set ourselves on the path to a successful semantic layer solution in the future, we looked at several candidates. Among other solutions we evaluated Mando (an internal framework), Minerva (an open source semantic layer from AirBnB), and DataJunction (another open source solution, “DJ” for short). All of them had some strength, but DJ offered one thing that the others did not at the time: a solid SQL parsing and SQL generation engine. At the time of the POC, DJ was also the only open-source solution that stored all its definitions in a connected graph, similar to how relational databases store metadata on their views and queries. Easy “SQL wrangling” with rich metadata was the main feature that convinced us to try it for our immediate use case.DataJunction originated from a similar internal Facebook project created (around 2015) by the data science and infrastructure (DSI) team, mainly as an answer to the need for metrics portability. During several iterations of the Deltoid project — which was the main experimentation platform at Facebook at the time — users were required to rebuild and repopulate their metrics when they wanted to move to the next version of Deltoid. But after implementing DJ and abstracting all metric definitions the team offered seamless migrations by separating the concerns between the metrics and the stats computation platform. That project was subsequently renamed to MDF (Metrics and Dimensions Factory) and promoted as a generic semantic layer used widely by various departments. In 2021, one of the Facebook engineers (Beto Dealmeida), started the DJ Github project and built out some basic functionality. Then together with some folks at Netflix expanded the scope further, and soon after that the project was introduced at Netflix. Following that introduction, three Netflix engineers (authors of this blog) together with a couple of external contributors, most notably Nick Ouellet, have been building out the remaining features of the platform.Architecture DecisionsA critical design constraint for building DJ at Netflix was resource scarcity. We refused to compromise on product quality, choosing instead to limit the feature set. Although DJ was clearly capable of addressing many simultaneous problems across diverse use cases, our lack of adequate resources and official support meant we developed it as a side project for over two years. Our hope was that demonstrating its value would eventually secure management attention.API First, UI and Other Interfaces LaterA metrics platform doesn’t need a flashy front-end to deliver value — its core is the semantic layer, which stores a consistent set of dimensions and metrics and serves this information through a rich API service. You can see below how we structured the main components of DataJunction in the Netflix implementation. Our DJ services (in the middle) interact with the underlying data (on the left) and supply our access layer with the business definitions in the form of SQL w/ metadata or directly with data depending on the client request.Consistent Graph First, Other Features LaterA complete semantic layer system can seem like an octopus, with its tentacles reaching out into many functional areas and can expand from a basic semantic metadata stored for some datasets to full dashboard functionality. Let’s call out some of them:semantic metadata authoring and management and monitoringmetric and dimension query / definition generationmetric and dimension data generation (requires a query engine)data visualization and data trackingperformance optimization (aka caching or materialization)integration layer with other tools andany / all the above functionality served as: UI, API, client libraries, MCP servers and more.Of course it is extremely hard to provide the best in class functionality across all these layers, so with our small “unofficial” team we decided to focus on the most important piece: solid semantic layer engine (and API) with the ability to parse SQL as the input to the graph and generate SQL as the serving layer. That is the core of the system and everything else can be added later with less effort and w/o the need to rebuild the core.Cube as The Perfect Consumption ProtocolAnalytical multi-dimensional cubes have been a fundamental concept for decades, with their representation integrated across various data processing and analysis platforms. While definitions and features may vary between tools, they all serve the common purpose of encapsulating, or abstracting, a subset of semantic objects.In DataJunction, we adopted cubes as first-class features for two main reasons: their familiarity to users and their suitability as a serving interface. They efficiently deliver both the semantic definitions (of metrics and dimensions) and fast data access. See an example of data “flow” graph (dotted lines) and dimensional relationships graph (solid lines) below.We simplified the creation of basic cubes, allowing them to easily aggregate arbitrary collections of shared metrics and dimensions. Furthermore, accessing, managing, and visualizing these cubes is straightforward for any client integrating with DJ. Custom integrations, such as for Netflix’s internal fast Druid data analysis tool (Data Explorer → see below), became trivial to build, and we also made it easy to materialize these cubes directly in our Druid clusters.One of DJ’s most powerful client integrations is the ability to link any DJ-originating metric definition back to the DJ ecosystem. This creates a valuable feedback loop: troubleshooting and introspecting business dashboards becomes simple if they display DJ-defined metrics that include links back to the definition source. This link then makes inspecting and running ad-hoc analysis, by modifying existing metrics, a trivial process. This integration is not only possible with our inhouse tools, but also with other well known open-source products, like Superset.Experimentation at NetflixMetrics are the backbone of experimentation. They translate data into signals that tell us whether a change is meaningful, safe, and worth scaling. Without trustworthy metrics and dimensions, experiments risk becoming inconclusive or misleading, so a robust metrics layer becomes crucial for data-driven decisions.At Netflix, the original metrics layer integrated into the experimentation platform was overly complicated. Creating new metrics not only required tribal knowledge of experimentation data artifacts, but also familiarity with complex engineering workflows. This unnecessary friction meant that onboarding a single new metric could take weeks, slowing adoption of new metrics and hindering experimentation.This is where DataJunction comes in: it provides a central hub for defining metrics and dimensions that can easily be shared and explored via the semantic graph. It also provides flexible, user-friendly interfaces (including a UI, API, and various clients), making it easy for users to author and manage their metrics and other semantic models.ConclusionsWith experimentation domain well supported by DataJunction, we would like to take on next steps to unifying of the general analytics at Netflix and to make it easier for Netflix users to both:find and use metric definitions across verticals (defined in DJ)easily access, modify and experiment with new metrics across Netflix data stackThat will require building out strong integrations between DJ and other tools, but that has been underway and it is one of the most enjoyable parts of this project, where most of the feature exists and we are simply enabling existing systems to exchange information in a structured and reliable way. With a system like DataJunction our internal LLMs can deliver accountable information about the business metrics as well as explanation of how they are defined and used across Netflix.Related resources:2023 : DataJunction at Netflix (slide deck)2025 : DataJunction talk at Data Engineering Open Forum 2025 (video)2025 : DataJunction: Unifying Experimentation and Analytics (blog)DataJunction as Netflix’s answer to the missing piece of the modern data stack was originally published in Netflix TechBlog on Medium, where people are continuing the conversation by highlighting and responding to this story.

#35818. Measuring the Impact of Personalized Recommendations

📅 14 Oct 2026 | Category: technology | Source

By Kevin Zielnicki, Guy Aridor, Aurélien Bibaut, Allen Tran, Winston Chou, and Nathan KallusIntroductionPersonalization is everywhere on the internet. It shapes the products we discover, the stories we read, the music we hear, and the movies and TV shows we watch. At Netflix, personalization has been central to the member experience for decades — from the Netflix Prize to our modern recommender system that helps members navigate a large and constantly changing catalog.But personalization is not only an algorithmic problem, it is also a measurement problem. How do we know how much value personalization creates? More precisely, how do we separate the appeal of a title from the value created by recommending that title to the right member at the right time?In a new article in the International Journal of Industrial Organization — which we are also presenting at this year’s ACM Conference on Economics & Computation (EC ‘26) — we develop a model of member behavior, title choice, and personalized recommendations. The model lets us estimate how recommendations affect both whether members choose to watch and, if so, which titles they choose. In this post, we summarize our approach and main findings.The Measurement ChallengeA natural first attempt at valuing recommendations is to ask: what share of viewing comes from titles that were recommended? This is an intuitive metric, but it fails to answer the causal question at hand.The problem is that recommendations are not randomly assigned. A title appears on a member’s homepage precisely because the recommender system predicts that the member might want to watch it. If a member watches that title, was it because the recommendation caused the member to watch it? Or would she have watched it anyway because of her underlying preference for that kind of title? This is the core measurement challenge: recommendations are targeted to demand, so viewing a recommended title combines the effect of personalization with the member’s underlying preferences.One way to solve this would be to run a large experiment that replaces Netflix’s personalized experience with an unpersonalized one. That would create clean variation — but it would also be a poor member experience. Personalization is a central part of how members locate valuable titles in a large catalog, so turning it off broadly is not an acceptable measurement strategy.Instead, we take a hybrid approach. We build a behavioral model that separates members’ underlying preferences from the incremental effect of recommendations. The model uses the natural variation in the recommendation system, for example due to algorithmic exploration, to estimate how recommendations affect choice. Then, to validate the model predictions about title substitutability, we use small-scale A/B tests that “nudge” the salience of a handful of titles exogenously, generating ground-truth substitution patterns for a subset of titles. This lets us combine the scale and richness of modeling with the credibility of experimental evidence.A Model of Member ChoiceTo measure the value of personalization, we need a model of what members would watch under different recommendation systems. Our model treats Netflix viewing as a day-by-day choice process. On each day, a member chooses among the titles available in the catalog, or she chooses not to watch anything. This gives us a concrete target to estimate: the probability that a member watches each title on a given day, as a function of their preferences, their viewing history, and the recommendations they see.A simple version of this model would be too restrictive. It might assume, for example, that removing one title from the catalog shifts viewing toward all other titles in roughly proportional ways. But that isn’t how member choice works. If a member is considering a romantic comedy, the closest substitutes are likely other romantic comedies, not every title on Netflix equally. For similar reasons, two members may react very differently to the same recommendation.To capture this complexity, we use a low-rank choice model. The model learns a representation of each title and a corresponding representation of each member’s current preferences. These representations are not limited to manually specified features like genre, cast, or language. Instead, they are learned from viewing behavior itself, allowing the model to capture nuanced similarities between titles that may be difficult to describe with coarse features, such as genre or release date, alone.Our Model Attributes Choices to Dynamic Member-Title Affinity and the Effect of RecommendationsMember preferences are dynamic. What someone wants to watch today depends on what they have watched recently. A member who just finished a crime thriller may be in a different preference state than the same member after watching a stand-up special or a kids’ movie. Rather than estimate a fixed preference vector for each member, the model summarizes each member’s past viewing history into a current preference state. This lets us capture heterogeneous and time-varying tastes while also scaling to millions of members.Our Model Architecture Allows Us to Scale to Millions of Members EfficientlyThe key component for our purposes is that the model explicitly parameterizes the effect of recommendations. When a title is recommended, the model gives it an additional recommendation effect, or “bonus,” that increases its probability of being chosen. This bonus is a reduced-form way to capture two related mechanisms. First, recommendations can create awareness by helping members discover titles they may not have known about. Second, recommendations can provide information by signaling that a title is likely to be a good match.Once estimated, the model becomes an engine for predicting counterfactuals. We can ask what would happen if a title were not recommended, if a different set of titles were recommended, or if the current recommender system were replaced with simpler alternatives. Because the model separately represents member preferences, title appeal, and recommendation effects, it lets us estimate not only how recommendations affect total viewing, but also how they change which titles are watched.The model is only useful, however, if it captures real substitution patterns. The next step is therefore validation: comparing the model’s predictions to randomized experiments that directly perturb, on a small scale, the visibility that each title receives.Validating the Model with Randomized ExperimentsThe model relies on an important assumption. After conditioning on the member, title, and state information available to the recommender system, we treat the remaining variation in whether a title is recommended as if it were randomly assigned. In our setting, this assumption is reasonable: we observe all of the information available to the recommender system, so we should be able to model its behavior accurately. But it is still an assumption. A useful model should not only fit historical viewing patterns; it should also predict what happens when recommendations change for external reasons.Thus, to validate the model, we used randomized experiments that introduce variation in title visibility exogenously. These experiments mirror the kinds of variation that occur routinely when recommendation algorithms are updated: some titles receive more impressions, while others receive fewer. The key difference is that, in the validation experiment, this variation was prespecified so that we could estimate clean experimental benchmarks.Specifically, we randomly assigned members to treatment arms that slightly nudged recommendations towards and away from different categories of titles. This let us measure diversion ratios: when a set of titles becomes less visible, how much viewing shifts to other titles, and how much shifts out of Netflix viewing altogether?We then asked whether the model could reproduce those experimentally measured substitution patterns. We estimated the model on the control group, simulated the same recommendation changes, and compared the model-implied diversion ratios to the experimental ones. The agreement was strong: the model’s predicted diversion ratios had a correlation of 0.86 with the experimental estimates and an R-squared of 0.73.Our Model Predicts Out-of-Sample Experimental Diversion Ratios WellThis validation gives us confidence that the model captures counterfactual substitution patterns accurately. Subsequently, we ask what would happen under more dramatic changes, such as different recommender systems.Counterfactuals: Aggregate Consumption and DiversityWith a validated model in hand, we can move from measurement to counterfactuals. Instead of asking only what happened under the current Netflix experience, we can ask what would have happened if members had seen recommendations generated by a different system.We compare the current recommender system to three baselines:The first is random recommendations: every member receives titles selected uniformly at random from the catalog. This is not intended as a realistic product experience, but it helps isolate the value of having recommendations that are relevant.The second is popularity-based recommendations: every member receives broadly popular titles. This is a natural non-personalized benchmark.The third is a basic matrix factorization model, a classic personalized recommendation approach and a useful baseline for older generations of recommender systems.In each counterfactual, we replace the recommended titles while keeping the rest of the choice environment fixed. This lets us estimate how much viewing would change if the recommendation policy changed, holding constant the catalog, members, and model of member choice.The results show that the current recommender system generates substantially more engagement than each benchmark. Relative to the current system, random recommendations reduce engagement by 16%, popularity-based recommendations reduce engagement by 12%, and matrix factorization reduces engagement by 4%. These are large differences at Netflix scale. Even the comparison to matrix factorization — a personalized baseline — suggests that continued improvements in personalization translate into meaningful gains in how effectively members find titles they want to watch.Personalized Recommendations Increase Engagement and its DiversityEngagement is only part of the story. A recommender system could increase viewing while concentrating attention on a narrow set of already-popular titles. That would be a worse experience for members whose tastes are not well represented by those titles, and it would make poorer use of a broad catalog.We therefore also examine consumption diversity: how broadly viewing is distributed across titles. The current system performs well on this dimension too. Popularity-based and matrix-factorization recommendations both substantially increase concentration, meaning that fewer titles receive meaningful viewership. Random recommendations produce slightly more diversity, as expected, but at a large cost to engagement.Taken together, these counterfactuals show that the value of personalization is not just that members watch more. It is also that members are matched to a broader set of titles. The current recommender system increases engagement while preserving a wide distribution of viewing, reflecting the diversity of member tastes across the Netflix catalog.Decomposing the Impact on TitlesThe aggregate counterfactuals show that personalization increases engagement while maintaining broad consumption across the catalog. We can also use the model to ask a more granular question: why does a recommended title get watched?There are three conceptually distinct channels:The first is exposure: simply showing a title to a member can increase the probability that the member watches it. This captures the basic value of surfacing a title that may otherwise have been hard to find.The second is selection: recommended titles are shown to members who already have a relatively high baseline probability of watching them. This channel doesn’t lead to incremental engagement, but it can smooth the discovery experience and help users feel that the recommender system understands their preferences.The third is targeting: the recommender system may identify members for whom the recommendation itself is especially effective, meaning members who are much more likely to watch the title when it is recommended than when it is not.The distinction matters because not all recommendation-driven viewing should be interpreted the same way. If recommendations worked only through exposure, then any reasonably visible surface could generate much of the same value. If they worked mostly through selection, then recommendations would mainly be finding members who were already likely to watch a title. Targeting is different: it captures whether the system is placing titles in front of members for whom the recommendation changes the decision.Decomposing the Impact of Recommendations into Selection, Exposure, and TargetingOur model lets us estimate these channels title by title. For each title, we can compare the predicted probability that a targeted member watches it when recommended to the probability that an average member watches it when not recommended. We then decompose that difference into selection, exposure, and targeting.We first show that exposure is meaningful: showing a title to an average member roughly triples its consumption probability relative to the baseline. Exposure is not the main driver of differences in consumption, as mechanical exposure explains only about 7% of the gap under the current recommender system, while selection and targeting account for the vast majority of the difference and the targeting effect is nearly seven times larger than the exposure effect.This pattern is especially informative when compared with simpler recommendation systems. Random recommendations are dominated by exposure because they do not personalize: differences in consumption mostly reflect differences in what titles are shown. Matrix factorization changes this by making recommendations personal, reducing the role of exposure and leaving exposure, selection, and targeting to contribute nearly evenly to consumption differences. The current recommender system goes further: its gains come less from mechanical exposure, but more from targeting the right titles to the right viewers.Targeting is also not uniform across the catalog. It is strongest for mid-popularity titles: titles with enough potential audience to be broadly relevant, but not so much mass appeal that everyone already knows about them. Very popular titles need less help from targeting, while very niche titles may have too small an audience to target effectively. The largest gains come from the middle of the catalog, where personalization helps connect specific titles with specific member tastes.Personalized Recommendations Give the Strongest Boost to Medium-Popularity TitlesConclusionPersonalized recommendations are valuable because they help members navigate a large catalog and find titles they want to watch. They are also valuable to Netflix because they make the service more useful, more engaging, and better able to serve a wide range of tastes.But measuring that value is not straightforward. Recommendations are targeted by design, so we cannot simply compare recommended and non-recommended viewing. At the same time, turning off personalization at scale would create a worse member experience.Our approach combines a large-scale model of member choice with true experimental validation. The model lets us separate underlying title preferences from the incremental effect of recommendations, and the experiments verify that the model captures real substitution patterns.Using this framework, we find that personalization affects both how much members watch and which titles they watch. The value of recommendations comes not just from exposure, but from matching the right titles to the right members.Measuring the Impact of Personalized Recommendations was originally published in Netflix TechBlog on Medium, where people are continuing the conversation by highlighting and responding to this story.

By Yogesh NagarurIntroductionIn Part 1 of this series, we shared how Netflix built the Ads Event pipeline and approached it as a system design problem: replacing fragmented pipelines with a centralized collection, enrichment, and a standard data contract. The result was Ads Event Publisher, the system that collects ad telemetry from client devices, enriches it with the context of the ad that was actually served, and publishes a single, unified stream of ad events to every downstream consumer: frequency capping, metrics, billing, measurement partners, and reporting. This post is about what was inside that box, and why we rebuilt it.Part 1 ended with a look at what was on the horizon: ads on live streams, de-duplication, and richer data signals. A single project delivered all three by rebuilding the Ads Event Publisher around stateful stream processing. We replaced a database lookup with a real-time streaming join in Apache Flink, which let the system scale for Live, took a dependency off the critical path, and replaced the engine of a high-traffic pipeline without downtime.Let’s dive in.A quick recap: enrichment and the Metadata RegistryWhen an ad is served, the ad server knows everything about it: the campaign, the creative, the price of the impression, the targeting context, and the tracking URLs that measurement partners expect us to fire. The client device, by contrast, knows almost nothing: just an opaque token and when to send each event. Enrichment is the act of reuniting the two.In the architecture from Part 1, that reunion happened through the Ads Metadata Registry, a Key-Value store:When the ad server made a decision, it wrote the metadata for that impression into the registry.It encoded only reference identifiers into the opaque token returned to the device, keeping the token small.Later, when the client fired an event carrying that token, the Ads Event Publisher decoded the token, looked up the metadata in the registry, and stitched the two together into an enriched ad event.This design served us well. The registry was globally replicated, which gave us a useful property almost for free: an event arriving in any region could find its metadata regardless of where the ad was decided. It future-proofed the token against ever-growing metadata, and it carried us through the first phase of Netflix’s advertising growth.The ad server writes per-impression metadata to the globally replicated registry on the serving path, and the Ads Event Publisher reads it back to enrich each client event.But as we looked ahead, especially to ads on live streams, the limits of this design became clear.Why we needed to evolveThe forcing function was Live. Netflix’s live slate has grown rapidly, from NFL games to blockbuster fight nights to the FIFA Women’s World Cup 2027, and viewership keeps growing. That traffic looks nothing like Subscription Video on Demand (SVOD): a single event can surge toward tens of millions of concurrent viewers within minutes, then drain just as fast when it ends. Building the ad pipeline for Live meant building for that trajectory.To see why the old design could not get there, let’s put some numbers on it. When Netflix streamed the Jake Paul vs. Mike Tyson fight to more than 100 million viewers, a single ad break at that scale would generate an enormous volume of ad events. Serving those ads in the old design would have meant writing metadata for each of them to the Metadata Registry and waiting for acknowledgment before the ads could serve: millions of must-succeed database writes per second on the ad-serving critical path. That pattern created three core problems at Live scale:It was a scaling bottleneck. Every ad and every ad break is a write. Multiply that by tens of millions of concurrent viewers, keep it non-lossy (a dropped write is a dropped ad, and lost revenue), add Live’s spiky on/off pattern, and the write load becomes enormous. But you cannot safely scale the registry up once the event starts. Adding nodes reshuffles data across the cluster to rebalance it, and doing that in the middle of a live event could slow or even fail the very writes you are adding capacity for. That leaves one safe option: pre-scaling for the peak in advance and hoping the forecast is accurate.Fewer dependencies on the critical path. Serving an ad meant a synchronous, must-succeed write to the registry, followed by a wait for its acknowledgment. Every system on the critical path is one more thing that can stop an ad from serving, and the metadata registry had no fallback, so an unhealthy registry meant serving stopped. Taking hard dependencies off that path was the goal, and the registry was the biggest one we could remove.It put event freshness at risk. If writing slowed down under load, or lookups timed out, events spilled into retries and dead-letter queues, and enriched ad events were delivered late. Some systems might be fine, but others, such as frequency capping, cannot. It needs events under a strict SLA to decide whether the next selected ad can run without breaking policies.So we asked a simple question: the ad server already knows and logs everything about the ad it served. Does it really need to write that to a database on the hot path? Can we match that against the downstream Kafka stream of client events, which is always there, after the fact?The write leaves the critical path entirely; no shared datastore outage can stop serving, and enrichment becomes something we scale elastically with Live rather than a cluster we babysit.Matching two streams after the fact is a streaming join, and that is where Flink comes in.Why FlinkWe chose Apache Flink because near-real-time, stateful stream processing is exactly what it is built for, and because it has deep, production-tested platform support at Netflix. Just as important, we already knew how to run it. As Part 1 noted, our Ads Sessionizer is a large stateful Flink job, so operating one at scale, tuning state, checkpoints, and recovery under production load, was familiar ground for the team. Enrichment on Flink was built on that experience rather than starting from scratch.The new architectureAt its core, the new Ads Event Publisher is built as an Apache Flink streaming job. Each event moves through a handful of stages:Normalize. Client events arrive from several sources, including SVOD, live, playback, and embedded ad breaks, each with a slightly different shape. We first normalize them into a common form.Re-route. Client events and response events may appear in different regions; we inspect and reroute them to the correct job-processing region for a successful join.Join. The heart of the job: we match each client event to the ad-serving response for the same impression, enriching it with the full serving context using a KeyedCoProcessFunctionDeduplicate. Revenue-bearing events are de-duplicated, so nothing is counted twice.Publish. We extract measurement beacons for our partners and route the enriched ad event to its downstream consumers.The ad server logs responses to Kafka rather than writing to a registry; the Flink job joins responses with client events in real time and routes enriched ad events downstream.The join is the interesting part. Client events and ad serving responses arrive on separate streams at different times, so the job holds recent responses in memory and matches events against them over a 60-minute window. Three things can happen:The response is already there when an event arrives → we enrich it on the spot.The event arrives first → we hold it for a configuration duration (currently set to 60 minutes) until the matching response shows up, then enrich it.No response arrives within the window → we mark the event as unmatched for later recovery.We built the join as a custom Flink operator (a KeyedCoProcessFunction) rather than a built-in windowed join. We do not need a windowed join ordering and time-window semantics, since downstream consumers handle ordering; we simply match each client event to its response by key. Owning the operator at its lowest API form lets us control the matching state directly, including how long we hold it and what we do with an event that never matches. This allows us to tune retention per stream or persist unmatched events to a durable store later without rewriting the join. Our goal is always to build a strong base to reap the benefits later as we scale.To make that concrete, picture a member watching a live game. When the ad server decides which ad to show, it logs its response, and a few seconds later, the member’s device fires a “first quartile” event carrying the opaque token. Both arrive at the job keyed to the same impression, and because the response is already waiting, the event is enriched on the spot with its campaign, creative, price, and tracking URLs, then published downstream in near real-time.Why 60 minutes? Almost all events, roughly 99.999% of them, reach the job well before that, so extending the window further would only grow the state store and lengthen checkpoints for a vanishingly small gain. The remaining 0.001% still matters for precise billing and reporting, and we recover those events via a separate path described later on.Cross-region: matching events where their data livesThe Metadata Registry handled cross-region enrichment for us because it was built on a globally replicated key-value store: a client event could land in any region and still find its ad’s metadata. Global replication was a feature the platform provided (see Netflix’s Key-Value Data Abstraction Layer), so serving an ad in one region and enriching its events in another was simply not something we had to think about.A streaming join does not come with that convenience, and here the two sides of the join live in different places. The ad serving response stays in the region where the ad was served. The client event, though, is fired by the device, and the device can be anywhere: a member might start an ad on home wifi and switch to mobile data partway through, and that network change can route the event to a different region than the one that served the ad. So a client event is routed to a region that does not have the matching serving response.The tempting fix is to stream every region’s serving responses into every other region so that each region can match any client event locally. We rejected it: that would mean every region storing every serving response worldwide, the vast majority of which will never be used in the join, which is wasteful in both cost and compute.Instead, we treat this as a routing problem and route the client event rather than replicating the response. We encode the serving region into the ad token, so when a client event arrives in the wrong region, we forward it to the region that owns its response. For example, an ad is served in us-east-2, the member’s device switches from wifi to mobile data mid-ad, and the resulting event arrives in us-east-1. That region reads the token, sees the ad belongs to eu-west-1, and forwards the event back there, where its serving response is waiting. Only the small trickle of genuinely cross-region events pays for the extra hop.Catching the long tail of late-arriving eventsSixty minutes covers the vast majority of events, but not all of them. A device may have been offline, an ad paused, or an engagement event delayed by hours. Rather than drop these stragglers, we set them aside and recover them through a companion path: an hourly Spark batch job, orchestrated by Maestro, Netflix’s workflow orchestrator, re-pairs each straggler with its serving response from our data warehouse (which has a far longer retention than our in-stream window) and publishes it through the same downstream sinks. This keeps the streaming job’s memory bounded while still catching the long tail.This also lets us support event types that arrive well after the 60-minute window, including certain tracking-related events, while keeping the door open to extending the system to additional event types down the line and improving our match rate as needed. We also designed this offline job with reuse in mind: it can replay dead-letter queues and backfill historical data, giving us a single mechanism for both long-tail recovery and operational recovery.What was hardBeyond the architecture itself, three things took real work, and together they are what made the pipeline stable, correct, and safe to switch on.Keeping the core lean.Operating a stateful streaming join at live-event scale took real tuning, and most of our hard-won lessons come back to one idea: the job’s only job is matching events. Anything else we were tempted to bolt on, such as external lookups or incidental side-processing, was better moved off the hot path, or it eventually showed up as instability under load. Getting this right was the difference between a job that comfortably kept pace with live traffic and one that fell behind. It is unglamorous work, and it is most of the work.De-duplicating EventsDuplicates are a fact of life here in an event processing pipeline. A client may retry an event, and our upstream delivery is at-least-once so that the same event can arrive more than once. For revenue-bearing events, the ones that feed billing, nothing should be counted twice.The solution here is a simple and idempotent key. For each event, we compute a stable ID by hashing a few fields that uniquely identify it, such as the ad ID, the event type, and, for repeatable events, an offset or timestamp. The same underlying event always hashes to the same ID, so if we have seen an ID before, we drop the repeat.What changed with Flink is where we remember those IDs. The previous design kept them in an external key-value store and did a check-and-write over the network for every event; Flink lets us keep that state local to the job instead. We key the stream by event ID, which routes every copy of a given event to the same place, and a local state store with a short time-to-live remembers the IDs we have recently seen. Keying by ID also removes the race the external store had to guard against, since a given ID is handled in only one place at a time, with no network hop on the hot path. Multiple improvements here alone.We are deliberate about the guarantee: at-least-once delivery with best-effort de-duplication, not exactly-once. In-stream dedup removes the vast majority of duplicates within its window. However, a few can still slip past, for instance, through a cross-region reroute, a late-arriving recovery, or a restart that replays recent events. So we also carry the event ID through to the enriched ad event itself, which lets consumers who need exact counts, such as billing, run their own idempotent de-duplication over their own, longer windows.Production RolloutThis was the most challenging part of the project. For a system that feeds billing and frequency capping, “trust me, the new one is correct” is not good enough, so we ran the Flink pipeline alongside the legacy one and made it earn its traffic.Both pipelines continuously processed the full event stream, allowing us to compare them in real time: we published their outputs side by side. We ran automated audits that alerted us when the two drifted apart. Those audits earned their keep, surfacing subtle discrepancies we would never have caught from dashboards alone.How do we know the enrichment logic between the two systems was equal for the audits to make sense? Early in development, we extracted that logic into a shared module that both the legacy service and the Flink job depend on, so any change to how an event is enriched is applied to both at the same time. A failure in the audits, therefore, pointed to a real difference in the pipeline mechanics.Cutover was a separate question from comparison, and this is the part that warrants precision. We could not simply send a random percentage of events to each pipeline, because the same event landing in both would be double-counted downstream. Instead, the split was deterministic: a stable key on each event decided which pipeline was responsible for publishing it, so every event was published exactly once, even though both pipelines saw all of them. Raising the threshold moved events from the legacy pipeline to Flink a slice at a time, and we could dial it back instantly if anything looked off.Both pipelines process the same events behind a gradual traffic dial; an audit compares their outputs until we trust them; then the legacy service and registry are retired.By the time we fully cut over, the two pipelines agreed on well over 99.99% of events, and we rolled it out region by region rather than all at once. Then came the satisfying part: we turned off the ad server’s writes to the Metadata Registry, and decommissioned the legacy service and the registry itself. The single point of failure on the ad-serving critical path is simply gone. One of the oldest ad serving components at Netflix was deleted.Operating the Flink platformOperating a large stateful streaming job in production is its own job, and we did not build that muscle alone. We leaned heavily on Netflix’s Flink platform team. Early on, the job’s checkpoints were slow and heavy. The first checkpoint in particular took a long time, and we were holding more state in memory than we needed. Working with the platform team, we trimmed the state, reduced checkpoint durations, and validated the gains in load tests. That is what keeps the job from stalling during Live bursts.The same team also gave us operational scaffolding we did not have to invest in, including continuous delivery and canary deployments, so we could ship and roll out the Flink job with the same guardrails and confidence as the rest of our production systems. Netflix operates more than 25,000 Flink jobs (see Building a Scalable Flink Platform at Netflix), and that maturity is what we have to build on. We layered on custom canary metrics that catch issues before a release, CI/CD that lets us stagger rollouts, and a deploy flow that keeps the old job running until the new one has detected healthy checkpoints, so we never destroy a working job before the new job gives us the green light.The resultsEnrichment is off the critical path for ad serving. The ad server no longer performs a synchronous, must-succeed database write to serve an ad. It logs and moves on, and an entire class of “the lookup store is down, so ad serving is down” failures no longer exists.Fewer systems, fewer schemas, fewer hops. We retired both the legacy service and the Metadata Registry.Built for Live, and continuously tested for it. An ad break is what this service is designed to handle, so during our journey, we also ran large-scale load tests to simulate real-life traffic. In our latest load test, a single AWS region handled one ad break for a large concurrent audience, processing hundreds of thousands of events per second, joining the large majority of them against their serving responses in real time, and outputting enriched ad events at a comparable rate, all with negligible timeouts and errors. We test this throughout development and keep doing so as live audiences grow, and we are not at the ceiling yet.We delivered the Part 1 roadmap. Live-stream ad events, in-stream de-duplication, and richer real-time enrichment all shipped on this foundation.Key takeawaysTake the database off the hot path when you can. Our biggest win wasn’t raw performance — it was blast radius. Trading a synchronous write-and-wait for a fire-and-forget log plus a downstream join removed an entire failure mode from ad serving.Match events where their data lives. Real-time joins beat global replication by routing events to their data rather than copying the data everywhere.Earn the cutover. Running both systems in parallel and diffing their output on live traffic was slower than a flag-day switch — and it’s the only reason we trust the result.What’s nextWe have an exciting list of projects on the horizon: scaling the live path for the next tier of concurrent audiences, bringing the remaining delayed engagement events fully into the streaming world, reducing the Flink state size we need to manage, working on bringing even more resilience to the eventing processing pipeline, and continuing to add new event types and richer signals for our downstream consumers.Re-architecting the engine of a running, revenue-critical pipeline in place was a real multi-team effort. If building real-time systems at this kind of scale sounds like your thing, Ads Engineering is hiring.AcknowledgementsThis was a multi-team effort across Netflix. #4588. Ace Combat 8: Wings Of Theve Review – Soaring Higher Than Ever

Reviewed on: PC
Platform: PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, PC
Publisher: Bandai Namco
Developer: Bandai Namco
Release: October 2, 2026
Rating: Teen

Ace Combat 8: Wings of Theve flies on the tailwinds of its predecessor, which brought life back to the series in 2019. Yet it also dips deep into the series' wealth of mechanics and themes – wingmen, huge mission areas, memorable rivals – to forge what feels like the ace of aces, and one of the series' best entries.

In the wake of an invasion of your homeland, you go down in a dogfight with Sotoa's elite Shadow squadron alongside the "Wings of Theve," a folk hero for the FCU that you discover is a little more tall tale than living legend. You don his identity and become the new "Wings of Theve" to help keep the war effort alive.

Though Ace Combat is certainly no stranger to stories about war, politics, and their effect on the world at large, Wings of Theve takes it to a new height thanks to its focus on your wingmen and the crew of your carrier, the Endurance. Over 30 missions, you build a small band of surviving fighters aboard your meager fleet, all hoping to make it to the conflict's resolution. From your Joker Squadron and sister Queen Squadron to enemy aces and crew aboard the Endurance, the cast is both packed and memorable.

Though there are still some odd story wrinkles, specifically when asking for an arbitrary button press to advance the scene, and the voice of the former Wings pilot Cope speaking to you from the beyond lays on a little thick at the start, Ace Combat 8's story soars thanks to its new first-person perspective and focus on the squadron around you and other members of the Endurance. Big moments in missions hit that much harder when you've spent time around these pilots, seen their struggles up close, and then have to desperately battle alongside them for your life in the skies above.

Each mission is a brilliantly laid-out box of objectives and tasks, an impressive feat given the variety of what Bandai's Aces team asks you to accomplish. In one, you soar across a vast desert, taking out strategic reserves of power, ammo, and hangars full of planes to whittle down an invasion force's resources. In another, you jet toward an island at high speeds and low altitudes to avoid radar detection; then, several missions later, you're trailing massive cargo planes in the sky, following their contrails because radar jamming masks their exact position.

A wealth of planes and weapons are available, and all feel fantastic to fly. The jets feel a little heavier than in Ace Combat 7, but it's a good heft; in the midst of a high-G turn, it appropriately feels like you're wresting the nose of a giant mass of steel at sonic speeds. You can also pack two special weapons at a time, which lets you specialize more and opens up more options in your hangar for each outing. I still found myself beelining straight for the F-35C, but enjoyed several of the other options as well; one special section involving an A-10 bomber was a particularly fun break from the whiplash-inducing missile rodeo of other missions.

 

It's not just missiles the enemy will throw at you, either. Get used to hearing "Rail Gun Warning" in your headphones, as Sotoa's fleet of land battleships and other terra-naval units make ground forces especially troublesome in Ace Combat 8. Sotoa's Lux Lunae, or the Shadows as your squad calls them, are also a highlight. From their sleek black paint jobs and open-comms chanting as they enter the battlefield to their mid-air maneuvers, the Shadows continually push you to the edge and beyond. Besting the Lux Lunae's aces was a consistent source of improvement and achievement as a virtual pilot.

Multiplayer pushes even further, with modes for those who just like to chase high scores and those who want to dogfight other human pilots. Modes where your four-flier squad compete for score, either alongside or against an enemy squadron, make up the bulk of multiplayer playtime. The star attraction is the emergency sorties: special missions that fire up every so often for all players, pitting them against Ace Combat superweapons like the Arsenal Bird. It's a solid base with room to grow for future support, though a lot of menuing, grinding for specific plane upgrade needs, and cosmetic monetization options put speed bumps on my playtime more often than I'd have liked.

Ace Combat 8: Wings of Theve perfectly represents why fliers have stuck with this series. A fantastic campaign, filled with missions I want to replay over and over again, is buoyed by a compelling cast and story, excellent flight controls, explosive dogfights, and encounters you can't wait to scream through at supersonic speeds. Ace Combat 8 proves the series is no museum piece; Wings of Theve soars with the best of the best, and it's a blast to play again and again.
