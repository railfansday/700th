#9794. Do You Compare Yourself to Your Partner? It’s Not All Bad

📅 25 Oct 2026 | Category: other | Source
After venturing through the wasteland of modern dating, it would be natural to harbor disdain for social comparison. Dating apps require you to stand out, which means you can’t just be yourself; you need to sell yourself as more appealing than the 50,000 sexy singles in your area. Putting yourself on display and knowing you’re being compared to others can feel exhausting. But once you’ve swiped right, matched, and established a lasting romantic relationship, surely the social comparisons are all in the rearview mirror…right? That couldn’t be further from the truth. Research suggests we compare ourselves to romantic partners more than anyone else in our lives—on average several times per day. In my own studies, I’ve endeavored to unpack these daily partner comparisons to understand: What kinds of people make partner comparisons, and why? The answer, it seems, depends on the type of comparisons partners are making: either based on our unique abilities, or on the opinions we hold. The good news: Comparisons can offer a learning opportunity and even bring us closer together as partners. Who’s doing better? Many partner comparisons revolve around who is better at a given activity—also known as “ability-based comparisons.” These comparisons can range from trivial (Who’s the better bowler?) to weighty (Who’s the better parent?). In my latest study with collaborators Robin Edelstein and Stephen Garcia, we studied patterns in who makes more of these ability-based comparisons. We surveyed over 1,100 U.S. adults in romantic relationships, asking them questions about how regularly they compare themselves to their partners. For instance, one question asked them, “To what extent do you pay a lot of attention to how you do things relative to how your significant other does things?” Participants also completed measures of self-esteem, relationship quality, and demographics. The results were clear: People who were younger, those with lower self-esteem, and those in shorter and less satisfying relationships were particularly likely to make ability-based comparisons. Men in relationships with men also reported making more of these comparisons than any other gender pairing, but we only surveyed 37 men in same-gender relationships, so take this finding with a grain of salt. Does my partner think differently? A second type of comparison involves comparing judgments and attitudes—known as “opinion-based comparisons.” In these comparisons, people evaluate their own opinions by comparing with those of their partners. For instance, before enthusiastically locking in a major purchase, a partner might wonder, “Would my partner consider this a good deal, too?” Similar considerations may arise in the contexts of voting, child care, or anywhere judgments lead to decisions. When it came to who was most prone to making opinion-based comparisons, our results flipped: These were most common among people in longer and more satisfying relationships. The equity-assessor and the self-improver Taken together, these individual characteristics paint two portraits of people who frequently compare themselves to their partners. The first portrait is that of a young, insecure partner who is in a newer relationship and unsure whether that relationship will work out. Think Bella Swan from Twilight who worries endlessly about whether Edward is too good for her. And, indeed, when we asked participants why they made partner comparisons, two of their motives included assessing equity and predicting the relationship’s trajectory. For instance, participants grappled with questions like, “Am I doing for her as she is doing for me?” The second portrait is that of someone in a long-term relationship who respects the knowledge and expertise of their partner and seeks to harness it as a tool for growth and decision making. For this one, think Leslie Knope from Parks and Rec who regularly compares her own judgments to those of her long-term work wife, Ann Perkins, as a means to make better decisions. This theme, too, emerged from our participants. For instance, one participant lovingly wrote about how his partner outshines him in most areas, and this dynamic continually inspires him to grow. Understanding me and you Yet partner comparison isn’t limited to these two portraits. Whereas these portraits describe those who compare most frequently, we found most people make some comparisons sometimes. What motivates occasional comparers? The two most common motives for partner comparison were striving to gain a deeper understanding of oneself and one’s partner. With respect to self-evaluation, one participant shared that they compare regularly to their partner because comparison helps them benchmark their progress toward goals. Want to know if your cooking class has paid off? One of the best ways to evaluate your growth is to see if your latest dish can delight your family in the same way that your partner’s cooking always does. Partner comparisons can also help us understand our partners’ quirks, what we call partner evaluation. For instance, one participant shared how she compares her upbringing to that of her partner, which helps her understand why her partner thinks, feels, and behaves so differently than she does. Partner comparison is a tool It’s no secret that social comparison can sting. No parent wants to hear, “I like Mom better,” and no newlywed wants to hear, “How did you land such a catch?” We find in our studies that partner comparisons are often involuntarily thrust upon people who would have preferred not to be compared. It can be true what they say, “Comparison is the thief of joy.” But comparison does more than push our emotional buttons. Partner comparisons help us assess our relationship and calibrate our behavior, such as how we divide household labor. They allow us to make important decisions, like who should take ownership over responsibilities. They serve as sources of growth, and they teach us about ourselves and our partners. Comparing yourself to your partner isn’t necessarily a sign that something’s wrong. Rather, partner comparison can be a handy strategy for making sense of your social world. The key is to use it as a tool that stays in its place, rather than letting it undermine your self-worth or relationship. Serena D’souza contributed to this article.

#9793. How Your Imagination Can Work for You or Against You

📅 25 Oct 2026 | Category: other | Source
I have a pretty vivid imagination, sometimes for the good, sometimes not. I love it when it helps me to create new stories or songs, or when I can daydream about fun times ahead. But it can also lead to fear or worry, like when I imagine something terrible befalling the people or places I love. Is there some way to nurture the positive aspects of imagination while minimizing the problematic? According to Cassandra Vieten, director of research at the Arthur C. Clarke Center for Human Imagination, there is. In her new book, Imagine That, she explains imagination’s importance in our everyday lives—a way to understand our past, navigate the present, and explore future possibilities. If we learn more about its inner workings, she argues, we can use it more consciously and deliberately to create the kind of life we want. “Imagination is something we all possess, something we can all practice, for free and with equal opportunity,” says Vieten. “When someone develops this capacity, they gain something almost magical: the ability to see multiple futures simultaneously and navigate toward the preferred ones.” Imagination as part of everyday life Many of us tend to conflate imagination with creativity, thinking that being able to imagine new ideas is the same as bringing them to fruition. While the two may indeed be related, this is far too limited a view of what imagination can be, writes Vieten. “Imagination is an essential, foundational capacity that allows us to simulate reality and transcend it to explore possibilities beyond the known,” she says. In our everyday lives, imagination is what helps us make predictions as well as come up with novel (or creative) ideas. For example, we might imagine how stopping for a coffee on our way to work will help us focus (simulation imagination, based on past experience). But we might also use our imagination to transcend what’s known and come up with a totally new innovation or product idea once we arrive at the office (generative imagination). “Imagination weaves throughout the fabric of our cognitive, sensory, and relational lives—you are constantly speculating, visualizing, rehearsing, wondering,” she writes. These types of imagination show up differently in the brain, writes Vieten. Simulation is primarily centered in the default mode network (DMN), a part of the brain that remains active when we aren’t engaging in a specific task. The DMN is where we tend to mull over future interactions, plans, or options, often subconsciously (or even unconsciously). Generative imagination, on the other hand, comes by freeing ourselves from the DMN’s limitations (or, some would say, tyranny). If we can get out of the everyday chatter and let our minds truly wander freely around disparate parts of the brain, making new neural connections, we have a better chance of generating something truly unique. “Rather than simulating or iterating, comparing and contrasting, generative imagination is focused on combining, reconfiguring, or transcending existing knowledge to generate novel outputs,” writes Vieten. Either way, imagination isn’t frivolous, she writes; it’s important for our everyday functioning as well as creative self-expression. It helps us solve problems, while also expanding possibilities of what could be, giving us “the gift of wonder, whimsy, and surprise, the whoas and the wows of our lives,” she writes. Imagination’s darker side Imagination can sometimes be problematic, though. For example, we might imagine that our spouse is mad at us and worry about what might happen when she gets home from work when, actually, she’s not mad at all. This can cause us needless worry, not to mention potentially trigger a fight. We can also imagine that other people are unpleasant or even dangerous based on untested biases. These can be formed in part via our imagination, which tries to prepare us for unknown encounters, often behind the scenes at an unconscious level. “We regularly imagine entire narratives, scenes, and interactions without intending to or even knowing we’re doing it,” writes Vieten. Our imagination is often busy filling in the missing parts of our memories and perceptions, which are by nature incomplete. Memories, rather than being etched in our minds, are recreated (and often changed) each time they’re recalled. Similarly, we can’t perceive everything at once, so our imagination completes the picture for us, adding interpretations, too. This means it’s easy to corrupt memories or miss details of a situation—which can sometimes lead to problems (think eyewitness accounts of crimes, for example). Understanding these darker sides of imagination can help us tame them somewhat, says Vieten. “You can practice bringing greater attention to what you are imagining, recognize it as imagination, and decide whether it holds useful information, is irrelevant, or is actually causing harm.” How to increase your imaginative capabilities While the book covers the neuroscience behind imagination, it’s also about how to harness our imagination more effectively. Your personal imagination style may depend, in part, on your genetics (such as neurodiversity), culture, and life experiences. Understanding this can help you figure out where you might want to try moving the needle one way or the other. To assess your own imaginative strengths and weaknesses, here are some key imagination modes Vieten outlines in her book. 1. Receptiveness. “How willing you are to imagine—and how receptive you are to whatever bubbles up.” If you are high in receptivity, you may find it easy to do guided visualizations, for example; if low in receptivity, you may be less willing to imagine unknown scenarios. 2. Presence. How much do you embody your imagined experiences? If you tend to be absorbed, immersed, and emotionally involved in imagined scenarios, you’re high in presence; if you tend to observe these imaginings from a distance, you’re less so. 3. Embellishment. “How vivid, detailed, and elaborate people make their imaginings.” Those high in embellishment can imagine intricate scenarios with much more detail, clarity, nuance, and complexity. Those low in embellishment might be more “big picture” folks, who may not be able to conjure up details as much. 4. Originality. How much your imagination is fueled by what is or could be, based on reality, versus how much it veers from that and is based more on fantasy and novelty. 5. Temporality. How much your imagination is associated with time (in other words, assessing the past or projecting into the future) versus how much it is agnostic around time (not based on any particular time). (Vieten admits the science on this mode is not completely settled, but says there appear to be some differences in how people engage with time while imagining.) Each of these modes has pros and cons, she argues. For example, being high in receptivity may mean you’re more cognitively flexible and open to new experiences, but can also mean you’re overly compliant and easily manipulated. Similarly, the upside of being high in originality is that it can fuel advancements in art and science, though it can lead to boredom with everyday life. Considering my own strengths and weaknesses, I noticed that I seem to be high in receptivity and embodiment, but low in embellishment and originality (and mixed when it comes to temporality). Recognizing this makes me think about paying more attention to details in my environment and playing around with more fantastical ideas, especially if I’m wanting to fuel my creativity. To that end, Vieten provides avenues for stretching our imagination in different ways. Some ideas for accessing different aspects of imagination deliberately include rehearsing something before doing it, brainstorming ideas, engaging all of our senses when imagining something, reframing an idea from a different perspective, and freewriting. Vieten also suggests creating environments conducive to being more spontaneously creative. Setting time aside to daydream, being in solitude where your mind can wander, taking walks in the woods to de-stress, or, possibly, taking psychedelic drugs (in a safe, controlled environment) can all help us disengage our DMN and allow creative thoughts to enter more freely. “One of the key ways to make imagination welcome is to remove the practical pressures of the world,” she writes. Beyond self-help Imagine That is full of suggestions for unleashing your imagination, all based on the emerging science of imagination that Vieten and her colleagues (and others) are exploring. But while the book reads mostly as a self-help guide, there’s also an important message about why using our imagination is beneficial to society as a whole. Too many of us are experiencing the hopelessness and despair that come with being alive right now, writes Vieten. It’s too easy to get lost in the negative news cycles and focus on all of the problems in the world, making it more difficult to imagine anything better for our collective future. Learning how to engage our imagination more readily can create a sense of hope in our current situation. Not only can it bring more joy and aliveness to our individual lives, countering malaise, it can also lead to new ways of collaborating with others and innovating to solve societal problems, thereby providing a better life for everyone. “So many of the issues facing us locally and globally are rooted in our collective inability to imagine new solutions, alternative futures, creative pathways forward that could build a world that promotes thriving for all,” writes Vieten. “My deepest hope is that [you] take a deeper dive into the science, the practice, and the magic of imagination and [see] how it can re-enchant your life and our world.”

#9792. Nine Conversation-Starting Movies to Watch With Your Romantic Partner

📅 25 Oct 2026 | Category: other | Source
Movie date nights have long been a ritual of romance, from first dates to married couples’ standing nights out. You can probably name movies that leave you feeling warm, hopeful, excited, stimulated, and sometimes a little more in love with your partner. But sometimes, the movies we watch become more than that—they become conversation starters, litmus tests, or unexpected mirrors of our own relationships. These nine movies expose the underbelly of romantic relationships: the resentments, fantasies, secrets, compromises, and alternative lives that can coexist alongside love. Watching them runs the risk of cracking open a door that would be better kept closed, for the longevity of your relationship. Last month’s The Invite, starring and directed by Olivia Wilde, tops our list of nine movies you might want to watch without your partner—or at least be very sure you know what conversation you’re signing up for in the post-screening debrief. Each movie asks a question that might be safer left unanswered. 1. The Invite (2026) What happens when you say the quiet part out loud? The Invite turns the dinner-party setup into an examination of everything couples are usually too embarrassed, jealous, or afraid to say to each other. Angela (Wilde) and Joe (Seth Rogen) portray a married couple whose relationship has become strained by years of resentment and disappointment. When Angela invites their upstairs neighbors, Piña (Penélope Cruz) and Hawk (Edward Norton), over for dinner, what begins as an attempt at connection becomes a much more revealing conversation about sex, attraction, love, and what people want from their partners, when they’re being honest. As Piña and Hawk probe the tension between Angela and Joe—also glimpsed through their apartment windows and in elevator encounters—they force the quiet parts of the other couple’s relationship into the open. The questions Angela and Joe were afraid to ask each other come to the surface, like, “Do we love each other anymore? Can we salvage this relationship? Do you want to be with someone new?” “Novelty is a powerful aphrodisiac. In long-term relationships especially, cultivating novelty—whether through stories or experiences—is key for sustaining passion,” says Esther Perel, sex therapist and consultant on The Invite, on her blog. “Maintaining a sense of mystery and surprise helps counteract the routine and repetition that can sometimes lead to a decline in desire over time.” We tend to think of monogamy as a set of universally accepted rules: Don’t sleep with someone else. Don’t cross the line. But long-term relationships are also built on unspoken knowledge about what our partner wants, what they find attractive, what they would never do, and what they might secretly wish they could do. Lifting the lid on that box may reveal that the relationship is beyond repair. 2. Indecent Proposal (1993) What’s more important: Love or money? Would you risk the former for the latter? Can a couple withstand different answers to this question? David (Woody Harrelson) and Diana (Demi Moore) are a financially struggling, yet happily married, couple, who are offered $1 million if the wife spends one night with a wealthy stranger, played by Robert Redford. The movie takes a question that sounds like a parlor game and puts it inside a marriage: What, exactly, would you be willing to sacrifice for a life-changing amount of money? Diana’s decision to revisit the proposal after David’s initial hesitation—an uncomfortable pause that both she and the audience notice—begins her agreed-upon one-night stand. From there, suspicion and mistrust begin to take hold, gradually eroding the relationship and ultimately pushing Diana toward the wealthy stranger. What happens when a hypothetical becomes real? When money, desire, jealousy, and pride enter a marriage, it becomes much harder to separate what you thought you would do from what you did. Indecent Proposal can turn a hypothetical into an uncomfortable inventory of your partner’s values—and your own. Although it’s not every day a millionaire asks to sleep with a spouse, the question, and the length of the pause before answering, can reveal a lot about both sides. 3. Unfaithful (2002) Can anyone be tempted, and can one handle the consequences of infidelity? Unfaithful, starring Diane Lane and Richard Gere, is a movie about a married suburban woman who meets a younger man and becomes increasingly drawn to him. There is no grand declaration that her marriage is over, no decision to go looking for an affair. There is simply attraction, followed by curiosity, a series of small choices that gradually lead down a road from which there is no return. Some people believe that infidelity happens only to certain kinds of couples—unhappy couples, disconnected couples, couples whose marriages were already in trouble. But some people in loving relationships can still notice other people, feel chemistry, or wonder what it would be like to be with someone else. In fact, that is part of what sucks you into Unfaithful: Richard Gere’s character is a quintessentially good husband—a devoted father, attentive partner, and decent man. “I often find that the affair is a form of self-discovery. It’s not that the individuals having the affairs want to leave their partners, but the people they have become. They are looking for another version of themselves—which is the most powerful variety of ‘other’ there is,” says Perel in an interview with Goop. A crush can remain a crush. A fantasy can remain a fantasy. But sometimes people begin feeding the attraction, creating opportunities to see the person again, or keeping secrets that gradually become more important than the relationship they are protecting. Interesting fact: Both Indecent Proposal and Unfaithful were directed by Adrian Lyne, who’s been married since 1974 to his wife Samantha. 4. Marriage Story (2019) Can you love someone and still need to leave them? Marriage Story begins with a husband and wife listing the things they love about each other. It’s tender enough to feel like the opening of a great romance, but the film quickly reveals a more complicated reality. Inspired by director Noah Baumbach’s own divorce, the story explores what happens when the things we love about our partner begin to pull us in different directions. Charlie (Adam Driver) loves that Nicole (Scarlett Johansson) makes people feel comfortable, knows how to handle difficult family situations, plays with their son, knows when to push him and when to leave him alone. Nicole loves Charlie’s determination, his energy, his intelligence, and the way he throws himself into the things he loves. It is an intimate portrait of a couple who, despite everything that will follow, genuinely know and admire one another. The film follows Charlie and Nicole through the painful mechanics of divorce: lawyers, custody arrangements, money, competing versions of the marriage, and the realization that ending a relationship does not mean you stop having to deal with the person you are leaving. Nicole has spent years adapting herself to Charlie’s life and putting parts of her own ambitions aside. Charlie has built a career and a world around his creative work but has failed to recognize how much his wife has sacrificed to support it. Neither is simply the villain. Neither is simply the victim. That is one of the film’s most uncomfortable truths about long-term relationships: Love does not necessarily disappear before a marriage ends. Their issues aren’t due to flat-out incompatibility; they are problems of identity, compromise, resentment, and the slow accumulation of problems that were never fully addressed. Another question the movie asks is whether love is enough to make a marriage work. 5. Gone Girl (2014) Do we really know our partners? Gone Girl turns marital secrets into psychological warfare; this is anything but a love story. Based on Gillian Flynn’s novel, the film follows Nick Dunne (Ben Affleck) on the day his wife, Amy (Rosamund Pike) disappears. As the investigation unfolds, the image of their marriage begins to unravel, leaving everyone—including the audience—to wonder whether Nick killed her. At the heart of the film is a deeply unsettling possibility about how little access we have to another person’s reality. One of the qualities that relationship researchers have found important is emotional responsiveness: the feeling that your partner understands you, validates your perspective, and cares about your well-being. In other words, a healthy relationship depends less on “I know everything about you” than on “I feel safe being known by you.” 6. Eyes Wide Shut (1999) Is fantasy a form of betrayal? In Eyes Wide Shut, Alice (Nicole Kidman) and Bill Harford (Tom Cruise) appear to have a comfortable marriage—secure in the routines and flow of the life they built together. That is until Alice tells her husband about a sexual fantasy involving another man. Bill becomes consumed by the idea of what his wife has imagined. The fantasy begins to feel like a secret life, and the distinction between imagining something and wanting it becomes increasingly difficult for him to see. According to Perel, our “sexual fantasies are a fount of information about our internal lives and the relational dynamics of our partnerships.” People also can make complicated decisions about whether to disclose fantasies to their partners. Sometimes disclosure can create intimacy. Sometimes people keep fantasies private because they fear judgment or because the fantasy itself has nothing to do with what they want in real life. Eyes Wide Shut makes that ordinary fact feel like a threat. 7. Revolutionary Road (2008) Are we living the life we actually wanted? In Revolutionary Road, Frank (Leonardo DiCaprio) and April Wheeler (Kate Winslet) have built the respectable suburban life they were supposed to want, in this movie set from the late 1940s to mid 1950s. But April begins to wonder whether they have mistaken a socially approved life for the life they truly desire. What happens when the pendulum swings too heavily toward one person’s idea of the life they could have lived, leaving the other to defend the life they have actually built? In Revolutionary Road, April’s longing for a different future increasingly collides with Frank’s investment in the life they have created together. Long-term relationships inevitably require compromise. But compromise can feel very different when it is experienced as a choice rather than a sacrifice. A relationship should provide a sense of “we” without erasing the “I.” Researchers have also found that relationships can be important sources of personal growth: Partners can help one another explore new identities, pursue goals, and become more fully themselves. Revolutionary Road painfully takes us through the psychological unraveling of Frank and April—who seem to experience their marriage as a narrowing of possibilities rather than an expansion of them. Content warning: The film explores difficult themes involving self-administered abortion, death, and questions autonomy. 8. Closer (2004) Can you love someone and still hurt them? Closer, directed by Mike Nichols and based on Patrick Marber’s play, strips relationships down to their most uncomfortable elements like attraction, jealousy, dishonesty, and the desire to be wanted. The film follows four people—Dan (Jude Law), Alice (Natalie Portman), Anna (Julia Roberts), and Larry (Clive Owen)—whose relationships become increasingly entangled. What begins with a chance encounter between Dan and Anna eventually develops into a complicated web of love affairs, betrayals, and shifting loyalties. It leaves viewers asking, “Do we want to be loved, or do we want to be desired?” The characters repeatedly demand honesty from one another, but when they get it, the truth often hurts more than the lie. They want to know whom their partners have slept with, what they felt, whom they wanted, and whether they were ever truly enough. Yet knowing the truth doesn’t necessarily bring them closer. Sometimes, it gives jealousy something concrete to attach itself to. 9. The Drama (2026) Would you still love your partner if you knew the worst thing they ever did? The Drama takes place in the days surrounding the wedding of Emma Harwood (Zendaya) and Charlie Thompson (Robert Pattison), a young couple whose courtship seemed out of a storybook. Over drinks one night with their best man and maid of honor, they each confess the worst thing they’d done. This revelation sparks disgust and soul-searching, as they’re forced to confront the question: “Do you still love me?” We all seek unconditional love from our partner. The Drama stretches this promise to the breaking point, complicated by the judgment of friends who also know the ugly truth. If you dare to watch this film with your partner, you’d be wise to think twice before sharing each other’s deep, dark secrets.

#9791. Three Qualities That Can Make You an Inspiring Leader

📅 25 Oct 2026 | Category: other | Source
Imagine you’re on a plane and suddenly hear an explosion. The whole plane rattles, and you see the left engine is on fire. After another loud noise, you realize there’s a hole in the side of the aircraft. As air rushes out, the plane is rapidly descending—over 20,000 feet in less than five minutes. If you faced what passengers of Southwest Airlines 1380 experienced, you would probably do what they did: frantically write goodbye messages to your family and loved ones, thinking that these are your last moments on Earth. But on that flight, the pilot, Captain Tammie Jo Shults, came on the intercom and said 10 simple words: “We’re not going down; we’re going to Philly.” The passengers would later talk about how those 10 words were transformative, turning their panic into possibility. By sharing those 10 words, Captain Shults was helping her passengers make sense of their experience and reframe the dramatic descent, from “plummeting to Earth” to a planned and orchestrated path to safety. As Captain Shults expertly landed the plane, she calmly and peacefully talked to air traffic control. After landing, she got up and went row by row, looking every passenger in the eye to make sure they were OK. People talked afterward about her words, her stature, and her empathy as she walked down that aisle. Contrast Captain Shults’s experience with another captain who also dealt with a massive hole in his vessel, Francesco Schettino, captain of the Costa Concordia. Headed out on a seven-day cruise in the Mediterranean, the ship got too close to shore. It’s debatable why that happened: Captain Schettino claims he was saluting mariners on the shore; prosecutors claim he was trying to impress his mistress who was on board. But what isn’t in dispute is what happened next: The rocks tore a 115-foot hole in the side of the ship, which caused it to eventually capsize. Before calling the Coast Guard, Captain Schettino spent time getting his story straight. The crash had caused an electrical blackout, but he was going to try to say that the blackout caused the crash. He tried to pin the responsibility on the helmsman. It took him almost an hour after the crash to sound the evacuation siren. While people were frantically trying to find safety, Captain Schettino was nowhere to be found. Finally, the Coast Guard discovered him in a lifeboat—though Captain Schettino claimed that he “accidentally” fell into the lifeboat. But he also somehow fell out of his captain’s uniform. He was sitting in a lifeboat watching his boat sinking, hiding in civilian clothing. You can hear the outrage in the Coast Guard’s voice when he says in an audio recording, “I’m gonna make you pay for this.” And Captain Schettino did pay. He was sentenced to 12 years in prison for manslaughter and abandoning ship. These two people faced very similar situations. They were both captains, dealing with holes in the side of their vessels, but how they responded to their crises was completely different. Captain Shults helped people make sense of their experience. She was courageous, competent, and calm in landing the plane, and she compassionately connected to each of her passengers. In contrast, Captain Schettino caused the accident, refused to take any responsibility, and did not take any action to help his passengers save their lives. As a result of his selfish negligence, 32 people died, and numerous people were injured. Why do some leaders create feelings of inspiration that percolate a wellspring of hope and possibility in others, while other leaders create seething cauldrons of infuriation and resentment? I’ve asked this question to tens of thousands of people across the globe, and here is what I learned about what makes a leader inspiring. Three aspects of good leadership My first big idea is that “inspiring” and “infuriating” leaders exist on an enduring continuum that is made up of three universal factors:Being a visionary, how we see the world Being an exemplar, how we are in the world Being a mentor, how we interact in the world We can inspire people in how we communicate, how we present ourselves, and how we interact with others—through our words, our actions, and our interactions. My second big idea is that these are the three factors of inspiring leadership because each one fulfills a fundamental human need. Being visionary fulfills the fundamental need for meaning and understanding. That’s what Captain Shults provided when she said, “We’re not going down; we’re going to Philly.” She helped people make sense of their experience. Being an exemplar fulfills the fundamental need for protection and passion. Captain Shults courageously protected her passengers with her calm and competence. And being a mentor fulfills the fundamental human need for belonging and status. That’s what Captain Shults offered as she walked down the aisle: She made her passengers feel recognized and seen. By surveying tens of thousands of people across the globe, I have found these three dimensions to be universal. There is not a single inspiring characteristic that is mentioned in one country that’s not mentioned in the dozens of countries in the world where I have collected data. Visionaries Effective visionaries not only offer a clear perspective on the world, but they also make their vision easy to follow and understand, and so engaging that it captures our attention. The easiest way to make something easy is to make it simple. I call this the Inception Rule, after the movie Inception, where Leonardo DiCaprio tries to plant an idea in someone’s head during a dream. He discovers it’s not about how deep you go; it’s about how simple the idea is. As a leader, you want to constantly be thinking how to reduce your ideas to their simple core. Another way that we can make things easy to understand is to repeat them, the way a chorus repeats in a song. The more people hear it, the more they will remember it. Let’s consider Zoom’s vision: Delivering Happiness. It’s simple, and bubbly. But that vision is also deeply practical—it helped Zoom’s employees efficiently make high-stakes decisions during the chaos of the COVID-19 pandemic. As millions of people began using the product for the first time, Zoom needed to decide on its product’s baseline level of functionality. Too many features would overwhelm people, but too few features would stymie people. They needed the Goldilocks baseline of features to deliver happiness. Similarly, delivering happiness led Zoom to invest heavily in customer service. Most of us know how much an hour-long wait for customer service can destroy happiness. So, Zoom hired many agents so wait times would be as short as possible. To make our visions more engaging, we can use visual language. Andrew Carton, an associate professor of management at Wharton, did a study where people were told that a company’s mission was to either “make our customers satisfied” or “make our customers smile.” Then, he had people create a product. When they were working under the more visual vision (“smile”), they created more engaging products. How to become more visionary: We can inspire people to be more visionary by having them reflect on their values. In one of my studies, we worked with a Swiss employment agency. When people came in to register for benefits, half of them were asked to reflect on their values, why those values mattered to them, and how they demonstrated them. For example, my own values include generosity, courage, and kaizen (a Japanese word for continued improvement). It interrupts the invisible web of relationships that makes everyday life feel familiar, that makes us feel like we belong. And the majority of the 13 million American adults who move each year experience this. At the US Chamber of Connection, our recent study on social connection in America explored the impact of life transitions on social connection. It found that 40% of people navigating a relocation struggle with social connection, and they report 35% more barriers to connection compared to everyone else. Two of the most common barriers are not knowing where to start and not wanting to go out alone. The impact is lasting, as someone shared with us. “Two years ago, I was going on a vacation, and I could not find ONE neighbor to water my plants while I was away. I had lived in this building on and off for over 15 years. I was mortified. How could it be that I had nobody in my building to help with such a small task?” The invisible cost of a new address Moving ranks among the most stressful life events a person can experience, on par with divorce and job loss. But the stress isn’t just logistical; it’s psychological. Moving severs the informal ties and daily routines that quietly anchor our sense of who we are and where we belong. The result is a predictable but rarely named experience: not just loneliness, but identity dislocation. The question isn’t only “who do I know here?” but “who am I here?” To understand the scale of that loss, consider what a typical adult social network requires to build: roughly 200 hours to develop a close friend, 90 hours for a friend, and five hours of repeated interaction for each of the weak ties (neighbors, familiar faces, community contacts) that make daily life feel anchored. A typical adult carries around 1,300 hours of accumulated relationship time. When you move, you leave all of it behind and start from zero. Disruption breaks old habits and makes people unusually open to forming new ones, a phenomenon researchers call the habit discontinuity effect. People who have just moved are more likely to join things, try things, and reach out than they were six months earlier at home. What closes that window isn’t lack of desire. Both the newcomer and the potential neighbor hold back, each assuming the other isn’t interested. Both tend to be wrong. And the effects of getting it right last longer than most people expect. I wanna create spaces where people can land and feel comfortable.” For others, it came from an experience more akin to Darilys’s. “I would have loved to find this kind of thing when I moved,” shared one early volunteer. “I’ve been in my neighborhood and city since 2019 and have yet to make close friends in the neighborhood or city where I live. Kaitlin created a traveling tin of homemade baked goods that moves neighbor to neighbor down the street, each recipient required to bake something and pass it on to someone new. Sarah hosted a gathering for other people named Sara(h) in her town. Courtney started flyering the neighborhood to organize a dog gathering in someone’s backyard. Nithya left a flower and a handwritten note for each of the five nearest neighbors inviting them to coffee. Many of the volunteers nostalgically tried things they remembered from their childhood or had seen in a movie. They mirrored much of what we have learned from social scientists over the last 50 years. Four themes emerged that help to inform how we can all do more to help new residents, and any neighbor, feel more welcomed. 1. The first step is the most important. Stop playing “invite chicken.” Many of our volunteers shared a version of the same experience: sitting at home hoping someone would call and invite them to hang out, attend an event, or even just watch Netflix together. They assumed that the people they wanted to call were probably busy or uninterested. Those people were likely sitting at home thinking the same thing. Nobody called. Everyone stayed alone. Call it invite chicken: a standoff where everyone loses despite everyone wanting the same thing. The barrier isn’t reality; it’s anticipation. Someone just has to go first. And sometimes, when we do break the game of chicken, we don’t get the response we wanted. When Hazel hosted her first neighborhood coffee gathering, one neighbor texted that he was too nervous to come. She didn’t give up. She texted back encouraging him. He came. At one of Elizabeth’s gatherings in San Diego, a man had been dragged along by his wife, who promised they’d stay just five minutes. He was the last to leave. We are poor forecasters of our own social experiences. People overweight the discomfort of initiation and underweight the pleasure of connection. The host who sends the follow-up text, the wife who insists on five minutes, isn’t being pushy. They’re correcting for a cognitive error the reluctant person can’t see from the inside. 2. We are more motivated working together. Partner with someone. Erin hosted a neighborhood gathering in New York City and reflected afterward on what she’d do differently. Her top lesson: “I appreciated the help I did get from neighbors and wished I’d had a co-planner to share more of the work with.” This is consistent with what research finds: People are significantly more likely to initiate and sustain kind and helpful behavior when they’re doing it alongside someone else. A partner doesn’t just make the work easier; they make follow-through more likely, because the social cost of backing out now involves someone else. Several Welcome Committee volunteers named the US Chamber of Connection cohort itself as the thing that got them out the door. Not the training, not the tools, but the fact that others were doing it, too. 3. We gain comfort with repeat exposure. Build a rhythm. Hansen has been hosting a monthly porch gathering (coffee, bagels, same spot) for two years. Recently, he watched neighbors spontaneously make plans to go cycling together and quietly organize to look out for a boy on the street whose father is dying of cancer. Neither of those things was planned. They happened because the same people had been attending his events and they had built comfort with each other. Hansen didn’t suggest they help the boy but had created the environment for it to be possible. Research finds that repeated contact with the same faces makes us like and trust them more, independent of what’s said. Those repeated encounters build weak ties, which are how people find help in a crisis, how trust spreads beyond close friends, how a community starts to look out for itself. The first gathering gets you in the door. Showing up again is what builds the neighborhood. 4. Spontaneous actions of connection are powerful. Look for opportunities. The best welcoming often isn’t planned. When a neighbor left cookies on Kaitlin’s porch with a note that just said “happy spring,” the gesture was small, spontaneous, and agenda-free. It didn’t just make her smile; it made her want to pass something on. Walking through her neighborhood, Annie spotted a moving van, walked straight over, and introduced herself as a Welcome Committee member. The mover, who didn’t even live there, told her he wished he could move into the community. Another volunteer simply invited people they saw running to join their running group. They were seen and welcomed. What these moments share is spontaneity and clarity of intent. Research finds that unexpected gestures create a positive disruption that formal invitations don’t. They bypass the social calculation people run when a stranger approaches, and they signal warmth without asking for anything back. Research finds that being noticed is the precondition for feeling like you belong. And having a clear role removes the ambiguity that makes first moves feel risky. When Annie said “I’m a Welcome Committee member,” her motives were transparent before she said another word. You don’t need a plan. You need to look up. Connection is a lifestyle—a rewarding one Giving to others produces more sustained well-being than spending on yourself. The helper’s high is real. Welcoming a newcomer isn’t just a form of service; it’s one of the highest-return social investments you can make. Liz knows this firsthand. The woman who arrived in the Bay Area not knowing a soul, wondering if anyone would notice if she disappeared, is now the person who creates spaces for others to land. “People here took a chance on me,” she says. “So it gave me this strong sense of duty to pay that forward. I want to create spaces where people can land and feel comfortable.” All friendships begin the same way: by talking to strangers. Someone just has to go first. Years later, Liz became the person who notices.

#9788. The Overlooked Ways Love Shows Up in Urban Spaces

Over the past two decades, my doctoral students and I have focused on studying love, care, kindness, and compassion in urban settings. Why? Because social science research on life in America’s cities is desperately incomplete. Much like the vision of urban life that permeates most of American popular culture, social science research imagines urban areas as sites of overwhelmedness, disengagement, social and moral crisis, threat, and violence. But, as someone who has lived and worked in large urban centers—New York City, Chicago, Detroit, Istanbul—I know firsthand that urban centers are also sites of human virtue. People in urban contexts, including those living in high-stress, oversurveilled, underresourced communities, draw daily from deep wells of love, compassion, empathy, kindness, and generosity in the service of caring for each other. From holding doors open for one another, to offering seats on buses and trains, to stopping to help with directions, to paying unexpected compliments, city-dwellers create environments that are saturated with powerful but easily missed acts of care.  My former students and I have been eager to understand how love, generosity, and care manifest and operate in the hyperdiverse, densely populated, loud, fast-paced complexities of urban spaces. Through surveys and interviews, we have sought to understand how adults residing in big cities define love, how they see love being expressed, and their views of the cascading effects of giving and receiving care, generosity, and love.  We have learned that the decision to express love and care for others in large cities is not simply a moral or emotional decision. We offer two examples of the lens needed to notice the presence of love in cities. First, we have learned that identities matter in experiences of compassion, love, and care between strangers. Urban dwellers’ identity (for example, race or gender), lived experience, context, familial, cultural, and legal systems’ realities work together to shape decisions about whether to extend acts of love to people you do not know. For example, Kehinde, a 26-year-old African American man living in a large, densely populated city, described his experience of giving and receiving kindness, care, and love in his city as shaped by how he navigates other people seeing him as a Black man. “I will see folks all the time that may need a hand with their bags or crossing the street or just little things like that. I don’t want to come off as a threat.” To get through these moments, Kehinde describes engaging in a practice of analysis and shape-shifting. Before he steps in to offer help, he assesses the environment and changes the way he carries himself so that people will perceive him as a possible helper rather than as a threat. He says: “I smile a lot more. My voice might change a bit.” He might shrink his body and keep his head down, to avoid seeming intimidating. After helping someone, he moves along without lingering. Reflecting, he acknowledges not being his full, true self in these interactions. “I have to realize I’ve been socialized—made to believe that this is what I need to do in order to navigate the situation.” Kehinde’s story highlights the point that identities matter in economies of care. The images and stereotypes that we construct about people come at a cost even in moments of compassion. Stereotypes affect who we perceive as deserving of care, concern, and protection. Stereotypes also inform our expectations about who can be trusted to help. Stereotyping is not unique to urban settings. But because urban settings tend to be especially diverse spaces, they create abundant opportunities to study how identities and the social scripts we hold affect the way that strangers care for each other across lines of difference. What Kehinde and others bring to the fore is the reality that the targets of stereotyping are aware of the ways that they are perceived. They learn that to be seen as helpful rather than as threatening, they need to read situations correctly, make adjustments to their approaches (e.g., keep “appropriate” distance), and exit situations strategically. Targets of stereotyping must strike a balance between their own deep-seated motivations to behave caringly and their desire to stay safe. For individuals like Kehinde, miscalculations can be costly. If, in the effort to behave lovingly and compassionately, someone is misidentified as a threat, that can lead to a range of consequences from being rebuffed and embarrassed to becoming the target of violence, arrest, or death. The end result is that people consciously or unconsciously learn the rules of engagement around helping strangers. A second lesson that has emerged from studying love in urban settings is that love lives in the built environment of cities. I highlight this lesson in my Positive Psychology in Urban Settings classes. Cities are often saturated with murals, billboards, and symbols that convey messages of love, solidarity, and belonging. As residents move through neighborhood streets, they may encounter signs prominently posted in shop windows or near the entrances of religious institutions that remind them that “Black Lives Matter,” “Love Is Love,” or “Immigrants Are Welcome Here.” People display rainbow flags and national flags from apartment windows, on cars, and in other spaces, declaring both their own identities and pride for and solidarity with those identities. The architecture of urban spaces may also play a role in expressions of love and care. In many urban neighborhoods, porches, stoops, and front steps serve as places of connection and care. Simple greetings (e.g., “Morning, baby!,” “You doing good?”) voiced by neighbors sitting on their steps serve as ways of spatializing love, care, and connection in urban landscapes.  I return here to my initial point. Our studies of urban areas are desperately incomplete and will remain incomplete until we more fully examine how various forms of human goodness—including love and care—flourish in urban settings. Likewise, our understandings of love and care will remain underdeveloped until we explore how these experiences emerge in the most densely populated, diverse, complex social structures in which people live. We will truly understand love when we understand how it is titrated through place, context, history, culture, religion, structures of power, identities, and a host of individual and relational factors. Urban centers give us powerful opportunities to know love.

Finding love is big business (a multi-billion–dollar industry), and artificial intelligence is transforming that business. Dating apps are increasingly outsourcing user matching to AI. Recent examples range from Overtone, an AI-powered matchmaking service started by the former chief executive officer of Hinge, to SciMatch, a dating app that aims to use AI to detect compatibility from a single selfie. Now people can talk to AI about their hopes, dreams, and ideal partner preferences and AI can scan the pool of dating app users to find candidates who fit those criteria exactly. AI can also track behavior in the app to learn what people really want (something behavioral scientists call revealed preferences). As a relationship scientist, I see both peril and promise in this new generation of dating apps. Despite long-heralded promises by dating app companies that their apps are based on relationship science, the truth is that there is no strong foundation of science upon which to build these matching algorithms—relationship scientists do not know what makes two particular people compatible with each other. Not yet, anyway. Sure, we know some of the factors that generally predict liking—physical attractiveness, positive personality traits, similarity, familiarity, reciprocity—but we have yet to predict with the kind of certainty dating apps might like to claim whether any two people will hit it off before they even meet. So if a robust science of compatibility doesn’t exist yet, what is the AI matching based on? One of the first AI dating apps, Fate, uses “similarity and reciprocity of personality,” according to an interview with the founder. That sounds reasonable, and research from Amanda Montoya and her colleagues looking across 313 different studies of similarity and attraction does suggest that similarity may predict attraction—before people meet. Once people experience even a brief interaction, similarity has much less, if anything, to do with how much two people like and are attracted to each other, as seen in an analysis of actual and perceived similarity of personality traits in potential partners, by Sarah Humberg and her colleagues. A systematic inquiry from my research lab, led by Annika From, also yielded little evidence that romantic couples are happier if partners are more similar to each other. What about “reciprocity of personality”? If this phrase refers to the idea that two people may have complementary personalities that meld well together, this theory of compatibility, unfortunately, has even less empirical support. Perhaps it is not too concerning that we lack a strong, agreed-upon scientific basis for predicting compatibility. After all, one of the great promises of AI is that it will learn as it goes, based not just on what a user says they want, but how they actually behave (who they swipe right on, whom they actually chat with after matching). Emerging evidence from different fields suggests that AI may be able to detect complex patterns that humans have been unable to discern. In the medical field, AI has been used to create more precise and accurate predictions that are aiding diagnosis and treatment. From weather prediction to fraud detection, the use of AI has enhanced forecasting. So maybe it can do the same for our love lives? If the problem has simply been that we have not been able to identify the complex patterns that make up human compatibility, then AI may, in fact, be the answer. If, however, the problem is that humans themselves are too complex, then AI may not get us any closer to being able to forecast love. What do I mean by this? Well, researchers have found that people will list their preferred partner traits before taking part in a speed dating event, only to have all those preferences go out the window when they actually interact with people. In my own work, I have discovered that people will dismiss another user’s dating app profile for reasons like “same name as my brother” and “bad haircut.” Yes, they will rule someone out as the possible love of their life because of something as irrelevant as their name (give them a nickname!) or hairstyle (arguably our most changeable physical characteristic). Why do I bring up these examples? They raise the question of whether AI even has the right information to rely on when gathering data based on people’s stated preferences or swiping behavior. So what data might AI need? In my lab, we hope to find some answers to this question with a series of studies that aim to combine dating-app data with real-life interactions. This project will allow us to see what chemistry looks like online and offline, uncover who has online chemistry that translates into real-world sparks, and identify the factors that make chemistry more likely between two people (if there are any meaningful predictors). The truth is, researchers at the forefront of this work do not always agree on whether forecasting love is even possible. While many relationship scientists have devoted their careers to testing the complex ways in which personalities and preferences interact to predict compatibility, they have arrived at different conclusions. Some researchers believe it will be possible to build an algorithm to predict compatibility; these researchers would likely see the promise in AI. On the other hand, there are those who have concluded that compatibility is a dynamic process that evolves as two people interact with each other and cannot be predicted ahead of time. For these researchers, finding love is less about the perfect match and more about being in the right place at the right time and then building love together through shared moments, from special nicknames and in-jokes to regular rituals just the two of you share. With the research projects in my lab, we hope to test these theories by having people meet different numbers of times. We’ve seen from our early work that pairing two random people together does not typically produce high levels of chemistry. That instant attraction seems to be relatively rare; but what if they met repeatedly? Could chemistry develop? Does it need some sort of initial spark to set it off, and can we create that spark with the right equipment, such as a well-placed question or activity that brings two people together? In other words, is chemistry possible with anyone if the right conditions were met, or is there some, as yet undiscovered, set of requirements for two people to be able to fall in love? I don’t know the answer to these questions yet, nor does the field of relationship science, although many have theories. I see AI as both an exciting tool that might help us answer this question as well as a technology that people looking for love should employ with caution. When used as a way to explore and test models of compatibility, AI may yield invaluable insights. But we must always remember that AI is trained with specific beliefs about what makes two people compatible. If researchers do not yet know what makes two people compatible, then letting AI do all the work to vet potential partners means you are only going to see the people that AI thinks are right for you, while likely remaining in the dark about how AI decided they’re a good match. This gap in knowledge may keep you from meeting someone unexpectedly compatible with you. An alternative approach to dating is to think about it as a time for exploration. Instead of only connecting with people who have similar facial structures or reciprocal personalities, try dating people with all kinds of different experiences, personalities, beliefs, and desires. If you go this route, Paul Eastwick offers a valuable piece of advice in his recent book Bonded by Evolution: for those who have serious dealbreakers (e.g., you know you want children, have strong religious views, or do not eat meat), go ahead and surround yourself with people who share those views. This will help you avoid facing potentially difficult lifestyle tradeoffs in the future if you do find yourself falling in love. But don’t eliminate someone just because of a bad haircut or a shared name. Instead, be open to exploring and then see for yourself who you connect with and who you do not. You might end up being surprised.

#9785. Am I Doing This Right? Teen Edition

📅 25 Oct 2026 | Category: other | Source
Parenting a teen can sometimes make you nostalgic for the early years, when you could still scoop up a frustrated toddler and redirect her with bubbles. In the teen years, sweet hugs can provoke eye rolls; a well-intentioned “How was your day?” may be misconstrued as meddling. That’s just your teens becoming independent. Decades of research finds that portraying adolescence as a period of “storm and stress” is not only misguided, it’s harmful. In fact, developmental science shows this time of life is better recast as “the breakthrough years.” According to research on over one thousand adolescents by Ellen Galinsky, author and president of Families and Work Institute, there are key messages that teens want to get through to adults: Teens want adults to seek to understand their development and listen and talk with them and not at them. They want grown-ups to stop stereotyping them and appreciate that they’re trying to understand themselves and their needs, too. Finally, teens want adults to know that they are drawn to learn skills like goal setting, perspective taking, collaborating, problem solving, and meaning making. None of this is easy, of course. Along the way, tough situations crop up, and it’s hard to know what to do. Here are common questions, with answers backed by scientists and their research. Q: My 16-year-old doesn’t seem to be motivated to do anything beyond hanging out with friends and playing video games. When I bring up the prospect of college, he says he doesn’t care. What should I do? A: As parents, we want to set our high schoolers up for success. But, at that age, young people are still trying to figure out who they are, what interests them, and what their future looks like. “Identity development and purpose formation are underway, but rarely fully formed by high school,” says Kendall Cotton Bronk, professor of psychology at Claremont Graduate University.  Not finding any answers yet is expected. “Parents need to remind themselves that where their child is at this moment in time isn’t necessarily where they will be in the future,” says Bronk. To help teens along, encourage them to try different activities at school and in the community, whether it’s, for instance, robotics, the debate team, chorus, improv, or the school paper. Leave it to them to select what to try. Don’t do it for them. Don’t judge their choices. “While it’s not appropriate for parents to be too directive, they can help their teenager reflect on what they liked and didn’t like and what felt meaningful and not so meaningful about their various experiences,” says Bronk. “Having someone to debrief with can help them deepen their reflection and more quickly identify a purposeful direction.” Research suggests that when teens are guided by their own interests, free of external pressures, they’re more likely to take charge of their own career development. But when parents are overly involved in their adolescents’ lives, says Bronk, young people don’t have an opportunity to cultivate a sense of agency, and this can make career development planning a big challenge. Instead of trying to manage their path, says Bronk, parents should simply “be curious.” Ask your child: What interests you? What feels satisfying for you? “This kind of questioning can scaffold and deepen their young person’s reflections, and reflection is a key component of figuring out what kind of path a young person wants to follow,” she says. “Usually, what holds their interest is something they’re good at and care about, and that allows them to make a meaningful difference in the broader world,” she explains. Taking advantage of volunteer opportunities can help young people see themselves as useful and discover careers that might interest them—for instance, helping out in a food pantry, animal shelter, a kids sports organization, or hospital. Encourage your teen to ask people he knows about their jobs, what they entail, and what they studied to get there. Finally, don’t assume that your child’s lack of direction is apathy. Whatever their interest is, “work with it, not against it,” says Bronk. “At the same time, be patient. It may just be the case that they haven’t figured it all out yet. Their timetable may not be the same as yours, but they will eventually get there. Remember, this isn’t a race.” Q: My 15-year-old seems to be living a separate life. He spends most of his time in his room and has dinner more often with his friends than with us. How can we encourage him to be a part of the family? A: Seeking independence (while still navigating being dependent on adults) is one of the primary tasks of adolescents as they transition to becoming more self-sufficient into early adulthood. “I try to get parents to understand that it’s not about them,” says Laurence Steinberg, author of You and Your Adolescent. “The parents didn’t do anything wrong; the child isn’t rejecting them. This is a sign they’re growing up normally. It’s partly wanting to make their own decisions and have their own life, but it’s also wanting emotional independence—to show that they are more than their parents’ kid.” Chances are a closed door doesn’t mean trouble. Just as most adults don’t want others listening in on their calls, teens don’t want that either. They’re not distancing themselves from you, but simply distancing themselves from being a child. “There’s nothing the parents can do to stop this from happening,” says Steinberg, a professor emeritus of psychology and neuroscience at Temple University. “And they shouldn’t try.” That said, spending time with family is also important. To suggest that, say something like, “We want to spend more time with you, but we also know that spending time with friends is important. What’s the best way to catch up with you?” By making the planning of it a collaborative process, your child is more likely to stay engaged. Research suggests that they may even find the activity more enjoyable. Only rarely is a shut bedroom door cause for concern—for instance, you notice other behavioral changes, such as a drop in grades, a dwindling interest in his usual in-person activities. If you suspect an issue, let your child know that you’re there for him, and that he can get professional help, too. One last note: If your child is spending most of his time in his room online, setting limits on its own may not be enough, says Jacqueline Nesi, author of the Techno Sapiens newsletter and associate professor of psychiatry and human behavior at Brown University. You’ll also want to discuss with them what you want for him and what he wants. The evidence also supports “co-viewing” or “co-using” technology with your kids, whether that’s a video game, TV, or an AI chatbot, says Nesi. Using tech together can spark conversation and help parents better understand their kids’ digital lives. That said, it’s still important for you to protect their sleep and to make sure that they live a life in person. Again, to get their buy-in, decide on screen-time parameters, and replace screen time with in-person activities he might enjoy, such cooking a favorite meal together or an outing with family and friends. Q: My 17-year-old daughter recently suffered a breakup. How can I help her feel better without intruding? A: Even if a relationship doesn’t look like a monogamous romantic relationship in adulthood (it may be short, or the partners may not talk to each other very much), these breakups can still be painful. So always take your teen’s romantic activities seriously, as well as the emotions they are experiencing following a breakup, says Emily Bibby, a researcher at the Relationship Development Center at Stony Brook University. Be compassionate, validating, and nonjudgmental. Let her know that she is having a normal reaction to a difficult situation. Allow space to grieve and vent. “Listen hard to what your child is thinking and feeling,” says Barry Farber, professor of psychology and education at Columbia University Teachers College. “Ask what they’ve learned from the relationship. Framing the relationship as a learning experience, as opposed to a failure, can help nurture resilience and personal growth, and can help her feel more in control and more hopeful about future relationships.” As Farber and his coauthors point out in a recent study, while time doesn’t necessarily “heal all wounds” (because memories and thoughts can linger), it almost always “softens the blow” when other strategies are also employed, such as talking with a trusted friend or family member. While the study involved mostly white, female adult subjects, the same principle generally applies across ages, genders, and ethnicities. Letting your child know that things will get better can help. Trying to convince a teen to do anything is difficult, says Farber, so it’s better to simply listen, and then only encourage as opposed to insisting or dictating. Encourage her to engage in activities she enjoys, whether it’s treating herself to an afternoon manicure, going to a concert, or spending time in nature like hiking and walking on the beach. Encourage her to avoid all contact with her ex, both in person and on social media. If one person believes it’s really over, a clean break is usually best, says Farber. Any surge of adrenaline that happens when you see your ex in any form is only temporary, and only makes it harder to get over the breakup. If sad feelings are impairing day-to-day life—she’s not seeing friends, she’s failing her classes, there’s a dramatic change in eating or sleeping—have her consider seeking help from a mental health professional. “Therapy normalizes the experience,” says Farber. “It helps people find a new narrative when they’re stuck.” Therapy also gives people an opportunity to share their feelings in a private space and learn evidence-based emotion-regulation skills to improve their mental health and functioning, says Bibby. If your teen is debilitatingly depressed or harming herself, then advise that she get professional help as soon as possible, such as from her school psychologist, her primary care doctor (for a referral), or a therapist you think highly of. To help their teen navigate relationships in general, parents can model healthy relationships with not just a partner, but also friends and family members. Teaching your teen effective communication skills and boundary setting early on, in the context of friendships and family relationships, can prove helpful once your teen starts dating, says Bibby, who recently coauthored a research review on romantic relationships and mental health in adolescents. And as for parents of teens who are just becoming interested in dating, it’s helpful to talk about what healthy relationships look like, emphasizing the importance of mutual respect. “Thinking through what they would do [in different dating scenarios] and how to navigate different challenges that may arise can build confidence when they start dating,” says Bibby. Q: My 14-year-old has found herself in a friend group that spends a lot of money on expensive clothing and electronics. We don’t approve of these teens and don’t share their consumerist values. How do we prevent our child from becoming materialistic, too? A: You can empower your teen to make thoughtful, confident choices about money, friendships, and the pressures they face. Try leading with connection rather than control: Talk openly about your family’s values, help them practice setting boundaries, and remind them they do not need to match their friends’ spending to belong or feel worthy. Expensive tastes and social media pressures can be hard to ignore, but your teen can learn to make choices that reflect what matters most to them. People turn to materialistic things to fill a void, but that’s only a temporary fix, says Lan Nguyen Chaplin, a professor of integrated marketing communications at Northwestern University, and an expert in children’s and teens’ consumer behavior. “They end up in a vicious cycle of wanting and needing more to fill the same void because material things cannot fill real voids.” In her research exploring materialism in a group of 12- to 18-year-olds, she reported that those with high self-esteem were less likely to have materialistic tendencies. “Parents and peers [are] a source of emotional support and psychological well-being,” she writes. “Supportive parents and peers boost adolescents’ self-esteem, which decreases their need to turn to material goods to develop positive self-perceptions.” To that end, start at home and help your kids feel good about themselves. Gratitude journals can be a good way to curb materialism, but, of course, asking a teen who is new to journaling to start one can feel like a homework writing assignment if it’s yet another thing they are required to do. Rather, approach it with a light touch. Model gratitude by sharing with your teen who and what you’re grateful for, keep a gratitude journal yourself, or ask them what the high point of their day is. It also helps to stop tying accomplishments to material things. So, instead of, say, rewarding a good report card with money or sneakers, celebrate the hard work that was put into achieving the good grades over a fun experience together like a movie night at home. Most of all, spend time with your teen in lighthearted and playful ways, no matter how short. Even a 10-minute car ride can be a good opportunity to connect with them, and also reinforce the good things they have going for them, Chaplin says.

When the Stanford Center on Longevity launched in 2007, founding director Laura Carstensen spent a lot of time correcting misperceptions. “The initial response people had was, ‘Like the Methuselah Foundation? You are going to extend life?’” Carstensen was clear that the center’s aim was not to increase lifespans—which had nearly doubled during the 20th century—but rather to build societies that accommodated that new and unprecedented reality. Another persistent misperception was that “longevity” is a euphemism for aging and that the Stanford Center on Longevity was a center on aging. As Carstensen noted often, “Longevity is not synonymous with aging. It is a metric of length.” The center’s interest is in the challenges and opportunities that longer lives present. While the center is keenly interested in older people, in Carstensen’s view, “longer lives have implications for every stage of life, and the roots of many problems faced at advanced ages have their roots much earlier in life.” The center’s mission is to use longer lives to improve quality of life at all ages. In 2019, under Carstensen’s leadership, the center launched the New Map of Life initiative, designed to rethink the human life course in response to skyrocketing life expectancies, and it has trained nearly 40 postdoctoral fellows, mentored by faculty affiliates spanning multiple disciplines, about the ways in which longevity is transforming societies, cultures, and economies. After nearly 20 years, Carstensen, the Fairleigh S. Dickinson Jr. Professor in Public Policy and a professor of psychology at Stanford, will step down this summer from her role at the Stanford Center on Longevity to pursue a new project alongside her research and teaching. I spoke with Carstensen about what she’s learned along the way and what’s next. This interview has been edited for length and clarity. Karen Breslau: What’s changed since the center’s early days? Laura Carstensen: I hope it’s clearer that the aging process has not changed much, if any, genetically over the last several thousand years. Rather, longevity—the length of life—increased, and that has enormous implications for nearly all aspects of our lives. The modifiability of aging, on the other hand, is also clear. Even though we’re essentially the same physiological beings that our ancestors were, we changed the world in ways that are allowing us to live healthier and longer. That’s why we are confident that we can build a world where the majority of people live well for a century. They’re thinking about all the factors that contribute to long, healthy, and engaged lives and literally programming them into the community. What’s really cool about the Queenstown Health District is that it can provide a proof of concept that social and physical designs can improve long life. So they flipped a switch and said, “OK, everybody’s putting in more money into their retirement account.” KB: If you had to give a grade to where the U.S. is in implementing the New Map of Life principles, could you take out your red pen? Unlike the Singapore government, which can require people to save more for retirement, for example, the U.S. cannot quickly implement such programs. I am discouraged that we are sleepwalking through this seismic demographic shift without investing more in the opportunity to rewrite the life scripts that have guided life and imagining ways that we could use them to improve life. It’s also discouraging that as longevity has entered public consciousness, much of what we hear about are pitches for questionable products that supposedly make people live longer. There are so many snake-oil salesmen in this space that it’s hard for consumers to distinguish what is evidence-based and what is not. KB: As you often say, transitions are a feature of longer lives. What’s happening next for you? LC: I’m going to focus more on research and teaching, but first I’m going on sabbatical and look forward to having more time for the things I love—my garden, being at home, sitting in the sun, going for walks. I want to take Pilates lessons. I’m going to take a break in my schedule to say, “How do I create a healthy lifestyle going forward and build the rest around it?” While I hopefully will have a more relaxed schedule, during my sabbatical I’ll be developing an online course called The Fundamentals of Longevity, which will tell the story of longevity and the potential to live longer, better lives, a change we really need in the world. The course will be accessible far beyond Stanford, so that we can help frame the conversation around longevity and help people appreciate the extraordinary promise of science, technology, and social change to improve life. There is a crisis on the horizon if we don’t change the way we live. We have the opportunity to change our lives for the better. We have opportunities no previous generation of humans in history has had, and we need to start implementing them. This story was originally published by Stanford Center on Longevity. When I’m producing a documentary, one of my favorite parts of the process is the moment when a bit of our footage sticks to me. Permanently. I never know when it will happen, but it always does. The sticky bit could be a snippet of dialogue or a quote from an interview. After watching the footage over and over again, the bit becomes part of me. Often it’s a funny line—a perfect, punchy distillation of the truth that gets adopted as an inside joke among the team of producers and editors. We repeat it like a collective spell that buoys us when we’re sinking under the weight of the project’s heavy subject matter. Sometimes the thing that sticks is not funny at all. Sometimes it’s a splinter, a cutting insight that I would have rather avoided. Or it’s a solidly true, heartfelt moment that sits like a smooth stone in my pocket. The touchstone grounds and guides me on the long journey of making a film. For the better part of the past year, I’ve been working as an executive producer on a series of short documentaries for the Greater Good Science Center called Connecting on Campus: Stories of Courage, Curiosity, and Empathy. The series features students at four universities across the country who are attempting to connect in some meaningful way with people who have different life experiences, political beliefs, or visions for the future. At Iliff School of Theology, master’s and Ph.D. students imagine a version of the United States that embodies their values, and then they share those visions with one another. As they listen to one another’s values, they discover where they align, where they diverge, and where empathy can keep them in relationship with each other even when they disagree. In a two-part story set at Bowie State University and the University of Maryland, students from both campuses gather at an event hosted by the schools’ Social Justice Alliance, a first-of-its-kind collaboration between an HBCU (historically Black college or university) and a PWI (predominantly white institution). At Dickinson College, a deep friendship between the presidents of the Dickinson College Republicans and the Dickinson College Democrats creates the conditions for an unexpectedly collaborative relationship between the two clubs. Members of both clubs gather regularly and listen to each other’s thoughts about complex and often contentious political issues. Back when we were in the middle of editing these episodes with our collaborators at Anaconda Street Productions, I spent an afternoon reviewing footage from one of the shoots, and then I took my dog for a walk. On the walk, I found myself repeating a line from an interview. It stuck! It’s been one of those smooth stones in my pocket ever since. Of course, I’m not the only one who was changed by the experience of working on this series. To celebrate the release of Connecting on Campus, I invite you behind the scenes to meet my collaborators as we share some of the many things we learned as we helped tell these stories. 1. Your voice matters and everyone has a part to play It’s a pretty straightforward message, yet I hear it over and over in my head in the voice of Mrs. Dawn Collins. Your voice matters and everyone has a part to play. Mrs. Collins says these words during an interview in the first of our two episodes about Bowie State University (BSU) and the University of Maryland (UMD). Mrs. Collins and her husband, Mr. Richard Collins Jr., founded the BSU–UMD Social Justice Alliance after their son, Lt. Richard Collins III, a BSU student, was murdered on the UMD campus by a student in 2017. The Alliance brings together faculty, staff, and students to join the Collins’s commitment to “eradicating the ideology of intolerance, while confronting individuals and combatting systems that normalize indifference, justify injustice, perpetuate hate, and inflict violence.” Mrs. Collins’s words have stuck with me, in part, because they insist on every person’s dignity, power, and responsibility. Our voices matter, and we all have a role to play in eradicating the ideology of intolerance and in combatting systems that perpetuate hate and inflict violence. That’s a strong call to action. Mrs. Collins’s words have also stuck with me because they clearly articulate the heart of this documentary series. The students featured in this series offer us examples of what happens when people step into their power and accept the responsibility to understand themselves, understand each other (differences and all), and work together to build something new. That new thing could be a collaboration between clubs on campus or a vision for our future as a nation. Either way, these students know their voices matter and they have what it takes to practice doing this work together. It was so helpful to have Mrs. Collins’s voice in my head, keeping me anchored in this truth as I worked. — Kelly Rafferty 2. Small moments can lead to meaningful choices When we first imagined Connecting on Campus, we wanted to find places where students were actually practicing the skills and qualities that make connection across differences possible, and to make those moments visible.  As I spent time with these students and their stories, and as we shaped the episodes in the edit, I found myself thinking more and more about choice. Across the series, you see students staying curious when they could shut down, listening to understand someone else’s experience, sharing their own perspective while making space for someone else’s, or finding the courage to remain in a difficult conversation.   In our Dickinson College episode, that choice shows up in a moment that could have easily passed. Ryan Thies recounts meeting Matthew Craig at a Halloween party during his freshman year. Matthew was dressed as Donald Trump. Ryan could have seen that costume, made an assumption about who Matthew was, and walked away. Instead, he leaned in and got to know him. An unlikely connection became a friendship, and eventually the two chose to become roommates.    Ryan’s choice changed what became possible between them. That choice reflects the kind of empathy we talk about in our Bridging Differences work: the deliberate, and often difficult, choice to engage with someone else’s experience even when it feels unfamiliar or challenges our own worldview.   The choices look different in our Bowie State University and University of Maryland episodes—and the stakes are obviously very different, too. Here, the students and the Collins family are stepping into a history that is devastating: the murder of Lt. Richard Collins III on the University of Maryland campus and the work that emerged from it to confront hatred and bring students from the two campuses together.   Here, courage looks like choosing to show up and engage when discomfort, conflict, and fear might make it easier—and completely understandable—to withdraw. It’s the decision to say: This matters. I’ll attend. I’ll represent. I’ll listen. I’ll do my part.  Practices like “Listen with Empathy,” “Understand Their Values,” “Expand Your Views and Circles,” or “Perspective Giving and Getting” from our Bridging Differences in Higher Education Playbook can be learned.   But the choice to engage is one each of us has to make. My hope is that people watch these stories asking themselves, “What did that person do that made greater understanding possible, and where might I try that myself?”  — Juliana Tafur 3. Connecting with someone across a difference isn’t about erasing what separates us I came away thinking that “bridging” is sometimes misunderstood as compromise. Across all four videos, something more interesting emerges: You can build a relationship across a difference without necessarily resolving the difference itself. At Iliff School of Theology, students imagine very different versions of America without being asked to arrive at one shared vision. At Dickinson, Republicans and Democrats engage seriously with each other without abandoning their political beliefs. And at Bowie State University and the University of Maryland, students move beyond assumptions, allow themselves to be vulnerable, ask difficult questions, and really listen to people whose backgrounds and experiences differ from their own. The circumstances are very different, but the lesson is consistent: Bridging isn’t about erasing what separates us. It’s about developing the curiosity, trust, and courage to stay connected across those differences.  The series asks a harder and more useful question than “How do we get everyone to agree?” It asks how we live, learn, and build community with people who may never see the world exactly as we do. The answers aren’t presented as a formula. You get to watch people experiment with listening, vulnerability, curiosity, disagreement, and repair, then decide for yourself what actually works. — Omar Lugones 4. Disagreement can actually bring us closer together and create new opportunities In my own experiences in smaller, more intimate classroom settings, I often observe an echo-chamber effect when opinions are shared, especially when the class is discussing a controversial topic. I have noticed a general apprehension about “going first” or initiating the conversation. Speaking for myself, I am often afraid of offering the “wrong” opinion, meaning a perspective that could be interpreted as supporting a politically or socially unacceptable position and cause my peers to view me as an outsider. I do not necessarily think my peers are experiencing the exact same thought process, but I sense that they are also self-conscious about expressing a conflicting opinion. As a result, people may default to agreeing with whoever offers an opinion first. I thought it was remarkable that at Iliff School of Theology, Lindsay Ambler and Stephanie Nelson initially disagree with one another, and that moment opens space for others to enter the conversation and offer their own perspectives. By disagreeing respectfully, they demonstrate how disagreement can produce a deeper understanding of an issue than a conversation built only around agreement. Lindsay takes a strong position on the effects of religion based on her past experiences with Christian institutions. By sharing an extremely vulnerable personal story, she creates the conditions for others to share their experiences and, in turn, promotes a greater sense of empathy within the conversation. When Stephanie offers her own perspective on whether society can reach a state of perfection and equality, her response conflicts with Lindsay’s position. #9782. You Might Be Experiencing Rustout, Not Burnout, at Work

📅 25 Oct 2026 | Category: other | Source
Some forms of distress at work are easy to recognize because they interrupt someone’s daily performance and the work of those around them: For example, a person on your team becomes visibly exhausted, they are irritable, or they’re frequently absent. But other forms of distress are harder to see precisely because the work continues uninterrupted. It’s common to talk about burnout at work and to recognize it in others and in ourselves. But a new paper of mine with David S. Smith examines a less visible and less understood form of disengagement at work: rustout. Rustout, as we studied it, often involves being underutilized, intellectually stagnating, and feeling a deep-seated sense of unfulfillment. It can arise when people feel their work no longer offers enough meaning or challenge to keep them intellectually and creatively engaged. But because it’s different from burnout, the solutions are also different—both for individuals and for organizations. Rustout vs. burnout I’ve presented this research at numerous conferences, and each time I have been approached by professionals who say, “That’s what I’ve been going through, but I never had a name for it until now.” These people, young and older, are committed to their work, they are hardworking, and they haven’t stopped caring. It is often the case that they care very much, and they meet expectations set before them. They are often the ones who are called upon because they are reliable and highly conscientious. If you look around your workplace, you probably know many people who fit this bill. The problem is not a loss of effort on the part of the individual; it is a breakdown in the relationship between effort and meaningful work. From my study of this topic, rustout is really about the decline in professional vitality, when a person’s capacities are not used in ways that feel meaningful to them. In our research, participants described experiences shaped by administrative overload, reduced autonomy, and a misalignment between their professional aspirations and assigned tasks. Many people highlighted feeling professionally stuck: “Sadly, the elements I love about the job . . . are often overshadowed by the other administrative aspects of the role,” “I feel like I’m feeding the machine,” and “I’m a square peg in a round hole.” They highlighted a sense that the work they were doing no longer drew sufficiently on their talents: “The better you are at it, the more of the administrative side of the job you get. It’s lacking opportunities to be creative.” Rustout can be quite psychologically disorienting. From the outside, a person may appear successful and competent. There is no question about their productivity; they meet set targets, but could do so “with [their] eyes closed.” However, they may feel increasingly detached from the work that once animated them. Their distress isn’t dramatic; it’s a slow process of psychological erosion. In many ways, rustout overlaps with burnout, but they are not the same. Burnout involves prolonged negative stress that isn’t adequately managed. It’s about too much—working too much, caring too much, too much pressure, and too little time to recuperate. In contrast, rustout is associated with too little positive stress or challenge over time, too little stimulation, and limited opportunities for individual growth. However, while burnout is part of organizations’ mental health literacy, rustout rarely gets a mention. And this is problematic—both for the individual and the organization. “I often feel I have produced nothing at the end of the week, and there is no sense of development,” one person said. Some will stay in their roles out of a sense of duty (and for other practical reasons like salary or seniority), but others will choose to leave. Another said, “I am functioning at my job, but I’m not thriving, and I am looking elsewhere.” Why don’t we talk about rustout? Rustout often hides behind competence, and the reliable person is often given more of the same work. The person who does not complain is assumed to be coping and doing OK. The person who stabilizes others and is a good team player is rarely asked whether they themselves feel developed or challenged. One person said, “I have no experience of ever having a conversation with anyone around it. No line manager ever named it, it has never been mentioned in a communal setting or in a one-to-one setting. . . . No one has ever asked, ‘Do you feel fulfilled or do you feel you have opportunities to use your skills and talents in a meaningful way?’” It’s easy to see how doing overtime and being dependable can become a trap. Eventually, the employee becomes valued less for what they might become and more for what they can continue to absorb—they won’t “rock the boat” by opening the can of worms that is their dissatisfaction. “You put up, and shut up,” one person said. Meanwhile, many workplaces are organized to detect individual or team shortcomings (or failure) more readily than stagnation. If someone stops performing, the problem becomes visible. However, if someone continues to perform while becoming inwardly disengaged, the organization may see no problem at all. And if anyone notices their dissatisfaction, they may not openly discuss it, as another of my interviewees reported. “It suits everyone not to talk about it; when everyone is working and doing their jobs—then don’t disturb anything. Everything is cosy.” How rustout arises Participants experiencing rustout described barriers that limited their ability to use their strengths or pursue professional growth, which are particularly important for having a sense of meaning and fulfillment at work. For many people, their working life gives them a sense of identity and purpose, but when work no longer offers that sense of meaning, their relationship with the job can become fractured or feel “off.” In some cases, they can’t put their finger on what is happening, and that might be why I often hear after presentations, “I never had a name for it.” Naming rustout won’t solve the issue, but it can help the person to understand why they feel underutilized or dissatisfied. Naming rustout can help people understand this isn’t a personal failure but an experience that is shaped by the design or conditions of work (much like burnout). It is also shaped by the culture of work, particularly when there is a resistance to change or a failure to see the value in helping people to flourish. From my discussions with participants and colleagues about rustout, it is clear that it isn’t a pre-retirement phenomenon. It seems to arise for those who are experienced, perhaps mid-career, and those who have been in their role for a long time. One participant explained, “I have been doing [this role] for years. There must be better ways to organize things to ensure the expertise of experienced staff is used in more effective ways.” Others said, “It’s the seven-, eight-, nine-year mark when there isn’t an opportunity to be stimulated,” and “It’s the seven-year itch, you’ve been doing something creative and then you reach a point where you are doing the same thing on repeat.” In such ways, we can’t dismiss rustout as a consequence of aging in the workplace. It can happen whenever people are pigeonholed into a system that no longer offers them meaningful development. Although some repetition and routine are an expected part of every job, there should also be a sense that your abilities and talents matter in your role. That there is a future and something exciting to work toward. What to do if you are rusting out The language of burnout has helped many people name distress that had previously been normalized or dismissed. It has also helped organizations take workplace well-being more seriously. But any dominant mental health vocabulary can become limiting when it starts to stand in for the whole field of experience. If burnout is the only category available for occupational distress, then recovery is often imagined in terms of rest and resilience. They may be necessary, but they are not always sufficient. A person experiencing rustout may not simply need less work; they may need different work: work that restores their sense of intellectual engagement and curiosity. As Maya Angelou once said, “We aren’t designed to be static”—that could apply to all aspects of life, including the workplace. If you are experiencing rustout, it would be good to do an inventory of what is happening for you. Do you feel fulfilled in your role? Do you have opportunities to use your talents? Do you gain some sense of meaning from your role? Are there ways to seek renewal in your work? What might this look like, and can you have this conversation with your line manager or boss? For organizations, the questions are also direct. Look around your team. Are you giving your most dependable employees opportunities for development? Do you continually lean on their “safe pair of hands” because it is easier than asking someone else to learn the ropes? Are you willing to put the conversation about professional dissatisfaction on the table? How can you help your team to flourish? That is the unsettling truth at the heart of the rustout. A person can be conscientious yet disengaged, reliable yet underused, competent but unfulfilled. They can be good at their job and still feel themselves receding from it. The silence around rustout allows it to thrive, which is why I often hear the same response after conference presentations: “I thought it was just me.” I think there is a particular loneliness in that sentence, the loneliness of continuing to do the work, while privately wondering why it no longer feels like yours.

#9780. How Love Saves Lives

📅 25 Oct 2026 | Category: other | Source
When someone takes his own life, the grief of parents, lovers, children, and friends can be unspeakable. At a memorial for one young suicide I attended a few years ago, the father of the deceased could barely walk to the altar to light a candle. Everyone was crying. How could this talented, beautiful young person not know she’d be terribly, horribly missed? Most experts attribute suicide to severe mental illness caused by a brain injury or genetic predisposition, or due to some other, as yet unknown, physical or chemical trigger. Severe depression, bipolar disorder, and schizophrenia are all inordinately common among people who die by suicide. Severe mental illnesses are no doubt responsible for many, if not most, of the eight hundred thousand or so suicides that occur around the world each year, including, perhaps, that young person’s whose memorial I attended. My book, Why Live: How Suicide Becomes an Epidemic, isn’t primarily about such cases—or about how to prevent suicide. Such a book would have detailed sections about psychotherapy, restrictions on firearms, safety barriers for bridges, and other measures that really do save lives. Instead, my aim was to explore why some people want to take their own lives in the first place and how that desire might be reduced, in both individuals and communities. Having spent the past few years reading about suicide, speaking with scores of people who’ve experienced suicidal feelings, and having experienced them myself from time to time, I’ve come to understand that what suicidal people feel is the indifference, not of the universe, but of those closest to them. They sense that their own feelings don’t matter to the people they care most about. These experiences are backed up by studies showing that the main reason suicidal people want to die is because they think no one cares about them. Some suicides even seem to believe they’re doing everyone a favor by taking their own lives. Psychologist Thomas Joiner, who’s been studying suicide for thirty years, calls it a force of nature that distorts people’s minds so they become convinced that they are a burden to others and no one wants them around. In study after study, the most common trigger for suicide is a perceived rupture in a close relationship, either with a lover, parent, or other close relative, that seems irreparable to the suicidal person. Given that suicide seems driven by fraught or broken connections, it’s perhaps not surprising that I also discovered that suicide can spread within social groups like a disease. The suicide rate of particular groups can jump—sometimes increasing three- or four-fold or more—in a short time, meaning it behaves like an epidemic, like malaria during the rainy season or flu in the fall. Suicide epidemics unfold more slowly than these microbial plagues—over years or decades, not weeks or months—but they happen far too quickly to result from genetic changes, and affect far too many people to be explained away as spontaneous cases of biologically caused mental illness or brain injury. The causes therefore must have something to do with the changing social environment in which these people live. Suicide epidemics have occurred on virtually every continent. Between the 1820s and World War I, suicides nearly tripled in Belgium, Italy, and France, and they quadrupled in Austria. In 2014, young veterans of the Iraq and Afghan wars were roughly five times as likely as same-aged civilians to take their own lives. The suicide rate doubled among middle-aged Russians after the collapse of Soviet communism in the early 1990s. It quadrupled in white middle-aged working-class Americans after deindustrialization commenced in the 1970s. Between 1980 and 2003, suicide among Inuit youths in the Canadian Arctic soared from almost zero to a rate 10 times higher than that of the white majority Canadian population. What’s scary about this is that it means suicide could come for any of us. The very existence of suicide epidemics proves that people with suicidal feelings aren’t a species apart. Anyone can feel suicidal under certain conditions—and the key to stopping suicide epidemics is to change those conditions. Why suicide spreads I teach and write about public health and have long been fascinated by epidemics because they always tell a story. In most literary depictions of epidemics, such as Albert Camus’s The Plague or Daniel Defoe’s A Journal of the Plague Year, they seem to strike from nowhere, but this isn’t how epidemics typically behave. Individual cases of disease are hard to predict. Some of us will become ill tomorrow, but there’s no telling who. Even those who take risks may well escape the consequences. What might cause a suicide epidemic? First, it’s important to understand what doesn’t. Suicide isn’t more common during wars or where people are poorer or have recently experienced some environmental disaster like a flood, earthquake, or wildfire. Suicide was common among African slaves, especially on the ships en route to America and when they first arrived, but despite segregation, police brutality, mass incarceration, and other outrages, the suicide rate of African Americans has been generally lower than that of U.S. whites since record-keeping began in the 1930s. What seems to matter is whether people believe someone, somewhere, really cares about and understands them. The mental pain that drives suicide is really the flip side of love, says Israeli neuroscientist Yoram Yovell. “It probably evolved along with motherhood. . . . Inherent in us are very strong emotional forces that function to keep mothers and babies together. When they get too far from each other, they feel mental pain,” which we experience when we search for love. This is why “so many children’s stories are about a boy looking for his lost mother.” Evidence supports Yovell’s theory. A 1999 study found that some 78% of high lethality suicide attempters had recently experienced the loss of a close personal relationship, and suicide notes often express love for those left behind. In 2012, Zimri Yaseen and his colleagues reported that suicidal people interviewed in emergency rooms were especially likely to report that in the three preceding days, they’d felt “unusually intense or deep feelings of love,” which they presumably also felt were not reciprocated. But why would large groups of people—from the islands of Micronesia to the Arctic Circle, from the cities of post-communist Russia to the towns of contemporary America—suddenly begin questioning their most intimate attachments? How does love suddenly disappear—or seem to—from the lives of thousands of people? In my book, Why Live, I argue that it has to do with how we internalize socioeconomic change. Fragmenting societies, isolated individuals The first well-researched suicide epidemic occurred across Western Europe during the 19th century. Intellectuals debated the causes as we debate the vicissitudes of the stock market. Rising suicide rates were attributed to socialism, atmospheric moisture, the vice of masturbation, and the Romantic era inclination to preserve—through death—the passions of youth against the onslaught of Bourgeois boredom brought on by industrialization and urban life. Doctors even searched, in vain, for a “suicidogenic” organ in the cadavers of people who’d died by suicide. But others blamed society, that collective property of human groups that shapes our thinking and behavior. As a Jew growing up in France amid increasing anti-Semitism, Émile Durkheim had seen how shared values and beliefs buffered his people against the effects of discrimination and how anti-Semitism tightened bonds among them. He went on to become one of the founders of sociology, which is the study of those bonds: what holds us together, what drives us apart, and what happens as a result. In his 1897 book, Suicide, Durkheim theorized that suicides increase wherever society is fragmenting and people are growing more detached from one another: where divorce rates are higher, for example, and where Protestants, with their individualistic culture and aloofness from collective ritual, outnumber Catholics. He likened it to entropy—the universal tendency toward chaos and disorder at the atomic level discovered by physicists earlier in the century. Appearing on the eve of humanity’s, as yet, bloodiest century, Durkheim’s book seemed to foreshadow the trouble to come. What is it about fragmenting societies that causes mental pain? Durkheim was vague, even a little loopy, when it came to explaining this. At the time, psychologists were just discovering the unconscious, which they explored through studies of hypnosis and hallucinations. Durkheim followed this research closely, and believed that values and meanings flowed through populations like electrical currents, as if societies had what scholars Laurent Mucchielli and Marc Renneville refer to as a “collective soul.” These currents could change people’s behavior through unconscious mechanisms, just as changes in temperature and pressure change the behavior of molecules. Many late-19th-century scientists believed that our brains generated signals analogous to radio waves. Alexander Graham Bell, Nikola Tesla, and James Clerk Maxwell even wired up helmets and other gizmos to try to measure them. Mark Twain told Harper’s Monthly that thoughts could shoot from brain to brain, without their owners having to open their mouths to communicate. Likewise, Durkheim maintained that whatever suicidal people said about their feelings were just expressions of resolutions planted in their minds by the vibes of a disintegrating society. The brain-wave theory eventually fell out of fashion. In the 1930s, one of Durkheim’s protégés came closer to explaining the psychological effects of social fragmentation. Maurice Halbwachs taught at the University of Strasbourg from 1919 until his death in a Nazi concentration camp in 1945. His 1930 monograph The Causes of Suicide identified an existential loneliness that, if not entirely new, flourished in the growing towns and cities of 19th-century Europe and the countryside they engulfed. According to Halbwachs, cities create opportunities for social and economic advancement, but they also create the conditions for exploitative relationships, swindles, romantic betrayals, fragile friendships, broken families, unstable jobs, and many other sources of despair. As European cities grew, so did a new class of people who were particularly prone to suicide, including abandoned lovers, lonely widowers, workers who’d lost their jobs, nobles shamed by some dishonorable act, gamblers who couldn’t pay their debts, merchants who’d ruined themselves, heirs who’d squandered their fortunes, and others who’d suffered a common bitter fate: sudden exclusion from a cherished social community. As Halwachs writes: They all see their social level lowered. They are to a certain extent declassed. But what does it mean to be declassed? It is to pass from a group that one knows, that esteems you, to another that ignores you and whose appreciation one has no reason to hold. One then feels a void opening around oneself. Those who once surrounded you, with whom you had so many ideas in common, so many prejudices in common, with whom so many affinities brought you together because you found yourself in them as they found yourself in you, suddenly move away. You disappear from their concerns and their memory. Those among whom you find yourself understand neither your disorientation, nor your nostalgia and your regrets. Detached from the group by a sudden shock, you are incapable, or at least you believe yourself incapable, of ever finding in another any support, nor anything to replace what you have lost. In the midst of the 19th-century suicide crisis, the very meaning of the word “lonely” changed, from an adjective typically used to describe empty places and people who stood alone before God to one with the emotional and psychological connotations it has for us today. Suicidal despair, Halbwachs wrote, had been rarer in the past because traditional rural life was simpler and more predictable: We lived on the spot . . . adapted to each other, knowing each other too well to be frequently exposed to the clashes that occur when we move from one place, one situation, one profession, to another. Business carried less risk. Ambitions were less aroused, humiliations rarer. We thought and felt in common. Sorrows and troubles, instead of concentrating within the consciousness of this or that individual, dispersed and died out within the group. What Halbwachs was describing was the transition from what German sociologist Ferdinand Tönnies called Gemeinschaft, or communities based on trust, mutual respect, and sharing of whatever material goods one had, to Gesellschaft, societies based largely on market transactions and political calculation in which ties of friendship and loyalty are relegated to private family life, and may not even reliably be found there. As far as I’ve been able to determine, wherever that transition has occurred, a suicide epidemic has followed swiftly behind. But there is another ingredient, too. As villagers shifted away from subsistence and small-scale farming to wage labor and trade, their intimate relationships became both more fragile and more precious. The extended family, which once formed a social mesh that supported everyone, waned in significance, and walls rose up around increasingly private and nuclearized families, usually dominated by a single, generally male breadwinner, upon whom everyone else in the family depended. If you felt your family didn’t respect and care about you, you were in a new sort of trouble, not just because the community no longer offered you a livelihood, but because your whole identity was at stake. Love and belonging Modern capitalist society creates, in all of us, something of the double consciousness described by the great African American sociologist W. E. B. DuBois. When a modern person looks at a stranger, he wonders not only, as his villager ancestors did, “What can I do to help this person? And is he the kind of person who will help me?” but “What does that stranger see when he looks at me? What essence does he detect? A beauty or a beast? A success or a failure? A respectable person or a fool?” The answer sometimes comes as a disappointment. In traditionally cohesive societies, this mattered less, because a person’s worth was measured by what he did for others, and everyone was capable of contributing something. You might not be the best hunter, fisherman, storyteller, or drummer; you might not be the best grower of breadfruit or builder of igloos; but you still mattered. Only the greedy and selfish were looked down upon and excluded. In modern societies, generosity still counts, but for far less than it used to. If others find us wanting—because of our looks, talents, graces, bank accounts, intelligence, or for whatever reason—we are alone with it, and must make sense of it, somehow. For most of us, what makes modern life, with all its loneliness and insecurity, bearable are close relationships with people we count on to see and accept us for who we really are—whatever our flaws. Romantic rejection is exquisitely painful because we feel that that special quality within us, the thing that makes us lovable—and everyone has something—has either been unrecognized or, worse, seen and rejected. The pain of rejection by a parent or other close relative is even worse because it can make us feel as though no one could ever understand and sympathize with us. After all, they know us best of all. In the absence of an abstract sense of group belonging, interpersonal love, especially between parents and children and romantic partners, has become the main source of our psychological resilience, our ability to withstand the disappointments encountered in the outside world, just as the mutually reinforcing group gave strength to our ancestors fighting for survival on the plains, tundras, beaches, and forests of the past. Love is not just a feeling; it’s also a culture, which developed fairly recently, first in Western societies, and then around the world on the heels of capitalism, modernity, and individualism. It’s no coincidence, the social philosopher Erich Fromm pointed out, that as modern ways of life displaced the traditional, cohesive village and clan, love poems, songs, and stories came to occupy the center of Western culture, displacing archaic tales of heroism and godliness. It’s not that love was absent from the art of the ancient past, but as often as not, it was seen as a cause of calamity and human folly. Think of Helen of Troy, Dido and Aeneas, Adam and Eve. Then, more or less in lockstep with commercial culture and urbanization, love came to be seen as a force superior to the gods or God or honor or historic destiny. The behavior of parents and spouses changed, too. Until the 1800s, childrearing in England, for example, was seen as an exercise less in nurturing than in training for adulthood. Men who were fed up with their wives could sell them at markets or abandon them and marry someone else without divorcing. Infants who died weren’t even listed in parish registers. Only in the 19th century did heartfelt observations of sons and daughters begin appearing regularly in the diaries of American and English mothers. Regrowing community With the growth of markets and formal state institutions, people came to rely less on kinship networks for social welfare and trade and the family gradually shifted from an economic enterprise to a child-centered haven in a heartless world. The 1700s saw the first toy shops, women’s magazines, popular child care manuals, and family portraits. Christian churches, which had previously stressed family discipline and sometimes even the virtues of wife-beating, now promoted ideals of conjugal and parental love. Today, birthday parties, children’s allowances, and Valentine’s Day cards are symbols we use to assure each other that our most important relationships are real. Meanwhile, parenting manuals, happily-ever-after fairy tales, Hollywood movies, and the novels of Jane Austen, George Eliot, Charles Dickens, Barbara Cartland, Colleen Hoover, and countless others illustrate the enactment of love according to Western norms. Premodern Europeans, as well as Indigenous groups the world over, didn’t need this culture because in the mutual aid communities of bygone days, you knew who you were and what you needed to do to gain the esteem of others: You had to pitch in for the group. You helped others and they helped you. That was love, and according to accounts of anthropologists, it was everywhere, as ubiquitous as air. But when these groups modernized, in some cases very rapidly under the forces of capitalism and colonialism, the rules changed. Now it was unclear what the common goals were and what others were looking for in us. If they didn’t like us, there wasn’t a lot we could do about it. If the people we loved—our romantic partners, parents, and children—felt that way, it was intolerably painful because our hearts had nowhere to turn. Every rejection could now elicit devastating feelings of aloneness. “Who am I?” we are left to wonder. “Am I worth anything at all to anyone?” Signifiers of love—birthday cards and so on—aren’t enough. We need the confidence that our own feelings are understood. Some people, either because of the distortions of mental illness or because they really are lost and alone, simply don’t have it. But all is not lost. As I argue in Why Live, communities can regrow, just like decimated forests. Why it matters: What grief looks like Research suggest that those with internalized stigma of chronic illness are more preoccupied with how their illness detracts from their view of themselves, and also have a greater tendency to overlook positive aspects of life with a chronic illness. Later analysis found that what predicted whether a male took on that role was not the infant’s sex, and not relatedness, but the strength of the grooming bond he’d shared with the mother before she died. Touch carries power in humans, as well. Research across five European cultures found that the area of the body you’ll let someone touch maps almost directly onto the strength of your emotional bond with them. There is cultural variation. British people are notably more guarded about casual contact than many of their European neighbours, and touch between men in many Western contexts carries particular social weight. Touch conveys intimacy and trust. Yet holding someone’s hand or shoulder can also be used to intimidate or harass. We could learn from primates to stop withholding touch where it would help most and back down from reaching for it where it shouldn’t be used at all. One key area where more touch helps is sports. A study of all 30 NBA teams during the 2008–09 season found that the teams who touched more early in the season, through fist bumps, chest bumps, hugs, and high fives, went on to perform better and play more cooperatively later, even after controlling for player salaries and pre-season expectations. In women’s college basketball, players who received more physical contact from teammates after missing their first free throw were more likely to convert the second. After five straight shootout losses, England’s governing body in football, the FA, spent 18 months rebuilding England’s approach to penalties under Gareth Southgate, not just the technical side. Players were coached on how to treat each other after a miss. They learned to place an arm around the shoulder rather than leaving each other isolated. Celebrating wildly after a scored kick became a deliberate technique for steadying whoever was up next. Locker-room culture has never been shy about physical contact between teammates. Now we know that the contact might be doing real work. To be sure, humans show exceptional diversity in our cultures and behavior, for better and worse. But when humans are violent or cruel, people call it primal, or animalistic. We emphasize our altruism as unique to our species. That’s unfair to animals. Humans have bullied others until they took their own lives. We’ve wiped out entire tribes and cultures. And beyond targeting other people, even now, humans are removing protections for endangered keystone species that ecosystems depend upon to thrive. But we don’t need to accept the drive to dominate as inevitable. If the apes show anything, it’s how much of that drive gets managed and set aside day to day, and how much depends on the community you find yourself in. We are a part of the natural world like all other animals, needing to balance our cooperative and competitive urges. And the evidence suggests we sit at the better end of that range far more often than we might think. Where to start Now that we know how central touch and cooperation are to primates and human nature, I hope we can stop being pessimistic when we predict others’ behavior and motives. A study published in Science this June, covering 125 countries and over 100,000 people, found that 69% of us cooperate with a stranger when given the choice. Asked to guess, people put it at 47%. That pessimism showed up in 124 of the 125 countries surveyed. This matters in the grander scheme of things because those of us who expect others to cooperate are far more likely to cooperate also. When researchers simply told participants that most people worldwide see climate change as a threat, belief in others’ willingness to cooperate ticked up, and actual cooperation followed it, slightly, but in the right direction. Building on the lessons of primate relationships is easier than it seems. Seek simple interactions. One of the main ways our primate cousins build affiliative relationships is spending time sitting in close proximity. They may groom, or play, or even sit in contact. More often than not they just like to hang out near their close partners. Just being close to one another is meaningful. Try a car journey or a five-a-side game. Build trust and relationship through activities where nobody has to look anyone in the eye. If a mate isn’t much of a talker, doing something alongside him might get further than sitting him down and asking how he’s doing. Reach out before a crisis, not after. Great apes exchange most of their reassurance in the tense minutes before competition, not the wreckage afterward. Reconciliation and comforting are commonplace in humans and primates, but it’s much easier to prevent escalation than it is to smooth things over after a fight. Don’t wait to be the one who’s approached. That 22-point gap between how we actually cooperate and what we think everyone else will do shows the power of offering an invitation to relationship. Most of us are waiting for someone else to go first. Influencers selling young men and boys on their primal nature fly in the face of the science and what nature actually looks like. Force and competition are real parts of biology, and something we humans have to manage in our everyday lives. But, if there’s one thing I’ve learned from my many years studying our closest relatives, it’s that touch, care, cooperation, and even love are the most instinctive natural components of being part of the primate family.

#9775. 17 Ideas From Educators to Start the School Year With Joy

📅 25 Oct 2026 | Category: other | Source
Joy! What a beautiful way to start the school year, not only because it feels good, but also because science shows that joy is linked closely to learning, as well as individual and collective flourishing. We asked Greater Good Education newsletter readers to share a favorite strategy for bringing joy into their school or classroom at the beginning of the year. The responses did not disappoint! Here are a few that span ages, time commitments, and subject areas. Dance party Friday Laura M. (Newark, DE): “Dancing it out” is a strategy that has been shown to reduce stress, release held feelings, and encourage kids to be in the moment. Providing time for activities like dancing is often seen as an “extra.” Building this activity into our weekly schedule has become a favorite stress-relieving activity—adding tremendous joy and connection between children and children and children and teachers. Related Greater Good in Education (GGiE) practices: The World Is a Dance; Getting to Know Each Other Through Dance; We Are Better Dancing Together Finding joy in our lives Josh (New Haven, CT): Every Monday morning meeting, students greet each other by asking, “What brought you joy (made you smile/laugh) over the weekend?” Laura H.: At the end of the day, with second graders, we share the activity from our day that brought the most joy. If I wasn’t there for it (i.e., recess, music, art, PE), I ask them to include details. Related GGiE practices: Noticing Kindness; Giving Thanks; Looking for Gratitude in School; Sharing Gratitude Stories Enchanting joy through surf, science, and storytelling Emi (Lobitos, Peru): Identifying forms of enchantment—I use this approach in our Women & Water Program. It’s an all-girls (ages 3–16) surf, science, and storytelling program based in Lobitos, a tiny fishing village in Northern Peru. We use “enchantment” as a way for young girls to foster their curiosity, creativity, and care for themselves, peers, community, and the coastal environment. We do this through photography (taking “macro” photos of small, wondrous things found in their environment, like a flower petal, a beetle, etc.). Or we do this in surfing—the girls notice the “awe” of riding the energy of a leftover storm until it crashes into the sand. These girls also made this song after an oil spill that rocked our community about two years ago. Related GGiE practices: Meaningful Photos for Students; Mindfully Finding Awe in Nature Sidewalk messages of joy Angela (Pleasant Hill, CA): I am a school counselor and with my students who experience anxiety we use sidewalk chalk to write positive welcoming messages and pictures in front of our school. It makes others feel welcomed and helps my students feel connected to our school. They even told me it helps get them to school the first couple of days knowing they would see their chalk messages. Related GGiE practices: Designing the Classroom to Promote Kindness; Kindness Buddy; Awe-Inspiring Affirmations “Would you rather” joy Lorien (St. Louis, MO): Have a “Would you rather…?” question on the whiteboard at the start of class—the funnier the better. Students put a checkmark next to their choice. It’s a fun way to welcome the “whole student self” into the classroom, not just their “learner selves.” Sometimes we talk about it, but often, we don’t. Related GGiE practice: Superstar Engineering joy through perseverence Pam (Muscogee County, GA): To kick off the year, my eighth graders design and build a cardboard maze that guides three different rolling spheres from start to finish. Working in teams of four, students sketch a plan, build it with cardboard and hot glue, test it, and revise their design. Nothing works perfectly the first time. Students quickly discover that failure is a useful part of the design process. By the end of the week, teams are cheering when a ping pong ball, golf ball, or rubber ball finally makes it all the way through, and that joy comes from earning it through real trial and error. It sets the tone for the whole year: Engineering is a process of building, testing, and improving. Related GGiE practice: Four 4s Discovering joy in little wins Kimberly (Williamsport, PA): Something that I introduce during the first week of classes is our 3 Good Things or 3 Little Wins Padlet. Each week students post about three good things or little wins they had during the week. Before the first post, we discuss why it is important to celebrate these things, no matter how small (e.g., a good cup of coffee!). We also discuss the impact that reflecting on these things has on our physical and psychological well-being. Each week I also post my wins for the week and comment on student posts as well. It creates a wonderful community! Related GGIE practices: Three Good Things for Students; Gratitude Journals Our favorite songs joy Jonah (Duxbury, VT): As a high school English teacher, the first writing assignment I give to students asks them to choose a (clean) song that represents them and tell me why they feel connected to this song. I then make a playlist including all the students’ songs, and play it during work times or use it to play students’ walk-up song on special days like birthdays. Related GGiE practices: Creating Musical Playlists for the Classroom; Prosocial Music Hub (13 practices to bring music into the classroom) Cake-pop stick joy Joe (Bridgeport, CT): Students interact with each other as they navigate around the crowded classroom, connected only by cake-pop sticks balanced between each other using only their index finger. Fosters concentration, cooperation, joyful failures, interconnectedness, and intimacy without actually touching one another. Related GGiE practice: 1-2-3 Clap Geological joy David (San Antonio, TX): For my introductory geology class, I work with the students to develop rules for a “rock-rock-rock” (igneous-sedimentary-metamorphic) version of “rock-paper-scissors.” Students develop the necessary hand signals and the action verbs that say what beats what. Once we have the rules established, the students engage in a best two out of three to crown the class champion. Branded university swag is available for the winners, and everyone gets a candy treat for participating. Related GGiE practice: Making Science Meaningful Joyful lies Susan (St. Paul, MN): I ask students to tell the rest of the class their name and a ridiculous lie about themselves—the more absurd the better. There’s always good-natured laughter and encouragement. Related GGiE practice: Truth or Tale Laughing joy Harry (Hailey, ID): I work to end every “session” (class, administration meeting, faculty meeting, etc.) with laughter, whether incited by me or coming from someone else. That moment becomes the punctuation. Related GGiE practices: Share a Laugh; The SEL 3 Signature Practices The joy of contribution—seeing the good in others Paige (Seattle, WA): About the second or third week of classes (graduate and undergraduate students) we begin doing this ritual that we do a few times during the semester: “The joy of contribution…I see this in you!” Students take turns identifying something they have genuinely observed in another student in the cohort/class: “One leadership capacity I see developing in you is________, because I watched you ________.” The BECAUSE matters. Examples I have heard from pre-certified teachers and principals: “I see courage building in you because I have watched you ask questions the rest of us were afraid to ask.” OR, “I see you really owning belongingness as you consistently notice who is outside the conversation and what is happening around us as you read the room.” Related GGiE practices: I See You. Everyone Matters; Behind Your Back What joy looks like Lorien (St. Louis, MO): I use a guided visualization to prompt students to think about what joyful teaching, joyful learning, and joyful interactions look like, sound like, and feel like. It makes what we’re doing much more explicit, and it helps me know what they want from me and vice versa. Related GGiE practice: Designing the Classroom to Promote Kindness Drawing for joy Angela (McMinnville, OR): A non-dominant hand drawing activity that gets us all out of our “perfection” thinking and into being learners…and laughing. Related GGiE practice: Drawing as a Way to Manage Emotions Joyful greetings Milton (San Diego, CA): As the associate principal, I stand out in front of the school in the morning with a BIG SMILE sign on and greet the students with a “Good morning & have a great day!” Some students like a fist bump or handshake as well. Related GGiE practice: Making Families Feel Welcome Closing the day with joy Donna (Arkansas): “JOY” is the grounding rhythm that closes every day, honoring the joyful moments that JUST happened, recognizing community attunement through something an OTHER person did, and celebrating internal self-efficacy via something YOU did. Each person is free to select whichever letter resonates with them to share. We pass a rainstick around the circle; once it is turned to let its gentle cascade settle into silence, the person holding it speaks. This tactile ritual and quiet sound not only brings a profound sense of calm to the transition from classroom to home, but it also serves as a gentle reminder to carry those reflections outward. J—Just happened (joyful moments): This includes moments of awe through visual art design, the natural world, or quiet classroom breakthroughs. Prompt: “What is a small moment of joy or beauty that just happened today—maybe a bird outside our window, a solved puzzle, or a moment of laughter?” O—Other-centered kindness (noticing moral beauty): This centers on acts of care, inclusion, or helpfulness witnessed throughout the day. Prompt: “Who is someone else you noticed doing something kind, caring, or helpful today—seen or unseen?” Y—Your own contribution (reflective pride): This is anchored in agency and emotional regulation, helping us to recognize how we foster our own joy. Prompt: “What is something you did today that you are proud of, or a time you showed patience and care to yourself or someone else?” Related GGiE practices: Inspiring Virtue With Moral Beauty; Stories of Moral Beauty; Noticing Kindness; Nurturing Students’ Wonder and Curiosity Thank you to all of the educators in the Greater Good community who shared activities for bringing joy into schools and classrooms. We received many, many more than we could share here. Even in the face of incredible challenges, you are bringing so much joy to your students and their families, your colleagues, and yourself.
