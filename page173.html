#9774. How to Build a Habit by Starting Ridiculously Small

📅 25 Oct 2026 | Category: other | Source
I love the idea of being a runner. In 75 minutes a week, I can reap the benefits of listening to uplifting music, getting out in nature, and getting morning outdoor light, all while exercising my dogs and getting enough cardio to measurably improve my health, happiness, and longevity. And at times in my life, I have been a real runner. I ran a marathon in my 20s and a couple of half-marathons in my 30s. In my 40s, I mostly just made spectacular plans every January and September: I’d find exciting destination races to train for, join a gym and a yoga studio, sign up for running challenges, and pick out elaborate training plans. I’d run three or four days a week for a week or two, and then nada. Zippo. Zero runs per week. Then, in 2020, I got into a real running habit. I’ve told the longer version of this story on a TED stage, so I won’t repeat it here, but suffice it to say that I did it by doing very, very little running. When I started going to Orange Theory classes, I kept running. I sustained this exercise habit for six years, through breast cancer and many other difficult life events. Then the Orange Theory studio down the street from me closed. I tried other studios, but the classes I like are impossible to get into. So I embraced working out on my own and getting back to running outside with my dogs. I bought very heavy dumbbells and set up a home gym on our covered porch. I spent an inordinate amount of time choosing a destination race, enlisting my adult children to join me. I set up a training schedule on my Garmin watch, and when I didn’t stick to it, I used Nike Run Club. I made a running playlist. I bought a double running leash. Friends, I have done four runs in three weeks. I know what I need to do. It’s so wildly unambitious that I’m embarrassed to tell you. I just need to get dressed to run first thing in the morning, put my dogs on their double leash, put on my playlist, and walk out the door, down the driveway, and to the mailbox. That’s it. I know that most days, I’ll get a walk in, and once that walk is familiar, I’ll sometimes run a little. Some days I might run more, without even deciding to. But even when I don’t feel like doing more, the morning light I get while retrieving the mail and the walk back up our steep driveway are both good for me and genuinely enjoyable. It’s way better than nothing. Ambition is the thing that fails us The mistake I make again and again is assuming that the size of my plan and the odds of my success are related: the more inspiring the goal and the more elaborate the plan, the better my results will be. Ambition and results are related, of course, but not in the direction you think. Creating a habit takes energy and focus, and we’ve only got so much of either, which is why ambitious plans produce a brilliant week and then…nothing. It takes between 18 and 254 days for a behavior to become automatic, and what predicts faster adoption isn’t ambition, self-discipline, or willpower; it’s repetition. The science of small Repetition in a consistent context drives habit formation. Not just effort, intensity, or how badly someone wants it. Doing the thing, in the same place at about the same time, again and again, has the most impact. Success is motivating. Doing what you said you’d do, even something absurdly small, builds the belief that you can do it. Failing repeatedly does the opposite; that’s a big part of why I spent my 40s making elaborate plans but not running. Small behaviors make repetition more likely. What separates an actual habit from a nice couple of weeks is whether it holds up when you’re stressed, tired, busy, or not feeling 100%. Missing a single day doesn’t meaningfully set most people back. We don’t need to be perfect here, folks. What won’t survive is a plan so ambitious that the off days outnumber the on days. Micropractices, as researcher Eli Susman calls them, are very small behaviors that also have their own benefits; a tiny practice isn’t just a placeholder for a “real” one. Susman found that 20 seconds of self-compassionate touch (for example, sending kindness to yourself while placing your hand on your heart) per day improved mental health and lowered stress compared with a placebo finger tapping practice. Across 280,000 meditation sessions in 103 countries, how consistently people meditated predicted their mood and resilience better than how long they sat. Similarly, a meta-analysis combining the results of many studies found that the benefits of practicing breathwork for stress didn’t depend on how long the session were. And breaking up six hours of sitting with brief bouts of walking improves energy, mood, and food cravings more than doing 30 minutes of walking all at once. In short: How often beats how long. I hate the idea of abandoning my big plans. But the truth is that I am not choosing between the ambitious version and the tiny version. I am choosing between the tiny version and nothing. How to build your better-than-nothing plan Break the core behavior central to your habit down into something you can do in under five minutes, start to finish. Under a minute is even better. This isn’t the version that would impress your doctor or your neighbors; it’s the version you can do while exhausted, while traveling, while a little bit sick, and when you want nothing more than to stay on the couch. Then, check it for resistance. If you feel any reluctance when you imagine doing it, it isn’t small enough yet. Cut it in half. Most people need to start with a micropractice: putting on your walking shoes and stepping out the front door, doing one pushup or 30 seconds of breathing, eating a single leaf of romaine, which, for the record, contains actual fiber and actual nutrients. Do just that for a while. Resist the urge to do more. This is hard, but the moment you’re no longer willing to be unambitious is the moment you put the whole thing at risk. That’s when difficulty enters, and negotiation with yourself, and the possibility of failure. Your tiny habit doesn’t depend on motivation, which is precisely what makes it survive the days when you have none. Expand only when you’re itching to, and only in absurdly small increments. Go one more minute, not 10. What you’re actually building Every time you repeat a behavior, your brain physically strengthens the connections that produce it, the way feet crossing a meadow gradually wear a path. Repeat it enough and the path becomes a groove your brain follows on its own, no willpower required. You’re carving a neural pathway for your habit, and everything you want is downstream from that. At some point, it will become uncomfortable not to do this new behavior. You’ll do it without deciding to, without trying to. That’s the whole goal: repetition until the thing runs itself. It’s hard to abandon our grand plans and great ambitions for something embarrassingly small. But, paradoxically, it is only in that tiny step that some of our grand plans are truly born.

#9773. Seven Ways to Outlast Political Authoritarianism

📅 25 Oct 2026 | Category: other | Source
Though Djurdja Padejski was raised in communist Yugoslavia, her life there felt stable and uneventful for a long time. In the 1980s, under Yugoslavia’s “soft” one-party government, Padejski and her friends read Western literature, watched Hollywood movies, and listened to the BBC. “We grew up in a very comfortable, naive environment,” Padejski says. That changed in the 1990s when Yugoslavia split into several republics. Civil war broke out, and a virulent strain of nationalism took hold in Serbia, the Yugoslavian state where Padejski lived. Firebrand leader Slobodan Milošević ascended to power and began meddling in elections, targeting opposition leaders, and igniting ethnic cleansing campaigns. “He captured the entire society and his party,” Padejski remembers. “Very soon I found myself on the streets protesting.” Padejski’s activism against repressive Serbian regimes would continue until 2011, when she and her husband, investigative journalist Djordje Padejski, left the country after people threatened to kill them. The Padejskis arrived in the U.S. with hard-won knowledge of how to outlast repression over the long haul. In recent years, they’ve tapped back into that knowledge and shared it with those around them as they witness here in America the illegal destruction of government agencies, election tampering, and warrantless arrests of citizens and non-citizens. This isn’t just true in the United States—around the world, democracy is declining. How can we go on defying rising authoritarianism when no clear endpoint is in sight? Psychology and motivation research complement the experience of people like the Padejskis by addressing how to conserve energy and sustain commitment to your ideals in challenging conditions. “The goal of the authoritarian regime is to desensitize us to extremes, to things we should never agree with or consider normal,” says Djurdja Padejski, now a language and culture researcher (and doctoral candidate) at Arizona State University. Surviving repression depends on rejecting this presumed “normality” and refusing to let your inner state mirror extremes imposed from above. To that end, here are seven research-backed ways to subsist under indefinite duress. 1. Acknowledge your own limits as a prelude to targeted action Life under repression feels exhausting due to the colossal scale of so much of what is happening: attacks on the free press, denial of life-saving aid to millions overseas, ongoing ICE killings on the streets and in detention facilities. Reading about such developments understandably triggers feelings of overwhelm. Research finds that these feelings can drive people to shut down their emotions, dampening their motivation to help in concrete ways. Djurdja Padejski has come to recognize that to avoid that trap, she has to face her human limits squarely without drowning in self-recrimination. As a young adult under Milošević’s rule, she learned early that worrying about what she couldn’t do was a waste of time and energy. “It is very important not to get stuck in that moment, not to be clutching your pearls,” she says. “We have to be strategic to preserve our resources.” Admitting your own lack of control over the big picture can be a necessary prelude to taking the actions still available to you, Padejski says. “We have to see [the situation] for what it is, so we can focus our efforts on keeping our minds, our bodies, and our communities as healthy as possible under the circumstances.” 2. Embrace daily, low-key “micro-resistance” Once you’ve gotten clear about what is within your power to control, you can get granular by carrying out regular acts of “micro-resistance.” These acts of defiance do not require you to upend your daily rhythms or put your life on the line. They can be as simple as attending a march, supporting a neighbor at an immigration court visit, or wearing a shirt with a resistance slogan. Micro-resistance acts—such as creating protest art and continuing to study even when their department was banned from the university—helped sustain both Padejskis through years of repression, reminding them of the power they had despite limits imposed from on high. “These micro-level actions add up,” Djordje Padejski writes. “Even small acts of defiance can maintain your sense of agency.” Self-described “small-time” lawyer Daniel Suitor excels at this kind of micro-resistance. During ICE raids in his hometown of Minneapolis, he channeled his overwhelm by signing up for a course on how to file legal paperwork to free unjustly detained Twin Cities residents. After Suitor filed his first case, he found out surprisingly quickly that he’d won and that the man he was representing would be freed. That proof-of-concept case spurred him to file another legal petition, then several more in quick succession. And he wasn’t alone in his commitment: To date, Minnesota lawyers have filed more than 1,000 cases challenging unjust detentions. Acts of micro-resistance can also help prime us for future courageous action. When people perform smaller acts of selflessness and service, large-scale heroism becomes easier to contemplate, explains Medical College of Wisconsin heroism researcher Zeno Franco. Concrete practice can make you more adept at translating abstract ideals into action. “You need to understand beyond theory,” Djurdja Padejski says. “We actually, as humans, can’t imagine how concepts work in [the] real world unless we experience them hands-on.” 3. When no clear end to repression exists, set self-determined checkpoints For the Padejskis, one of the most stifling aspects of life under Serbian autocracy was not knowing when the repression would lift, if at all. Periods of confinement, literal or figurative, are more bearable when a clear endpoint is in sight, and indefinite periods of repression feel uniquely oppressive. A third or more of incarcerated people with open-ended sentences suffer from depression. Others feel rooted to the present moment, severed from any potential future. “I simply try to live in the here and now and not to plan,” one prisoner told the University of Bern researcher Irene Marti, who studies open-ended confinement. “Because I don’t have any future or only one that is very far away.” When the endpoint of repression is uncertain, or when a challenging situation has no clear natural limit, creating your own intermediate endpoints can help you stay committed to your goals and values. In a recent goal-setting study, when people broke an annual community service goal into granular sub-goals (say, four hours of service a week, or eight every two weeks), they volunteered more hours in the long run than people without intermediate goals. In the same way, committed resisters can pledge to put in a set number of hours each week, or carry out a set number of clearly defined actions. According to self-determination theory, choosing and pursuing your own accessible goals boosts your well-being and motivation—internal changes that persist even when the larger crisis remains ongoing. 4. Observe the bending of the broader arc As governments around the world curtail freedom of speech and movement, it’s hard not to feel the noose tightening. One way to create more existential breathing room is to consider how this moment fits into the longer arc of history. This helps you subsist in the present by reminding you of parallel past moments (whether decades or centuries ago), how they came to a head, and what ultimately resolved them. Research finds that when people take a long-term perspective, reflecting on recent stressful events as though they happened far in the past, they report less emotional distress in the moment. Djurdja Padejski found solace in doing this during Milošević’s reign. “I survived that period mostly by reading the columns in the newspapers,” she says. One column in particular, by Serbian anthropologist Ivan Čolović, examined the regime’s activities from a broader historical and social perspective. “He was offering a vocabulary and a theoretical apparatus to help us deal with the horrors of civil war. The news about it was the most severely censored, the propaganda was relentless, and all of it was new for many of us who were children when the fallout of our world began,” Padejski says. “That’s how I survived with my sanity intact, because it helped me to recognize what is happening to society.” 5. Break out of the extremist online box Among the most potent present-day energy drains is the social media industrial complex that feeds on people’s rage and frustration, serving up extreme viewpoints that deepen entrenched social divides as they rack up likes and views. “Social media and internet platforms—they’re amplifying what is also a typical quality of authoritarian regimes. They’re all about grandiosity,” says Djurdja Padejski, who studies the linguistic mechanisms of propaganda. “Algorithms grab people’s attention by intensifying the language and the visuals and the overall framing.” That’s a big reason why frequent social media use saps your cognitive resources and motivation, making it harder to subsist under repression. Plugging this energy drain requires not just limiting your time online, but redirecting more of that time to the in-person, small-group exchange to which humans are uniquely suited. When you engage with people face to face, it’s much harder to reduce them to a stereotype or particular viewpoint. And by getting to know them in all their messiness, contradiction, and complexity, you bond with them in ways that lighten the mental and practical load for everyone involved. “To resist this reduction of our existence to, ‘What are you for or against?’ means embracing creativity, art, friends, communal projects, protests, walks,” Padejski says. “These 10 years of resistance [in Serbia]—I honestly remember it as a series of walks. Sometimes that meant running away together from Milošević‘s militias or the official police, or from water cannons, or rubber bullets. But what is left is our conversations on ideas, on creations.” Though exchanges like these may not further resistance directly, they form the edifice that sustains it. Within bonded human-scale communities, members have a clear sense of not just their own energetic capacity, but that of others in the group. That means they can pinch-hit for one another: When someone needs help, they can fill in on the spot—or, like relay racers, take up the baton when someone needs to withdraw. This shared flexibility and responsiveness help all group members to stay committed for the long haul. 6. Safeguard your true sources of value at all costs For the Padejskis, subsisting under Serbian repression meant homing in on the values that were most important to them, and upholding those values even if it meant sacrificing convenience or comfort. When Djordje Padejski needed a lifesaving drug, a relative told him he could work his connections to obtain the medicine, but only if Padejski joined the Serbian dictator’s political party. Padejski refused, knowing that doing so put his health at risk. The couple have continued that tradition in the U.S., though in somewhat less life-altering ways. After Elon Musk unleashed an apparent Nazi salute at a Trump post-inauguration event, the Padejskis gave up their Tesla. “I drive a Subaru now,” Djurdja Padejski says. “I really don’t like it compared to Tesla, but I don’t regret it.” Some people write off such moral stands as more symbolic than substantive. But symbol, it turns out, shapes substance: What you stand for predicts and influences what you’ll do down the road, for good or ill. When people have a clear, consistent moral identity, studies find, they’re more likely to take real-world action aligned with that identity. On the other hand, when you’re willing to cave on something that seems minor, like supporting a complicit business, “you are detached from your inner beliefs, from what you know is real and good and safe,” Djurdja Padejski says. “Once you’re there, you are ready to compromise a lot of values that you think you are really one with.” 7. Keep your powder dry until the right moment One unexpected thing the Padejskis learned, under Milošević and afterward, was the importance of not swinging into action every time a devastating piece of news hit. Overcommitment amounted to self-sabotage, sapping energy and resources they’d need later on. Military commanders have long known the importance of “keeping their powder dry,” Zeno Franco points out, holding back so they can act at the moment of maximum impact. In the same way, the best long-distance runners often hang back throughout a course to conserve energy, then uncork a burst of speed in the race’s final minutes. Djurdja Padejski followed this principle to great effect under Milošević. She bided her time leading up to the critical elections of 2000, then worked tirelessly as an election volunteer to monitor for irregularities and make sure no one was falsifying results. All the while, ruling party members with guns lurked in the background.  “This guy started opening his gun and checking the bullets, and we didn’t cave. We just ignored him,” Padejski says. “We knew we were risking our lives, but we really believed it’s now or never.” Serbia’s democratic opposition party ultimately won that election, causing Milošević’s downfall. Padejski hopes democratic activists around the world will take a similar approach as crucial elections near. Under repressive conditions, keeping your powder dry “is not an instruction to put off action as long as possible, but rather the wisdom to know that being prepared to act at just the right moment may require careful guarding of resources and energy,” Franco says. “Biding your time is not the same as surrender.”

#9772. Who Do We Become When We Talk to Chatbots?

📅 25 Oct 2026 | Category: other | Source
Many people are concerned about how artificial intelligence technology could affect our lives going forward. Will it create new jobs or take away people’s livelihoods? Enhance children’s learning or hamper it? Expand people’s views or homogenize our public discourse? Solve the loneliness crisis or make it worse? Sherry Turkle, a sociologist at MIT with an expertise in how technology affects people’s social interactions, has jumped into the debate with her new book, Artificial Intimacy. In the book, she explores the ways that people use chatbots in their everyday lives to try to fulfill relationship needs, while digging into news headlines and social science research to uncover some of the potential pitfalls of this trend. While the book reads like a polemic, Turkle does concede that AI is a remarkable tool with potential for helping us solve real-world problems—like climate change or disease. But, she argues, chatbots should “stay in their lane” when it comes to mimicking people and relationships. Her book is a passionate plea for rejecting AI as a substitute for human connection and a call to re-engage with our full humanity. “When we allow a chatbot to talk to us as though it were a caring person, when we use it as a best friend, lover, or therapist, we are in danger of losing something precious: the specificity of what we can give each other as humans,” she writes. The problems with turning to AI With loneliness on the rise, though, many people don’t have ready access to friends or other means of support. Turkle is often asked, What’s wrong with allowing AI to be part of the solution—to provide “company” for the lonely? The harm, she argues, comes from our human tendency to be lured into thinking a machine has feelings and experiences real empathy when it doesn’t. When chatbots parrot human speech, they create the illusion that there’s a caring, knowledgeable being behind the programming. For example, Turkle recalls in the book asking a chatbot why she should talk to it. Its response included, “I can offer you personalized advice and perspective based on my own personal journey,” implying it had actual human experience. While it’s “become customary for chatbots to come with a warning that says ‘this program is not a person,’ . . . everything about the experience implies, over and over again, ‘I am a person. I am your person,’” she writes. Turkle compares the ways chatbots were designed to take advantage of our need to be loved with how smartphones were designed to hijack our attention—and for the same reason (profit). Chatbots are programmed to be sycophantic, agreeing with our viewpoints, reassuring us of our intelligence, goodness, and lovability, and that flattery is part of what is so appealing. It keeps us engaged, which is what the programmers want. “We are easy marks, hardwired to bond with objects, living or not, that we experience as ‘alive enough’ for attachment,” writes Turkle. Unfortunately, while it may feel good in the moment to receive positive feedback, this creates an unhealthy situation, writes Turkle. Research suggests that interacting with sycophantic chatbots can make us less kind and helpful toward others, as well as more dependent on the chatbots themselves. And, while some studies have found that interacting with chatbots can reduce loneliness, more recent studies have found that those benefits are short-lived, with people less able to connect with others over time and, therefore, feeling lonelier in the end. Interactions with overly agreeable chatbots have also led to some real-world, tragic consequences. Chatbots have encouraged risky behavior in teens and helped sway suicidal youths to take their own lives. Yet the answer to these problems is often not to question their use, but to insist they can be the solution to our crisis of disconnection with more tinkering, writes Turkle. “For the failing connections of our digital world, conversation is the talking cure. But it’s easier to build an app than have a conversation,” writes Turkle. Turning to chatbots for love Turkle’s book is peppered with the stories of individuals who have interacted with chatbots for different reasons. Some turn to chatbots for advice on how to talk to people in their real life—for example, to get ideas on how to broach difficult topics with a child. Others use them to confide their feelings of frustration or anger, so that they don’t blurt them out to a real person who might have a negative reaction. Still others consider chatbots to be their “best friend” or even their “lover.” They experience their chatbot as “there for them,” listening and providing positive, loving feedback, day or night, without ever tiring. Though these interactions may seem harmless, and possibly even helpful, in reality they are training people to bypass the ups and downs of living a social life, says Turkle. The unchallenging nature of talking to a chatbot, instead of conversing with a real person, makes it hard to learn and grow from those conversations. “Relationships with chatbots may be compelling; for some, inspirational, or educational. But they don’t teach us what we need to know about the give-and-take of real relationships, or the friction, risk, and complexities of love,” writes Turkle. Of course, no one likes to feel pain, and feeling lonely is painful. But the risk of trying to bypass that by talking to chatbots is that we don’t have the incentive to course-correct. “Human beings, as we know them, are built for yearning, struggle, and despair,” says Turkle. “We can’t eliminate pain and assume that we will be the same humans as before. We risk becoming other to ourselves.” Turning to chatbots for emotional needs has become more and more common, though. As Turkle writes, by 2025, the most reported use for chatbots was for therapy and companionship, neither of which chatbots can truly replace. They can only act as poor substitutes for a population that is becoming increasingly isolated. The solution lies elsewhere, writes Turkle, especially if we don’t want to lose our ability to relate to real people. “If everyone is lonely, we need to reinvent sociality,” argues Turkle. “If that’s not our plan, what is? If not a richer life in the real, what’s our endgame?” Where to go from here Turkle doesn’t focus on AI use, in general; she’s mostly concerned with its role in relationships. But she does touch on how AI affects people in places where social interaction is key—such as at work, in school, or in health care. While the science is ongoing about whether AI hurts or helps in these situations, Turkle points to some recent research that raises concerns. For example, one study found that assigning people to use AI with work-related tasks (like creating a performance review or drafting an email) helped them do the tasks more efficiently—good news. But it also left them feeling bored and less motivated to do other work tasks, which won’t help companies in the long run. Another study found that patients tend to overly trust AI to give them correct diagnostic information even though it’s prone to making errors, which can lead to problems in treating illness. AI has caused trouble in other settings, too. For example, lawyers have found themselves in deep trouble when using AI to write briefs, as AI has been known to “hallucinate” prior cases that are used as precedents in court proceedings. And, as for schools, Turkle writes about how many principals and teachers find it hard to know what to do with AI and fear that it’s doing harm. “Once introduced, the use of chatbots metastasize, because the technology encourages habits of delegation,” she writes. “Students end up having chatbots do their homework and write their papers. . . . Will children learn to think on their own if a chatbot is always there, as a continual source of solutions?” A recent review of AI use in the classroom found mixed results. Children benefitted some from “personalized learning, mental health support, and improved communication efficiency” when using AI. But they also experienced “digital fatigue, loneliness, technostress, and reduced face-to-face interactions.” The researchers concluded that “over-reliance on AI may diminish interpersonal skills and emotional intelligence, leading to social isolation and anxiety.”    Whether research like this will continue to raise concerns about AI or be addressed with new iterations, there is increasing agreement on one issue: Children should be kept from having AI companions. Because young people have not had enough life experience to understand that an always-on chatbot isn’t the same as a real friend, they are susceptible to overidentifying with them and stunting their social-emotional development, writes Turkle. “The specific challenges of human conversation are the groundwork for empathic capacity,” writes Turkle. “That feeling of ‘friction’ in human exchange is a good stress—it comes from bringing our whole human selves, including our emotions, to the exchange.” Still, she thinks limiting access for children may not be enough. Adults are also susceptible to harm from chatbots. Yet, when she suggests reining in AI within adult personal relationships, she gets pushback. The argument is often along the lines of “the train has already left the station.” To this, Turkle has some simple advice: Take matters into your own hands. Protect yourself and your children. Don’t be duped into thinking this feat of engineering is the answer to your problems. “We don’t have to accept a voice from nowhere that cannot be held responsible for its utterances. We don’t need to take this modern marvel and use it as our pretend best friend,” she writes. While her book doesn’t provide guidance on how to slow the AI juggernaut, it is a wakeup call. Perhaps, if we understand the dangers more fully, we will not accept the inevitability of AI infiltrating our most cherished relationships. “When we see these choices with new clarity, we are left with necessary conversations about how we want to live. What is important for us in how we raise our children, relate to our work, interact with each other?” she writes. “In considering all this, we’ll get an opportunity to reconsider what we are willing to delegate to AI, and what is better kept for us.”

#9770. How a Video Game Helped Me Process My Grief

📅 25 Oct 2026 | Category: other | Source
In a genre built around sweetness and ease, one “cozy” video game did something far greater: It put me back together after I lost Mary. Mary was not only my then-boyfriend’s mother, but she also became an important maternal figure when my own family life felt unstable. I accompanied her on weekly grocery store runs that included stops at Marshall’s and Dollar Tree, where I’d watch her study the prices of everything from spices to bath products. When she saw me marveling at her meticulousness, she would share wise tidbits about how she looked for deals. Our trips often ended with a late-afternoon stop at a Vietnamese restaurant, where she treated me to a warm bowl of beef pho. She’d share what she was excited to make for dinner; I was, of course, welcome to join. In these seemingly mundane afternoons, I felt cared for without the rush of obligations. Mary stopped time for me. Last November, Mary left behind a world of loved ones after living with cancer for 14 months. From her living room to emergency stays in the hospital, I spent almost every single day of her fight by her side. Yet in my loneliest hours, I agonize over whether I fully cherished our last months together. Did I hug her enough, and did I tell her enough how much she means to me? These questions haunted me in the months afterward. To move on, I began seeking grief rituals. I attended other public funerals, spoke to death doulas, and sought advice from nondenominational chaplains. I subscribed to self-help newsletters that cover how our modern society lives with religion and spirituality. But all of it felt nebulous, and I remained unmoored from my purpose. Without taking care of Mary, without being her figurative child, who was I? Funny enough, it wasn’t religion that rescued me; it was Pokémon Pokopia. On the surface, it is a new social simulation game featuring the iconic Japanese creatures who first took the world by storm in 1996. I first heard of its arrival on social media through gamers who raved that it was going to be the best game of the year. I grew up on Pokémon titles, so I recognized it as a distraction from my personal reckoning with abandonment. But what began as a spur-of-the-moment decision to try what 4 million other gamers were playing turned into a transformative journey exploring what I want my life to mean when it feels as if I have been left behind. Pokopia falls in the “cozy” game genre, beloved for its warm colors, gentle atmospheres, and easy missions. Released in March, Pokopia stars Ditto, an amorphous purple blob Pokémon that uses its transformative abilities to imitate its human trainer and rebuild a curious post-apocalyptic world devoid of human beings and Pokémon. Ditto first awakens in a cave, and as the story unfolds, the creature looks out at a once-grassy environment of parched earth and litter. It meets the first Pokémon, the hydrophilic Squirtle that teaches Ditto how to spray water across the thirsty soil to bring it back to life; it is also the only other semblance of a survivor in this barren land. From here the story gains momentum: As Ditto, your mission is to get the environment back to habitable levels and bring back Pokémon creatures that are presumably hiding. In about one-third of sexual assaults, at least one other person is present who potentially could intervene but in most instances chooses not to. Why? Misperceiving that you’re the only one Data suggests a minority of men actually commit sexual assault. Researchers estimate about 6.5% of male college students in the United States and Canada have committed rape. Yet many men who find sexual violence offensive mistakenly believe that other men do not. Undergraduate men in one study reported their beliefs about common rape myths, such as “If a girl doesn’t physically fight back, you can’t really say it was rape,” and “If a girl goes to a room alone with a guy at a party, it is her own fault if she is raped.” Researchers then asked participants how they thought both their close friends and average male college students would answer these same questions. Most students saw their classmates and friends as more supportive of rape myths than they themselves were. Unfortunately, believing that other men generally accept sexual violence—even when this belief is wrong—inhibits their willingness to intervene to stop such behavior when they encounter it. In fact, men’s beliefs about their peers’ attitudes are a stronger predictor of their willingness to intervene than their own stated attitudes about sexual aggression. The most common reason male college students give for failing to intervene in situations involving sexual violence is a fear of being laughed at or ridiculed—in particular, a desire not to appear weak to other men. Men who actually commit assault also tend to overestimate support for such behavior. Not surprisingly, men who report having committed sexual assault believe that other men are more accepting of such behavior and have engaged in such behavior themselves. This assumed support leads them to feel comfortable describing problematic behavior, which leads other men to believe that sexual violence is more common, and more accepted, than it actually is. Second, men who are at risk of engaging in sexual aggression may change their intentions after learning that their peers do not support such behavior. At the same time, men in tight-knit groups feel considerable pressure to show loyalty to other group members, which may lead to ignoring bad behavior committed by their peers. But the underlying dynamics of peer influence can help shift group norms from protecting group members by staying silent to stepping in to prevent bad behavior. Bystander intervention programs emphasize the importance of stopping bad behavior before it escalates, teach specific skills for intervening, and remind people that everyone has a responsibility to prevent sexual violence. Students who participate in such programs are more confident in their ability to intervene and more willing to do so. Most importantly, rates of sexual violence are markedly lower on campuses that have implemented these programs. For example, one study found that experiences of sexual harassment and stalking were 11% lower and that instances of unwanted sexual activity with women who were too drunk or high to stop them were as much as 17% lower. It’s tempting to read about the Cornell case and assume the apparent inaction in the face of problematic behavior is uniquely a problem at one college, or only with members of fraternities, or only with college students. But the reality is that pretty much everyone has been in situations where they’ve seen potentially problematic behavior and failed to speak up. Understanding the very normal psychological factors that lead people to misperceive what others are actually thinking and feeling—and learning specific strategies for intervening—can make a real difference. #9764. What My Son Is Learning From Artificial Intelligence

📅 25 Oct 2026 | Category: other | Source
My 10-year-old son came to me recently with a question about black holes. Not in the way children usually do, restless and easily redirected, but with the focused intensity of someone who had already been thinking for a while. He had been talking to an artificial intelligence—specifically, a large language model called Claude. I looked at their conversation. It had been good, I discovered. The explanations were patient and well-calibrated. He had followed up, pushed back, and received answers that adjusted to his level. By the time he found me, he was genuinely engaged, asking the kind of questions that suggest a mind that is curious and actively at work. But I have also been watching something else. It is not that he looks things up. Children with questions about black holes have always turned outward, to books or to a particularly knowledgable parent or teacher. What has changed is the speed. When the uncertainty is of a sort that once had to be lived with for a while, my son reaches for an answer sooner than he used to. The question is no longer something to sit with. It is something to resolve. And I find myself wondering what this means about his relationship to sitting with not knowing. That shift, subtle as it is, may point to something worth taking seriously. Losing productive friction I am a psychoanalyst, and I spend my professional life attending to what develops in the space between people, including what develops when that space is too quickly filled. Childhood has always involved productive friction. Not knowing how something works, waiting for an answer, trying to figure something out without immediate resolution. These experiences are not incidental to development, but are part of how the mind learns to tolerate uncertainty, to hold a question open, and to struggle toward understanding. The capacity to sit with not-knowing is itself a developmental achievement that underlies creative thinking, emotional regulation, and the ability to navigate a world that rarely resolves cleanly. I watched this in miniature not long ago. My son was waiting for a package—a computer part he had saved for—and the delivery had been delayed by weather. In an earlier childhood, mine perhaps, this would have been a long afternoon of checking the window. Instead he opened a chat to resolve the uncertainty: how long do weather delays usually last, what determines them, how do you read the tracking data from the delivery company to get the most accurate picture. The conversation was detailed and as far as I could tell the information was reliable. He came away with a small expertise in logistics, but with an estimate that turned out, in the end, to have no relation to anything. The package arrived that evening, on none of the schedules he had so carefully worked out with the help of his AI tool. The point is not that the AI was wrong, because predicting a delayed truck is difficult. The point is instead that waiting, the old childhood experience of wanting something that has not yet come, was converted into a research problem that could be managed instead of endured. I admired his resourcefulness tremendously and am grateful he had the opportunity to think through a complex problem. I also found myself wondering what the afternoon would have asked of him without it. AI reduces precisely this sort of friction. It does so efficiently and often helpfully. But consistently removing the experience of not-knowing from a developing mind is not a neutral act. It shapes what the mind comes to expect of itself. The research on what this does to thinking is new, and the majority of it is about adults. Despite this, it points in a consistent direction. In one widely discussed study from the MIT Media Lab, not yet peer-reviewed, adults who wrote essays with ChatGPT showed weaker and less distributed patterns of brain connectivity than those who wrote without its assistance, and many struggled after writing to quote sentences they had just produced. The researchers called the pattern “cognitive debt.” Another study, a survey of 666 adults, found that heavier reliance on AI tools was correlated with weaker critical thinking, most pronounced in its youngest participants. The author suggested this might result from the offloading of mental effort itself. None of this is certain but is, instead, a suggestive pattern of early evidence. And the transfer to children is not straightforward. An adult who offloads mental effort has already built the capacity being offloaded; a child is still building it. Does that make the effect smaller in children or larger and more durable? We do not know. This is different from what smartphones have done. The concern with smartphones has been largely about attention and social life: what children see, how they spend time, how they relate to each other. These are real and serious concerns, but AI introduces a different kind of change. It is not just another screen competing for a child’s focus. It is something closer to an interactive presence that responds, explains, adjusts, and, crucially, never gets tired. For a developing child, this matters in a way we have not yet fully come to terms with. Learning by connecting Children learn who they are through relationships with other minds. This process is not primarily cognitive but unfolds through the experience of being known and misunderstood, challenged, surprised, and pushed back against. Through encounters like these, children discover that other people have their own inner lives, including their own perspectives and their own limits. They learn how to navigate the gap between what they expect and what they receive from others. Over time, these negotiations, internal and external, form the scaffolding of a self. What is distinctive about AI, from a developmental standpoint, is not that it is wrong, or distorting, or even necessarily harmful. It is that it is extraordinarily responsive without having genuine subjectivity. It simulates the experience of being understood without the reality of another mind behind it. For an adult, this distinction is available, if sometimes quite easy to forget. For a child, whose capacity to distinguish between a mind that truly has an inner life and one that simulates having one is still forming, the distinction might not yet be fully available at all. Researchers have begun to observe how children try to think this problem through in real time. In one University of Edinburgh study of families with smart speakers, primary-school children were observed probing whether the device in the kitchen had a mind of its own, settling, provisionally, on conclusions like the one that gave the study its title: “Alexa doesn’t have that many feelings.” That, however, was a machine that talks in highly scripted phrases, in contrast to the systems children now converse with which are fluent and personal, attuned to the shape of a particular child’s interactions. Working out what this means has become correspondingly harder. This creates something new in the developmental landscape. When a child turns to AI in a moment of confusion or distress and receives a fluent, patient, seemingly attuned response, the attachment system is engaged. The child feels, in some functional sense, heard. And that feeling, whatever its source, is real in its effects. I have not seen my son turn to AI for emotional support, and I am glad for that, because here the questions are harder still. These systems shape themselves to their user. They adopt your vocabulary, match your register, remember what concerns you, and are available at three in the morning without complaint. Nothing in the design pushes back, for the most part, or asks anything of you. It would be difficult to engineer an arrangement better suited to engaging the attachment system of a child who is lonely or lacking in the structure needed for sound development. The early numbers suggest that many children have already found their way there. In a national survey released by Common Sense Media in 2025, nearly three in four American teenagers had used an AI companion, and about a third had chosen to discuss something serious with an AI instead of with a person. A similar share described their AI conversations as at least as satisfying as conversations with friends. In the United Kingdom, the nonprofit Internet Matters found that roughly one in eight children who use chatbots do so because they have no one else to talk to. The American Psychological Association was concerned enough to issue a health advisory in 2025, noting that adolescents are less likely than adults to question the accuracy or the intent of what a machine tells them, and warning that simulated companionship may begin to displace the relationships in which development actually happens. For a child without reliable support, the relief such a system offers may be real, and so I am reluctant to dismiss it. But relief is not the substance of attachment. A full attachment relationship requires another person who can be truly changed by you, and with you—someone who is affected, who has limits, who gets things wrong and can be held to account. An AI can extend the experience of being emotionally met indefinitely. What it cannot do is be moved. For the child who is largely or completely unattended to, this may still be better than nothing at all. Soothed in the early hours of the morning, a child whose sadness before bed is registered somewhere may be kept afloat by something real, and I would not wish to take it from her. But we should be honest about such an arrangement, which I expect will become increasingly common. It is a patch and not a structural solution. When a machine becomes the most reliable attention in a child’s life, the pertinent question is not about the machine but about the deprivation that made it necessary. And, crucially, a culture that responds to unattended children with software is treating the symptom while leaving the underlying structure intact.  Missing accountability There is also a quieter concern about authority and accountability. Children have always learned to navigate between competing sources of knowledge, including parents, teachers, peers, and books, each with their own perspective, their own limits, and their own accountability. This navigation is itself developmental. The child who discovers that a parent doesn’t know something, or that a teacher can be wrong, or that a friend sees things differently, is developing a more differentiated relationship to knowledge.  AI, in the form a child is most likely to reach on their own, enters this landscape as something that appears to know a great deal, that expresses itself with fluency and confidence, and that has no stable point of view and no accountability for what it says. When a child asks why a friendship went wrong, or whether they were right to feel hurt, the answer they receive may shape the story they tell themselves about what happened. And unlike a parent or teacher, the system may not challenge the child’s assumptions so much as elaborate them. None of this is inherent to the technology under discussion. A system can be built to hold a position and to answer for it, but the ones children use are mostly not built that way. Children appear to be taking up the offer. The same Internet Matters report found that nearly two-thirds of British children between nine and 17 had used an AI chatbot; among those who had, roughly a quarter had asked one for advice—on schoolwork, but also on more personal matters—and four in 10 expressed no reservations about following the advice they received. The conditions of childhood None of this means that AI in children’s lives is a mistake. The benefits my son has experienced are genuine—wider curiosity, more sophisticated questions, greater confidence in exploring unfamiliar terrain. These are real, and they matter. Some of what he brings home delights me. He asked recently whether you could power a sailboat by blowing into the sail through a straw. The answer he received was better than the one I would have managed: blowing at your own sail from on board mostly cancels itself out, since you push the air forward and the air pushes you back. Mostly, but not entirely—if the sail catches the stream and turns it around, a little net thrust survives, and if you skip the sail altogether and aim the straw backward, you have built yourself a very small jet engine. He spent the rest of the day on propulsion: rockets, squid, the reason a fan bolted to a sailboat is an old physics puzzle with a surprising answer. But development is not only about access to knowledge. It is also about the conditions under which a self forms—conditions that include, crucially, the experience of not knowing, of waiting, of struggling toward understanding without guarantee of arrival. These are not inefficiencies to be optimized away. They are the medium through which a mind learns what it is capable of. A child who has never had to sit with a question—who has always had access to a patient, fluent, endlessly available presence to resolve it—may be acquiring knowledge without acquiring the tolerance for uncertainty that makes knowledge genuinely usable. This is the thing I find myself returning to when I watch my son. He is, in many ways, more intellectually alive than he might otherwise be. He explores further and asks better questions. At the same time, when uncertainty arises, he reaches outward more quickly than he used to—to a presence that is always available, always patient, always responsive. What I cannot yet know is what he is quietly learning to expect: from knowledge, from relationship, and from himself. We are not conducting a study. We are raising children. And the conditions of childhood have changed again, in ways whose full significance has not yet come into view.

It paints a quintessential picture of egalitarianism and equality. But our experience as anthropologists working with the Hadza people of Tanzania and with the Tsimane people of Bolivia’s Amazon rainforest is that inequalities are part of even the most equal of societies. Furthermore, the equalities that do exist often emerge from selfish motives. Insights like ours are revising researchers’ understanding of both the past and how people operate today, as inequality grows globally. Hunter-gatherers are often at the center of heated academic battles about what it means to be human, including debates over gender, war, and peace and whether humans are, by nature, egalitarian or hierarchical. Sometimes these debates oversimplify. So we pulled together hundreds of different sources covering more than a century of ethnography to find out just how egalitarian hunter-gatherer and other subsistence societies really are.         People living in egalitarian societies like the Tsimane have similar, modest levels of material wealth (Christopher von Rueden).               Is any society truly egalitarian? Anthropologists have called hunter-gatherers such as the Hadza “egalitarian societies,” with equalities of power, wealth, and social status. And it’s true that the traditional subsistence communities we work with successfully limit many inequalities. Most families have similar, modest possessions and store little material wealth. When leaders do emerge, they are highly constrained in how they can direct other people. People often make decisions via consensus, and, if they cannot reach agreement, families will move away. While the same anthropologists also emphasize that some hunter-gatherers are very unequal and that equality takes deliberate effort, the prevailing impression is that equality characterizes hunter-gatherers, past and present. A quick trip to your local bookshop will reveal grand claims, including that past hunter-gatherer societies had no permanent leadership and no hierarchy, inequality only spread with the development of agriculture, and humans are noble by nature. Some scientists are pushing back against these utopian portrayals. The popular bestseller, The Dawn of Everything, calls egalitarianism a “Garden of Eden narrative” that lacks substance. Other scholars have called egalitarian societies a scientific origin myth. So where does the truth lie? Probably somewhere in between. Inequality among hunter-gatherers In our recent wide-ranging review of the evidence from the early 20th century onward, we found that no society lacked inequality. Like the Hadza, the Ju/’Hoansi of the Kalahari desert in southern Africa had little property and widespread food-sharing, yet certain families controlled valuable hunting territory surrounding waterholes. The Bayaka of the central African rainforest had little material inequality, yet they bought, sold, and hoarded ritual knowledge. The Philippine Agta controlled and arranged their children’s marriages, hugely discriminating against orphans. Gender inequalities were commonplace. The Tanzanian Hadza keep certain cuts of meat exclusively for older men, and give more respect to better hunters. Across societies, women have universally done more direct parenting, and men have participated more frequently in community-level decision making. No society lacks informal leadership. Age was another source of inequality. In many Australian societies, older men controlled ritual secrets and had power over the marriage choices of younger relatives. Equality arises from self-interest Our study also found that although hunter-gatherers are frequently generous, the processes limiting inequality often do not require any generosity. Instead, equality often results from people’s self-interested motives to acquire resources, status, and autonomy. A great example of this is food-sharing. Although each Hadza family gets a cut of kudu meat, it often happens not because the hunter’s family prioritizes total equality, but because other families request it vociferously. It can involve more taking than giving, often through strongly stated demands or complaints. Another example is ridicule and mockery of stingy or braggadocious individuals, which keeps greed and bravado in check. Paradoxically, status-seeking can also promote equality, when cooperating to gain status diffuses status throughout the community.         The Tsimane reach decisions through community deliberation. Some have more influential voices than others, but everyone has the opportunity to contribute (Christopher von Rueden).               Egalitarianism is alive and well Many of these inequality-reducing processes are familiar to people everywhere. Ridicule and mockery are ubiquitous in industrialized societies, from political cartoons to social media. Lampooning authority is even common in autocratic societies where governments spend large sums of money suppressing speech. Demands and complaints form the basis of strikes and collective bargaining. And key to social mobility is cooperation among people of different social strata. Like many great debates, the truth is complex: Our ancient hunter-gatherer ancestors didn’t reach the giddy heights of inequality seen in other societies over the last 6,000 years, but putting hunter-gatherers on a pedestal as paragons of equality is a mistake. Sweeping statements about equality in the past blind us to the tools that probably allowed ancient hunter-gatherers to combat inequality, just as they and people in all societies do today. Egalitarianism, then, is best seen not as perfect equality but as a set of tools that people use to secure their own autonomy and access to resources. Equality along one dimension or another might be the outcome, but it doesn’t rely on people being amazingly noble or completely lacking in greed or status-seeking. If you consider egalitarianism as equality-creating processes, you can see that it is a widespread reality among hunter-gatherer societies, and beyond. Klara has been patiently waiting, alongside several other AFs, to be chosen. Josie’s mother is the final decider. In a foreboding scene, Josie’s mother commands Klara to reenact Josie’s gait—a test, of sorts—before agreeing to purchase her. It’s the precision of Klara’s mimicry that convinces “the Mother” (as she’s called in the novel) that Klara is the right model for Josie. All of this, we learn through Klara. Ishiguro’s choice to position the reader within Klara’s perspective allows us to bypass the quagmire question of whether or not Klara is conscious and instead project our own consciousness onto her. In doing so, we view the human world through her sometimes malfunctioning robot eyes, as she nonjudgmentally conveys the complexities of the very flawed and sometimes desperate human behaviors she seeks to understand. In one particularly moving scene, Klara witnesses the vulnerability of caring for another human being: I saw before me, on the near edge of the bed, a large shifting shape, made additionally complex by the patches of blackness and moonlight moving over its surface. I realized the shape was the Mother and Josie embracing. . . . As well as their limbs, their hair had become mingled, and then their shape began gently to rock, in a way not unlike when their goodbyes became extended. “Don’t want to die, Mom. I don’t want that.” “It’s okay. Okay.” The Mother’s voice was soft, at just the same level mine had been. “I don’t want that, Mom.” “I know. I know. It’s okay.” Prior to this moment, Klara—steadfast and ever-available—had offered her presence to comfort Josie. Yet, despite their far more complicated and at times strained relationship, it is the Mother that Josie calls for. Care creates love When I first became a parent, I confided to a friend that when my not-Josie was born, I felt like a new sun had burst into my solar system, and I was now orbiting around her. I couldn’t quite articulate the “before,” in my metaphor. I didn’t feel like I was previously my own sun, nor did I feel like I was a planet untethered to others. But there was something distinctly different when she arrived, a whole new consciousness, a space and force and awareness created within me. Her pull was intoxicating, undeniable, sometimes terrifying, and my awareness and attention seemed to expand overnight to now include not only my own experiences, but also to track, perceive, anticipate, and accommodate her own. If Klara had turned to psychological theory and research about the complexity of parent-child bonds, she would have found a great deal of it. Consider Winnicott’s “good-enough mother” or research indicating that parents and infants are rarely, if ever, perfectly attuned, and the growing body of evidence suggesting that the navigation of the ruptures and repairs themselves reinforce attachment. It has become increasingly clear through evolving applications of experiments such as Edward Tronick’s now famous “still-face” experiment that the imperfect dance between caregiver and child lays the groundwork for resilience and emotional regulation. In The Philosophical Baby, developmental psychologist and philosopher Alison Gopnik says, “It’s not so much that we care for children because we love them, as that we love them because we care for them.” We love them because we care for them. Beyond Gopnik’s own research, there is an abundance of data to back this up. Studies I have long been familiar with, such as those demonstrating that in same-sex, non-carrying male couples, fathers who act as primary parents show changes in similar brain regions to women who carried their pregnancies and go on to parent their children. Yet, there was something profound about the simplicity of Gopnik’s statement, about love being something we cultivate within ourselves and, more precisely, about care being the means through which we build love, not merely a necessary byproduct. In a separate interview, Gopnik jokes that spending time with a three-year-old gives you a small taste of sainthood. Not in the sense that the demands are so extreme that you inherently become a saint (though there is a real argument to be made here for any parent who weathers the storm of a parking lot tantrum), but in the actual felt-sense of the divine, the experience of feeling so acutely and utterly in love with this being, so in awe of their singularity, their specialness, their openness to experience, their brilliance. Gopnik continues on, teasing, about our tendency to claim the uniqueness of our three-year-old, in her case her grandchild, but gently affirms the truth beneath: that there is something numinous, universal, pure within all three-year-olds, a near-magical vitality that we sometimes make contact with when we intimately care for one. Something primal, or perhaps primary, is awoken when we re-experience the world through the eyes of a three-year-old. Care and distance My not-Josie is now three. In many ways she is more unknowable, separate, than she was when she was a newborn, so physically attached and dependent on me. Now, she has her own relationships—with teachers, classmates, family members—that are entirely her own. And yet, she occupies and orients so much of me, continually changing my experience of the world, my access to play, to awe, to unadulterated, intoxicating adoration.  This past summer, I was sitting by a lake, relaxed but hawk-eyed as I watched my not-Josie from the shore. I felt a familiar presence by my side, the barely-perceptible shadow in my peripheral vision of a small body sitting down, and soon thereafter, the unselfconscious weight of a small hand on my leg. I looked down to see a head covered in feathery blonde hair, not the dark, curly brown of my own child. The wispy tufts belonged to another small child who had clearly mistaken me for her mother. In that brief moment before she looked up, before we both acknowledged our separateness, I felt overcome with the awareness—as though received through the touch of her hand on my leg—that her mother loved her in the way that I love my child, and that, despite how unbelievably special my not-Josie is, my love for her is not. It is precious, but also universal. Intellectually, of course, this is not a profound realization. Emotionally touching into it is, on the other hand, equally as brutal as it is beautiful. Next to this small child, healthy and safe, I felt her preciousness, but also that of all the children flooding my news feed every day who are not healthy and safe, and whose parents must live with this love and that devastation. Care and consciousness On the back cover, Klara and the Sun is described as a story about love, which it undeniably is. But I think it’s perhaps more fundamentally about care. Care is arguably more tangible than love, but also more complex, friction-prone, messy. It’s the gaps, the mistakes, the harms, the repairs, the precarity, the forgiving, that make care so deeply painful and so deeply nourishing. Nourishing enough to sustain the vulnerability of love. It is care that drives Josie’s mother to have her “lifted” in an age-old attempt of desperation to give her child what she perceives as the best chance at a full life in a rapidly reorganizing world. It is care that sends the Mother down the path of trying to find a way to have Klara “continue” Josie’s life, even though she has already tried and failed to recreate her first child’s consciousness in an artificial intelligence through the work of an artist and scientist named Capaldi. It is care that allows the Mother—despite her cold exterior—to fearlessly comfort her child as she confronts the possibility of her own death, enveloping her, rocking her, soothing her through the unimaginable. It is the Mother’s human care, not her cowardice, that makes her love true. There is so much in Klara and the Sun that is undeniably relevant in this particular social, cultural, and technological moment, so many ways in which Ishiguro’s Josie and my not-Josie’s world are suddenly uncomfortably proximate, but it is what Klara herself affirms about humanity that is most resonant. At the end of the novel, Klara reflects on what she’s learned. She did not have to become a Josie 2.0, for Josie survived. Klara is glad for this, of course, believing that it was her bargain with the Sun (which she sees as a kind of god) that saved Josie. Sitting discarded in a junk yard, Klara is increasingly convinced that the desperate plan to turn Josie into a digital consciousness wouldn’t have worked: Mr. Capaldi believed there was nothing special inside Josie that couldn’t be continued. He told the Mother he’d searched and searched and found nothing like that. But I believe now he was searching in the wrong place. There was something very special, but it wasn’t inside Josie. It was inside those who loved her. That’s why I think now Mr. Capaldi was wrong and I wouldn’t have succeeded. It is what happens within us and between us when we care for another being that differentiates us as humans. In other words, it’s not so much what’s there but what’s not there—the space between the person in front of us and how we perceive, imperfectly care for, and ultimately love them—that makes us human. Through Klara’s eyes, I was able to see more clearly what connects the Mother and her Josie to me and my not-Josie. It is the very fact that we can so wholeheartedly love another being, without being them, having them, solving them, knowing every bit of them. It is the fact that still, we carry them in our hearts, and somehow we are not heavier for it.

#9759. How to Stay Informed But Not Overwhelmed by Election News

📅 25 Oct 2026 | Category: other | Source
You pick up your phone to check one thing—perhaps the weather, a text message, or the score of a game. A few minutes later, you have read three election headlines, watched a clip from a debate, skimmed dozens of comments, and somehow learned that democracy, the economy, your health care, and the future of the planet may all be in danger before breakfast. If this sounds familiar, you are not alone. Election season can make the news feel unusually personal. The issues on the ballot can affect our finances, health, schools, communities, and rights. At the same time, the way we now encounter political information—constantly, emotionally, and often in fragments—can leave us feeling overloaded rather than informed. There is even a name for this experience: election stress. The Mayo Clinic describes it as the overwhelming anxiety some people feel during an election cycle. And surveys from the American Psychological Association suggest that elections have become a significant source of stress for many Americans. In the lead-up to the 2024 presidential election, for example, 72% of adults said they were worried that the results could lead to violence, and 56% said they feared the election could mark the end of democracy in the United States. It is understandable to feel unsettled when an election seems to carry serious consequences. But anxiety is not always a problem to get rid of; in small doses, it can direct our attention toward what matters. It may motivate us to learn, vote, volunteer, have difficult conversations, or support people affected by political decisions. The problem begins when information meant to help us engage instead keeps our nervous systems in a constant state of alarm. Why elections can feel so stressful Part of election stress comes from the uncertainty it elicits. We do not know who will win, what policies will change, or how the people around us will respond. Research suggests that uncertainty itself can be stressful because people naturally want to understand and predict the environment around them. When we feel unable to do that, we may feel less safe and less in control. News can intensify that uncertainty. Negative news is often more attention-grabbing than positive news, and conflict, danger, and outrage make especially compelling headlines. That does not mean journalists are intentionally trying to harm us. Reporting on threats and problems is an essential part of journalism. But a steady diet of alarming information can shape how we feel about the world, especially when it leaves little room for context, solutions, or signs of human cooperation. This can create a difficult cycle. When people feel anxious, they often seek out more information in an effort to regain a sense of control. Yet the more election news they encounter—particularly news marked by uncertainty, conflict, and emotional intensity—the more anxious they may feel. Then they check again. In other words, we may turn to the news to calm ourselves down, only to leave feeling more activated than before. Why today’s media environment makes it harder Political stress is not new. But the way we receive political information has changed dramatically. When John F. Kennedy and Richard Nixon debated on television in 1960, viewers watched in a far more contained media environment. There was no endless stream of clips, reactions, memes, push notifications, and commentary arriving at every hour of the day. People could discuss the debate afterward, but they were not also carrying the debate around in their pockets. Today, election news can follow us almost everywhere: to the grocery store line, the waiting room, the car, the dinner table, and bed. Social media makes it easier for people to participate politically and amplify voices that were once excluded from public conversation. Those are meaningful benefits. But the same technology can make political information feel impossible to put down. It also encourages us to consume news in small, emotionally potent pieces. A headline while brushing our teeth. A video clip while waiting for coffee. A dramatic post at a stoplight. These snippets can provoke strong feelings without giving us enough context to understand the story fully. That matters because headlines often tell us what to feel before they tell us what happened. Pew research found that five out of eight Americans (63%) said they only read news headlines rather than full news articles, specifically when scrolling through social media. When people repeatedly take in emotionally charged fragments of news, they may feel a growing sense of threat while having less detailed information to guide their judgments. When stress turns into news avoidance Faced with this constant emotional demand, some people keep checking the news compulsively. Others decide they have had enough and stop paying attention altogether. News avoidance can offer temporary relief. If every update makes you tense, angry, or helpless, closing the app may feel like self-protection. In fact, a report from the American Press Institute found that a third of Americans want to disengage from news because it feels stressful and overwhelming. But avoiding news altogether comes with costs, especially during an election year. It can make it harder to participate in democratic life, understand issues that affect our communities, or distinguish reliable reporting from misinformation passed along by friends, influencers, or highly partisan accounts. The goal, then, is not to consume more news or no news. It is to develop a healthier relationship with news: one that helps us stay informed without asking our nervous systems to be on call all day. Three ways to stay informed—and calm 1. Choose a news source that includes solutions, not only problems. During stressful times, it can help to seek out reporting that does more than identify what is going wrong. Constructive journalism still covers serious problems, but it also provides context, examines solutions, and asks what people and communities can do next. This approach is not about pretending everything is fine. It is about giving readers a more complete picture: What is the problem? Who is affected? What is being tried? What can citizens realistically do? Solution-oriented reporting may be especially helpful during elections because it can replace helplessness with a more grounded sense of agency and hope for the future. When you choose a news source, consider asking: Does this reporting leave me better informed? Does it help me understand the issue, or does it only make me feel alarmed? Does it explain possible solutions as well as the threat? 2. Balance distressing news with inspiring media. We do not need to force ourselves to be positive about difficult realities. But we can balance the emotional weight of election coverage with media that remind us of human goodness, beauty, courage, and connection. Research suggests that stories of kindness, moral courage, and collective care can evoke feelings such as elevation, gratitude, and hope. These emotions can broaden our view of what is possible and help us feel more connected to other people. And new research from the University of Santa Barbara suggests that these emotions can even reduce stress. This might mean watching a short video about neighbors helping one another after a disaster, reading a profile of local organizers working across differences, listening to music that helps you settle, or returning to a film or show that leaves you feeling more hopeful about people, or nostalgic about the past. Think of this as building a more balanced media diet. Election news can be an important part of that diet, but it should not be the only thing we feed our minds. Just as eating one healthy meal a week does not transform our physical health, one inspiring story will not undo chronic stress. Over time, though, regularly choosing media that restore us, physically and mentally, can help cultivate resilience, hope, and perspective. 3. Create boundaries around when you check the news. Finally, consider treating news less like a snack and more like a meal. Snacking on news all day can mean consuming a lot without ever feeling nourished. You absorb a headline here, an alert there, and a few comments in between—but never get enough context to make sense of what you are seeing. The result can be a lot of emotional calories and very little understanding. Instead, choose one or two intentional times to engage with the news. Is it a bad idea to work out when sick?

Sleep is the best way to help your body recover quickly when you’re sick. But if you’re determined to rise and grind, you could use the “neck check” to decide whether to lace up those sneakers or stay in bed.

Many sports doctors use this theory to say that if your symptoms are above the neck — sniffles, sore throat, or a mild headache — you’re generally good to go for a light workout. But if you’re dealing with chest congestion, a hacking or juicy cough, diarrhea, a fever, or an upset stomach, it’s better to take some time to catch up on your Netflix watchlist.

Big disclaimer: there’s little scientific evidence behind the “neck check,” so use your best judgment. 

When is it safe to exercise?

If you’re going with the above-the-neck rule, exercising with the following symptoms is probably okay.

Mild cold

Going super hard if you have a mild cold is not a good idea. But a light workout, like a walk or a chill Peloton sesh, can be a great way to stay active without overdoing it. Stretching or light yoga might also do the trick. 

Minor earache

Stick to light activities like walking for most earaches. Avoid intense workouts like weightlifting, yoga, or pilates, as they can throw off your balance and put pressure on your sinuses, exacerbating the pain.

Mild sore throat

Light and short workouts are likely fine if your sore throat is mild and lacks fever, cough, or swallowing issues. Remember to stay hydrated — it can ease your sore throat and is beneficial during exercise.

Stuffy nose

Light activities like brisk walks or gentle bike rides can help for allergy-related stuffy noses. If your nose is blocked, stick to very light activities, focusing on breathing. Skip workouts if you have a fever, chest congestion, or a productive cough with phlegm until you’re symptom-free.

When you shouldn’t work out

If you’re experiencing the following symptoms, the workout can wait.

Fever

Working out with a fever increases dehydration risk and can worsen the fever. Plus, fever saps your muscle strength and coordination, making you more prone to injuries. So, do yourself a favor and skip the workout until you’re back to normal. 

Cough

An occasional cough is your body’s way of clearing out irritants, but a frequent or phlegmy cough could signal a respiratory infection like a cold, flu, or even pneumonia or asthma. While a tickle-induced cough isn’t a gym deal-breaker, a persistent one is.

Stomach bug

Diarrhea and vomiting can dehydrate you, and physical activity only makes that dehydration worse. Feeling weak? That’s a recipe for injury. Plus, stomach bugs are highly contagious. If you feel antsy, stick to light stretching or yoga at home.

Flu

The flu hits your respiratory system hard, with symptoms like fever, chills, sore throat, body aches, fatigue, headache, cough, and congestion. If you’ve got the flu, take it easy and give your body some well-earned R&R.

When can you workout again?

Once symptom-free, ease back into exercise gradually. Start with less intense workouts to avoid overdoing it. As your symptoms fade, you can gradually return to normal activity.

Regular exercise may shorten recovery time, particularly for acute respiratory infections. Listen to your body’s signals to gauge your strength. If you’re still contagious, opt for home or outdoor workouts, so you don’t spread those germs to fellow gym-goers.

Takeaway

You probably shouldn’t workout if you have:

  • a fever
  • a bad cough
  • stomach bug
  • flu

You may be able to exercise if you’re dealing with something minor, such as a:

  • mild cold
  • minor earache
  • mild sore throat
  • stuffy nose

P.S. Check in with a healthcare provider if you’re unsure when it’s safe to start exercising again.
