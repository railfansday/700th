On the election night of November 6, 2012, then-president Barack Obama clinched his second term by winning Ohio over Republican Mitt Romney. Fourteen years later, Obama returned to Ohio on the weekend to help the Democratic candidates for governor and senator win their races. He was greyer, sleeves rolled up like a man prepared to get his hands dirty, and with the familiar opener: “Hello Ohio!” Music from U2 and Springsteen energised the air. It felt like the old days. It was how he campaigned.

But throughout this second term of Donald Trump’s presidency, many Democrats were unsettled if not angry at Obama, and at former president Bill Clinton, for being absent and not taking the field to tackle Trump when he was abusing his presidency by violating his oath of office and exercising unprecedented executive power.

The two former presidents did not serve as opposition leaders when Trump dismantled the education department and foreign aid programs, firing hundreds of thousands of government employees in the process. They were barely visible when Trump’s legislation, passed by the Republican-dominated Congress, provided trillion-dollar tax cuts for the wealthiest, cut health insurance premiums and Medicaid and food stamps for tens of millions of Americans, gutted clean energy programs, added $5 trillion to the national debt ... The list is long.

When Trump initiated, together with Israel’s Benjamin Netanyahu, his war of choice against Iran in February – fancifully to destroy a nuclear program that Obama had placed under complete control in 2015 – neither former president told the nation about the profound mistake that was being made.

Trump is trapped in a perfect storm, and he cannot escape it. His approval ratings are at a record low: 31 per cent. The economy is punishing households across the country. Inflation is rising. Interest rates are going up. The war in Iran is unpopular and unresolved. Farmers are hurting from tariffs and high diesel prices. Trump is underwater on his management of key issues: health care, trade and tariffs, cost of living, immigration, foreign wars.

There is a wave of discontent across most of the country – even in states that Trump carried by double digits just two years ago. The future is uncertain. Obama told those gathered in Cleveland, “People are so ready for something new, so hungry for something better, so sick and tired of being sick and tired, that everywhere you go it feels like change is in the air.”

Obama argued that everything costs more, from gas to mortgages. And that “Trump is directly responsible for inflation … That he made a series of bad decisions and an unnecessary war no one voted for … This is why the price of everything is up.” And why Trump’s approval rating is down.

But then, on this 643rd day of Trump being in power, Obama took on Trump personally. “Does anybody in the White House think this guy in the White House is spending all this time thinking about these issues?”

Obama then turned to Trump’s “home renovations”. “There’s gold everywhere. What’s up with the gold? He has the White House looking like Saddam Hussein opening a Cheesecake Factory.” Obama drilled down on corruption, which has emerged as a major issue in this election. Trump’s “grifting on your dime. Selling favours”.

Obama has never been so personal with Trump. And if you attack Trump on these terms – even though Trump always attacks everyone he opposes on such terms, from female journalists to foreign leaders in countries allied with the United States – he comes back at you with a vengeance. And it will be more than simply referring to his predecessor as “Barack Hussein Obama”.

In getting personal, did Obama run the risk of mocking not just Trump but his supporters? Was he counterintuitively galvanising Trump’s flock by mocking congregants in the MAGA church? Isn’t the tone of sneering intellectualism from the establishment one of the reasons Trump came to power in the first place?

Scott Jennings is a conservative CNN political contributor and a leading contender to become the next White House press secretary. Moments after Obama left the Ohio stage, Jennings posted that Obama, “Makes speeches for 500k a pop. Hasn’t eaten dinner that costs less than $250 in years. Has never been in a Cheesecake Factory. Undefeated at condescending to regular folks. Will always be known for a presidency that ushered in the Trump era to begin with.”

Actually, a majority of voters did not go for Trump in 2016. Yes, the economy and the free trade agreements were not delivering enough good paying jobs and economic security for hard-working households. But the fact remains that Hillary Clinton received 3 million more votes than Trump – but lost the electoral college, including Ohio, which Obama had won by 200,000 votes four years earlier.

Still potent is the Trump team’s focus on the Democrat woke elites – whom he calls radical left socialists and communists – as being out of touch with so many Americans that the Democrats left behind. So Obama lifted his message. “There is a decency that we should expect of our public officials. The standards of behaviour have gotten so low,” and that the occupant of the highest office in the land must not “lie and cheat and bully”. “I’m tired of being angry. I’m tired of being divided.”

Obama concluded: “This election can be a course correction in America’s long journey toward a more perfect union.”

Come next week’s midterms, we will see if Obama’s message hit or missed the target.

Bruce Wolpe is a senior fellow at the University of Sydney’s United States Studies Centre. He has served on the Democratic staff in the US Congress and as chief of staff to former prime minister Julia Gillard.

#35743. Qatar pushes for Iran deal, new US strikes remain possible

📅 25 Oct 2026 | Category: technology | Source

A renewed diplomatic push to avert another round of the US-Iran war gathered pace on Sunday, with Qatari mediators in Tehran as President Donald Trump publicly weighed reaching an agreement with the Islamic Republic against taking further military action.

The Qatari team was in the Iranian capital as part of an effort coordinated with the United States to reduce regional tensions, an official briefed on the matter told Reuters.

The visit puts Doha at the center of an attempt to break a diplomatic deadlock even as the risk of renewed fighting has grown.

The urgency behind that effort was underscored by Trump himself. In an interview with Fox News on Sunday, the US president framed his options in stark terms.

“Do I make a deal with them or do I finish them off? That's the only question,” Trump said.

But Trump also left space for mediators to work. Asked separately on Sunday whether US strikes could resume that day, he said, “There will not be a strike today,” stopping short of ruling out military action later.

Iran, for its part, is also keeping the diplomatic channel alive without signaling that it is prepared to accept Washington's terms.

Foreign Ministry spokesperson Esmaeil Baghaei said on Sunday that messages were continuing to pass between Iran and the United States through intermediaries and that Tehran had not closed the window on diplomacy.

At the same time, Iranian officials have accused Washington of demanding what Tehran characterizes as its “complete surrender.”

The two sides also remain divided over the terms of a broader settlement. Washington has continued to make Iran's nuclear capabilities central to any settlement. Trump told FOX 2 Detroit on Sunday that the war would not end until the United States was satisfied Tehran did not have nuclear weapons capabilities, while saying he was prepared to take time to reach an agreement.

“I'm going to make a great deal. I'm a longterm person; I'm not a short term person,” he said.

Iran's ambassador to Pakistan said on Saturday that Tehran remained willing to resume negotiations under a Pakistan-brokered interim agreement reached in June. He said Iran had offered through Islamabad to reopen the Strait of Hormuz if Washington lifted its naval blockade.

The June agreement, brokered with Qatar's help, was intended to restore shipping, end the blockade and begin negotiations toward a broader agreement addressing Iran's nuclear program.

The Iranian ambassador accused Trump of undermining the latest diplomatic effort by insisting that the blockade would remain in place.

The Qatari intervention follows a broader diplomatic push by regional and European governments concerned that another attack on Iran could expand the war.

Foreign ministers from Turkey, Saudi Arabia, Egypt, Pakistan, Britain, France, Germany and Italy met in Istanbul on Friday, where Turkish Foreign Minister Hakan Fidan warned that renewed attacks on Iran would widen the conflict and said diplomacy remained the only way to end it.

The meetings brought together governments with different relationships with Washington and Tehran but a shared interest in preventing another round of fighting from spreading across the region.

That concern has grown as Iran has threatened retaliation against US and regional interests and attacks on commercial shipping have continued around the Strait of Hormuz.

Seven oil tankers were reported attacked or damaged by Iranian forces or naval mines in and around the strait on Friday. Despite the continuing risks, tankers have been moving oil through a US-protected corridor.

CENTCOM commander Adm. Brad Cooper flew over the Strait of Hormuz on Sunday as the US military said it had supported more than 3,000 commercial vessel transits through the waterway over the past six months and helped Persian Gulf partners export more than 1.5 billion barrels of crude oil since summer.

Cooper said the main shipping routes remained clear of mines laid by Iran's Revolutionary Guards. CENTCOM said more than 95% of vessels had passed safely in recent months despite attacks by the Guards.

The confrontation has also spread to Yemen, where renewed Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia have added to concerns over regional energy infrastructure and shipping routes.

The diplomatic push is therefore taking place while both sides retain significant means of pressure.

Washington continues its naval blockade of Iran while keeping open the possibility of renewed military action. Tehran, meanwhile, retains its ability to threaten shipping through the Strait of Hormuz and US and regional interests.

Those pressures also form part of the bargaining over a possible settlement. Tehran has linked reopening the Strait of Hormuz to an end to the US blockade, while Washington has continued to press Iran over its nuclear program, with the sequencing of concessions remaining a key obstacle.

The result is a diplomatic effort taking place under the threat of escalation rather than after that threat has receded.

For now, both sides continue to signal that an agreement remains possible. But neither has publicly indicated that it is prepared to bridge the differences that have stalled negotiations, leaving Qatar and other mediators seeking an opening while Washington and Tehran prepare for the consequences of another breakdown.

Iran may be approaching a mental-health emergency as prolonged uncertainty over war, economic pressure and the future takes a growing toll on the public, the head of the country’s Psychology and Counseling Organization said on Sunday.

Asked by ILNA how far Iranian society was from a mental-health emergency, Alireza Agha-Yousefi said: “I don’t think we are very far from it.”

He pointed to marriage, divorce and birth rates as areas of concern and said authorities needed to act before psychological and social problems became more severe.

Agha-Yousefi said the prolonged state of neither full-scale war nor peace was itself becoming a major source of pressure.

“The overall condition of society in a situation of neither war nor peace can create significant psychological strain,” he said.

He said people normally try to meet their needs while also responding to threats, but when both are present at the same time, threats tend to dominate.

“Uncertainty about the future is one of the most difficult situations,” he said, adding that it deprives people of the ability to plan their lives.

Agha-Yousefi said prolonged uncertainty could produce severe anxiety and, over time, lead to both physical and psychological exhaustion.

He cautioned that the consequences of intense stress do not always appear immediately.

“The effects may show themselves one or two years later,” he said, citing depression, marital conflict and physical health problems as possible outcomes.

He also pointed to prolonged muscle tension, joint and spinal pain and gastrointestinal problems as conditions that can be associated with chronic stress.

President Masoud Pezeshkian last week also described the current “no war, no peace” situation as the worst position Iran could be in, as tensions with Washington remained unresolved.

Agha-Yousefi said the same economic conditions contributing to psychological strain were also making treatment less accessible.

He said demand for counselling had risen, but inflation and declining purchasing power meant many people could no longer afford psychological services.

“Psychological services are not covered by basic insurance,” he said, adding that official figures on the number of people seeking treatment could therefore give a misleading picture.

He said some people who need help simply do not seek it because of the cost.

A proposal to bring psychological services under insurance coverage had been submitted to the organization’s central council around four months ago but had yet to be approved, he said.

Pressure on families and social stability

Agha-Yousefi also said Iran appeared to be moving toward greater social dislocation.

“It seems our social situation is, to some extent, moving toward anomie or social normlessness,” he said.

He warned that as social stress rises, the effects can spread into family life and wider social behavior, including later marriage, addiction and greater instability within households.

“When problems enter the family, they can be a sign of deeper social damage,” he said.

He added that severe family pressures could, in some cases, contribute to extreme violence, including killings within families.

Agha-Yousefi said delaying preventive measures would make the psychological, physical and social consequences much harder and more expensive to address later.

His comments come as speculation grows over possible renewed US and Israeli military action against Iran.

Recent reports have said the US military has prepared options for further strikes after the November 3 midterm elections if President Donald Trump authorizes them.

That uncertainty has added another layer of pressure to an economy already strained by inflation, a weakening currency and declining purchasing power.

The war with Iran raises a familiar question for Israel: Can battlefield gains produce lasting peace? Its military history offers mixed answers, from unresolved victories to withdrawals and ceasefires that left adversaries armed.

Destroying Iran’s military capabilities and preventing their reconstruction are different objectives, requiring different measures of success.

Since 1948, Israel has fought conventional Arab armies and armed organizations such as Hezbollah and Hamas, and has increasingly confronted Iran directly through missile, drone and air attacks far beyond its borders. These forms of warfare have overlapped, posing different challenges for bringing the fighting to an end.

Their endings show how battlefield outcomes interact with diplomacy, outside pressure and arrangements for security after the fighting.

Fighting between Jewish and Arab communities followed the UN’s 1947 partition plan. After Israel declared independence in May 1948, neighboring Arab armies entered the conflict. Israel survived and gained territory beyond the boundaries proposed for the Jewish state.

In 1949, it signed separate armistice agreements with Egypt, Lebanon, Jordan and Syria. These established lines separating the forces, rather than permanent political settlements. The Egyptian agreement explicitly envisaged a subsequent peaceful settlement.

Israel’s survival did not resolve the territorial dispute or the displacement of Palestinians. The fighting ended without peace between Israel and its neighbors.

1956: Military gains, withdrawal under pressure

During the Suez crisis, Israel attacked Egypt in coordination with Britain and France, rapidly advancing across Sinai.

Pressure from the United States, the Soviet Union and the United Nations forced a withdrawal, completed in March 1957. Israel could not retain the territory it had captured, despite its battlefield success.

The outcome nevertheless brought security gains: Israel regained access to the Straits of Tiran, while a UN emergency force deployed in Sinai. The US State Department’s historical account distinguishes those gains from the territorial withdrawal.

The episode illustrates why withdrawal is not automatically evidence of military defeat, and why military superiority alone does not determine a war’s political outcome.

1967: Six days of fighting, decades of consequences

Israel’s opening airstrikes devastated Egypt’s air force. Within six days, Israeli forces had captured Sinai, Gaza, the West Bank, East Jerusalem and the Golan Heights.The war ended through ceasefires, without a formal surrender by the defeated Arab states. Israel’s decisive battlefield victory transformed the territorial balance but did not settle the conflict.

UN Security Council Resolution 242 subsequently established a framework linking Israeli withdrawal from occupied territory with an end to belligerency and recognition of states’ right to live within secure, recognized boundaries.

The military result was immediate. Disputes over occupation, borders and Palestinian rights lasted far beyond the fighting.

1973: From surprise attack to a peace treaty

Egypt and Syria attacked Israeli forces in Sinai and the Golan Heights in October 1973, initially making substantial gains. Israel counterattacked and crossed the Suez Canal before US-Soviet diplomacy and UN ceasefire resolutions helped stop the fighting.

The US State Department’s history describes the war as opening the way for Henry Kissinger’s shuttle diplomacy and, ultimately, the Egyptian-Israeli peace treaty. Egypt did not recover all Sinai through military action, but the war helped break the diplomatic stalemate.

The 1978 Camp David accords provided the framework for the 1979 treaty. Israel agreed to withdraw from Sinai in exchange for peace with Egypt. The main withdrawal was completed in 1982; the disputed Taba area was returned in 1989 following arbitration.

Egypt recovered territory through a combination of war and diplomacy. Israel exchanged territorial control for a political agreement with a major adversary.

Lebanon, 1982–2000: Initial success, prolonged conflict

Israel’s 1982 invasion drove into Beirut, where US-mediated arrangements secured the departure of Palestine Liberation Organization leaders and fighters. This was an evacuation from Beirut, not the elimination of every Palestinian armed presence in Lebanon.

Israel withdrew much of its force in 1985 but retained a southern security zone alongside the allied South Lebanon Army. Its remaining troops left in May 2000, following years of conflict with Hezbollah. UNIFIL’s historical account records both withdrawals.

The withdrawal was unilateral, without a peace treaty or formal Israeli surrender. Hezbollah portrayed it as a victory; Israel ended a prolonged deployment without resolving the underlying confrontation.

The departure of one armed organization’s leadership in 1982 had not produced a lasting end to fighting.

The 2006 war followed Hezbollah’s cross-border attack and capture of two Israeli soldiers. Israel launched extensive air and ground operations, but Hezbollah remained armed and continued firing rockets during the conflict.

Neither side’s survival or military performance alone explains the settlement. The operative mechanism was UN Security Council Resolution 1701, adopted on August 11, followed by a cessation of hostilities on August 14.

It called for Hezbollah to stop attacks, Israel to stop offensive operations, and Israeli forces to withdraw as Lebanese troops and an expanded UNIFIL deployed. It envisaged an area between the Blue Line and Litani River free of armed personnel and weapons other than those of the Lebanese government and UNIFIL.

Hezbollah was not disarmed. Nevertheless, UNIFIL says the arrangements helped sustain almost 17 years of relative calm before the escalation beginning in 2023. An incomplete settlement still had significant practical effects.

The Gaza wars of 2008–09, 2012, 2014 and 2021 ended without Hamas surrendering or losing all its military capabilities.

Scholars Efraim Inbar and Eitan Shamir described Israel’s approach as “mowing the grass”: periodically degrading an adversary’s capabilities and restoring deterrence rather than expecting a permanent political resolution through force. Their study appeared online in 2013 and in the Journal of Strategic Studies in 2014.

After Hamas’s October 7, 2023 attack, Israel set broader objectives, including dismantling its military and governing capabilities and recovering the hostages. Shamir argued that the attack marked a departure from the previous limited-campaign approach. That was his strategic assessment, rather than proof that eliminating Hamas was achievable.

The ensuing war devastated Gaza. The October 2025 ceasefire did not settle its future: a year later, the UN human rights office was still reporting killings despite the agreement.

Lebanon after 2023: Military damage and state authority

Israel’s 2024 campaign killed Hezbollah leader Hassan Nasrallah and other senior commanders, severely damaging its leadership. The fighting nevertheless moved toward a negotiated cessation of hostilities rather than formal surrender.

The US-French-brokered arrangement took effect on November 27, 2024, and again rested on implementing Resolution 1701, Lebanese state authority and Israeli withdrawal.

Disarmament remained contested. In October 2026, The Wall Street Journal reported that Hezbollah demanded the return of explosives seized by Lebanese authorities, illustrating the continuing struggle over weapons outside state control.

Iran: Ending operations or preventing reconstruction?

The June 2025 war involved Israeli attacks on Iranian military and nuclear targets and direct US strikes on Fordow, Natanz and Isfahan. US involvement therefore preceded the broader war of 2026.

Israel’s Foreign Ministry described the nuclear threat as “existential and imminent” in its account of the operation. That characterization was Israel’s stated justification, not an independently established finding that Iran possessed nuclear weapons. The same document records Israel’s acceptance of a US-proposed ceasefire on June 24.The broader confrontation in 2026 extended to shipping and energy supplies. By October, the US naval blockade and the standoff over reopening the Strait of Hormuz remained unresolved.

Netanyahu has explicitly advocated moving beyond containment. Addressing military cadets in February 2026, he declared there would be “no more containment of threats” and emphasized preemptive action. This supports describing a shift in his stated approach, without assuming a uniform doctrine across every Israeli institution.

Destroying launchers or facilities has measurable immediate effects. Preventing their reconstruction depends on industrial capacity, supply networks, political decisions and whatever monitoring or enforcement follows.

Within the approach of seeking to prevent Iran from rebuilding its military threat, two scenarios can be considered. Neither is a prediction or an exhaustive account of how the conflict might end.

The first is a military defeat sufficiently extensive to destroy much of Iran’s offensive capability and substantially constrain its reconstruction. Lasting results would depend on measures that prevent replenishment, rather than destruction alone.The second combines military defeat with the end of the Islamic Republic and its replacement by a political system that abandons hostility toward Israel and support for armed proxies.

That scenario depends on more than a change of leadership. It assumes a successor government both chooses and can enforce a different policy, including ending financial, military and operational support for allied armed groups. A government’s collapse would not itself establish those conditions.

The scenarios may overlap, but neither guarantees stability. Their outcomes would depend on political authority, security arrangements and the capacity to enforce commitments after combat ends.

Other possible outcomes include a negotiated agreement that constrains Iran’s capabilities without changing its government, or a prolonged armed standoff sustained by deterrence. Neither would necessarily eliminate the underlying hostility, though either could bring the current fighting to an end.

Israel’s earlier wars provide different benchmarks: territorial withdrawal under international pressure in 1956, land exchanged for peace with Egypt, unilateral withdrawal from Lebanon, and ceasefire arrangements that reduced fighting without disarming Hezbollah. Each ended a particular phase of conflict; only some produced a broader political settlement.

Baghdad’s pledge to stay out of renewed fighting is being tested by reported Israeli jet flights toward Iran through Iraqi airspace, suspected strikes on militia stores reportedly holding Chinese-made air defenses, and drone attacks on Iranian Kurdish opposition bases.

Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi says Iraq will not participate in any war, while Iran-backed factions describe supporting Tehran against the United States and Israel as a duty, raising questions over Baghdad’s ability to control how its territory and airspace are used.

“Iraq will not participate in any war,” Zaidi told state-run Al-Iraqiya television on Friday, saying Baghdad wanted to remain a meeting point for regional rivals and was working to ease the confrontation between Iran and the United States.

On Saturday, the Islamic Resistance in Iraq, an umbrella of Iran-aligned armed factions, declared that standing with Tehran was a moral duty and portrayed the conflict as a struggle between righteousness and falsehood, Rudaw reported.

Trump initially welcomed the prospect of Kurdish fighters crossing into Iran, telling Reuters on March 5 that it would be “wonderful.” Two days later, he said he had told them to stay out.

“They’re willing to go in, but we really—I’ve told them I don’t want them to go in,” Trump told reporters. “The war is complicated enough without having, getting the Kurds involved.”

Nazanin Sharifian, an Iran-born former Mossad officer running for Israel’s parliament with Gadi Eisenkot’s Yashar party, told Iran International that Israel should step up military and economic pressure on the Islamic Republic’s leaders and work to end their rule.

“We must do everything we can to bring down the leaders of the Islamic Republic, so this regime is gone and the people of Iran are free,” she told Iran International’s Babak Eshaghi.

Sharifian, a Persian speaker who was born in Iran in 1982 and lived there until she was seven, is a candidate for the Knesset in Tuesday’s election. If Yashar joins the next coalition, she could hold a role in government.

Sharifian called the Islamic Republic Israel’s greatest enemy and an enemy of Iranians as well. Iran’s wealth, she said, goes to Hezbollah and the Houthis instead of to the people it belongs to.

Asked what Yashar’s policy toward Tehran would be, she said it would continue the course set by Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, Eisenkot’s main rival.

“We must intensify this pressure on the leaders of the Islamic Republic,” she said. “We do not want to put pressure on the people of Iran. The people of Iran are the last hope of Iran.”

She called for every available means to be used to end the Islamic Republic’s rule. “I have great faith in the people of Iran,” she said.

Her emphasis on pressure differs from her party leader’s. Eisenkot, a former military chief, said this month that the Islamic Republic’s fall is necessary but cannot be achieved by military action, and that it must come from within Iranian society.

Sharifian was born into a Jewish family. After the 1979 revolution, some relatives left for the United States and Israel, but her father, who ran businesses in Tehran and Isfahan, refused at first to leave. The family fled when she was seven.

In Israel she served in the army, then in the Shin Bet internal security service and the Mossad, Israel’s foreign intelligence agency. Her Persian, she said, was one reason she was moved to the Mossad. She has since taught Persian and Middle East strategy to hundreds of Israeli students.

“I am very proud that my being is Iranian,” she said. Representing Iranians in Israel would be a great honor, she said, adding: “I expect to speak Persian in the Israeli parliament in the future.”

She hopes to bring more people of Iranian origin into Israel’s state institutions, she said, and that one day Iran will be returned to its people.

In 1987, with Khomeini still calling for Israel’s destruction, Israel’s defense minister called Iran its best friend. Today every candidate for prime minister wants the Islamic Republic gone. Israel’s leaders have changed their minds about Iran’s rulers, but seldom disagreed.

That is the thread through 47 years of Israeli policy toward the Islamic Republic, and it runs straight into next Tuesday’s election. Whoever wins, the goal will be the same. What changes is how hard, how openly and with whom Israel pursues it.

Before 1979, Iran was Israel’s most important partner in the region. Under David Ben-Gurion’s “periphery doctrine,” Israel courted non-Arab neighbors to outflank hostile Arab states, and the Shah’s Iran was the prize.

Tehran recognized Israel de facto in 1950, sold it oil through a jointly owned pipeline, and let its intelligence services work with Israel’s Mossad. Israeli engineers, farmers and arms dealers worked across Iran. The relationship was discreet, profitable and close.

The revolution ended it overnight. Islamic Republic's founder Rouhollah Khomeini cut ties, and Israel’s mission in Tehran was handed to the Palestine Liberation Organization.

Israel’s first leaders after 1979 did not try to bring the new order down. They sold it weapons.

When Iraq invaded Iran in 1980, Menachem Begin’s government shipped arms to Tehran, and Israeli sales ran to an estimated $500 million between 1981 and 1983, according to Tel Aviv University’s Jaffe Institute. Saddam Hussein was the threat, and Iran was the counterweight. In 1985 and 1986, under Shimon Peres, Israel was the go-between in the Iran-Contra affair, passing American missiles to Tehran.

Yitzhak Rabin, then defense minister, explained the logic in October 1987. “Iran is Israel’s best friend and we do not intend to change our position in relation to Tehran, because Khomeini’s regime will not last forever,” he said. The expectation that the Islamic Republic would fall was already there. Israel simply did not think at the time that it was its job to hasten it.

Rabin was also the one who turned. Back as prime minister in 1992, he received the first intelligence reports on an Iranian nuclear program and concluded, aides recalled, that only a nuclear Iran could pose an existential threat Israel could not answer. Part of his case for peace with the Palestinians and Jordan was to clear the near front before facing the far one.

Ariel Sharon took the same view further. In November 2002, as the United States prepared to invade Iraq, he told The Times of London that Iran should be put under pressure “the day after” Baghdad fell, calling it a center of world terror.

As prime minister he carried aerial photographs of Iranian sites to George W. Bush and said Israel “cannot accept” a nuclear Iran. Ehud Olmert, his successor, kept the military option on the table while, according to later reporting, the first cyberattacks on Iran’s centrifuges were set in motion with Washington.

None of them spoke of regime change. Their target was the bomb.

Benjamin Netanyahu made Iran his signature cause. Across 15 years in office before 2021, he drew a cartoon bomb at the United Nations, addressed the US Congress against the 2015 nuclear deal, and displayed an archive of Iranian nuclear documents his agents had smuggled out of Tehran.

He also began speaking past the government to the people. He recorded messages to Iranians, offering water technology during the drought and insisting that Israel’s quarrel was “not with the Iranian people.”

The message was new: the problem is not Iran, it is the people who rule it.

Naftali Bennett, prime minister in 2021 and 2022, turned that view into a doctrine. Israel, he argued, had spent decades fighting the arms of the octopus, Hezbollah and Hamas, while leaving the head untouched.

He said he launched about 30 operations against the Islamic Republic with the Mossad and other agencies, not all of them military, and predicted it would collapse like the Soviet Union.

Yair Lapid, who succeeded him for six months, spent his term lobbying against a revived nuclear deal.

Since returning to power at the end of 2022, Netanyahu has moved from hoping to aiming. In June 2025, as Israeli jets struck Iran in the 12-day war, he told Iranians that “as we achieve our objective, we are also clearing the path for you to achieve your freedom.”

The war that began on February 28 this year, fought with the United States, killed former Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei. Last week Netanyahu went further than any Israeli leader before him: “This regime must fall and I will topple it.”

Netanyahu, leading Likud, has made bringing down the Islamic Republic his campaign’s primary objective and says he has told the security services to work toward it. He has also said he opposes a preemptive strike before the vote.

Gadi Eisenkot, the former military chief whose Yashar party is Likud’s main rival, says the Islamic Republic’s fall is necessary but cannot be achieved by force. “This must come from within the Iranian people,” he said, and he would strike again only if Tehran raced for a bomb.

Bennett and Lapid, running together on the Together list, want to hit harder, not softer. Bennett says attacks by Tehran’s allies should be answered on Iranian soil. Lapid has called for striking Iran’s energy infrastructure, including the Kharg Island oil terminal.

Avigdor Lieberman of Yisrael Beiteinu has long called for overthrowing the Islamic Republic and says past efforts fell short. Itamar Ben-Gvir of the far-right Jewish Power backs military operations until Israel’s goals are met. Mansour Abbas, whose Ra’am party draws Arab Israeli voters, has set out no position on Iran.

From Rabin’s prediction that Khomeini’s rule would not last to Netanyahu’s promise to end it himself, the conclusion has barely moved. What Israelis will choose on Tuesday is not whether to want the Islamic Republic gone, but how loudly to say it and how much to risk to get there.
