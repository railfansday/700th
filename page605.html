#35820. Inside Prometheus: Meta’s Journey to a Gigawatt-Scale Supercluster

📅 19 Oct 2026 | Category: technology | Source

Right now, in New Albany, Ohio, Meta is undertaking its most ambitious AI infrastructure project to date. Prometheus, our gigawatt-scale supercluster, is an advancement in our infrastructure that is training the next generation of foundational models that will bring new AI experiences to billions of people.

Two years ago, New Albany was a conventional data center site running web traffic. With advancements in hardware, networking and building design, we reworked this site to be home to training programs for frontier models. We’re building the supercluster in phases. The first phase has already been completed and was used to train our Muse foundational models, including the Muse Wonder models, our new frontier models that deliver state-of-the-art performance on personal and professional agentic tasks.

Large training jobs require everything to work optimally to sustain their performance; an issue on a single host can slow down or stop the entire job, lowering its ‘goodput’. By focusing on improving our systems’ stability and reliability we were able to achieve more than 85% goodput for pre-training runs and more than 90% goodput for reinforcement learning (RL) runs of Muse Wonder. During these training runs, we were able to achieve an overall wall-clock mean time between interruptions (MTBI) of ~47k GPU hours. Since then, we have landed further changes across our fleet including firmware fixes to increase MTBI to more than 54K GPU hours, and we are constantly improving it.

Now, we’re continuing to scale Prometheus with our next frontier models from Meta Superintelligence Labs in mind.

The Prometheus supercluster is housed in Meta’s New Albany, Ohio, data center.

Today, Prometheus comprises an array of hundreds of thousands of GPUs across multiple interconnected buildings, including traditional and AI-optimized data center buildings and rapid deployment structures, all tightly interconnected by miles of fiber and functioning as a single machine.

This capacity allows us to pursue larger models and longer context windows than were previously practical. At full scale, Prometheus will comprise roughly 1 million distributed GPUs, all functioning in lockstep.

Prometheus currently deploys two NVIDIA GPU platforms, with a third (based on NVIDIA’s Vera Rubin NVL72) arriving soon. Each represents a generational leap in performance and efficiency, leveraging our NVL72 rack architecture where 72 GPUs act as a single accelerator within the same network fabric.

Our first platform, Catalina, leverages the NVIDIA GB200 NVL72. We deploy the Ariel variant, a two-rack configuration in which 36 GPUs per rack are interconnected via NVLink to form a unified 72-GPU domain. Catalina provides improvements in raw compute and memory capacity compared to Grand Teton H100. It leverages our Open Rack V3 (ORv3) high-power rack (HPR) structure, an open hardware design contributed to the Open Compute Project (OCP).

The Ariel variant of GB200 NVL72 pairs each NVIDIA Blackwell GPU with an NVIDIA Grace CPU and 2x NVIDIA ConnectX-7 400G NICs at a 1:1 CPU-to-GPU ratio, letting us deploy 800G of I/O per GPU earlier than the rest of the industry. We chose Ariel because the GB200 NVL72 scaled up nearly every other dimension relative to the NVIDIA H100: FLOPS, HBM bandwidth, scale-up bandwidth, and scale-up domain size, while scale-out bandwidth per GPU would have stayed at 400G. We expected that imbalance to cost performance, so we opted for a configuration that delivers 800G of scale-out per GPU. This also gave us more uniformity of spec as the region expanded over time and 800G scale-out became available.

Our second platform, Clemente, moves to the NVIDIA GB300 NVL72 and improves on Catalina at both the GPU and the platform level. GB300 raises HBM capacity per GPU by 1.5x, from 192 GB to 288 GB, and dense FP4 throughput by a similar factor, while roughly doubling attention (softmax) acceleration. At the platform level, scale-out is no longer the constraint it was on the GB200 NVL72: NVIDIA ConnectX-8 SuperNICs deliver 800G per GPU natively, so the capability we deployed early on Ariel becomes the baseline. That, together with a 2:1 GPU-to-CPU ratio, allows Clemente to pack the full 72-GPU domain into a single rack, streamlining our physical deployment. Clemente can be deployed with our modular Air-Assisted Liquid Cooling (AALC) or use Facility Liquid Cooling (FLC) in our newly designed data centers. Serviceability also improves substantially, with reduced cabling.

In traditional web services, the data center network transports requests and responses between services whose instances typically don’t require high bandwidth, high performance, and tight, lock-step coordination across many servers. Large-scale AI training requires all these, meaning the network that connects them is now an important part of AI workloads. To best address this, in addition to the standard data center network connecting servers, several years ago we added a second dedicated data network based on Ethernet, called the Back-End (BE) network, to enable direct GPU-to-GPU communication both within and across data center buildings.

Large pre-training jobs partition the training into data-parallel model replicas, creating a hierarchy in network usage: Latency-sensitive and bandwidth-sensitive operations concentrate within a replica, while traffic that is more tolerant of higher latency and less bandwidth is scheduled between replicas. Our network is designed to match this hierarchy, with low-latency, high-bandwidth fabrics within buildings and an aggregation network connecting the building fabrics.

We work closely with Meta Superintelligence Labs to ensure the parallelism design, traffic patterns, and communication algorithms and software are optimized to minimize exposure of any network communication. This keeps the GPUs as busy as possible and not waiting on the network.

The Meta team has employed these networking concepts in earlier building-scale, 24K GPU clusters and our region-scale, 100K+ GPU cluster over the last several years, and we took them to a new level of scale and distance in Prometheus.

At the building level, each Ethernet-based fabric interconnects up to 20,736 GPUs through a high-bandwidth, low-latency multi-layer topology. Our initial design used a Disaggregated Scheduled Fabric (DSF) to mitigate the network congestion caused by training collectives that typically have a small number of large, long-lived network flows that collide under traditional load-balancing techniques; it does this by utilizing sub-packet-level multipath routing and packet re-ordering in the fabric along with virtual output queues and credit-based end-to-end flow control.

As Ethernet NIC technology advanced, enabling packet re-ordering at the endpoint, we were able to update to a Non-Scheduled Fabric (NSF) utilizing shallow-buffer 51.2T Ethernet switches, such as Minipack3 with Dynamic Load Balancing and the NVIDIA Spectrum-4 switch ASIC-based Minipack3N with NVIDIA’s Adaptive Routing on ConnectX NICs. Both DSF and NSF are based on Meta’s FBOSS networking stack, giving us the control to rapidly iterate on and optimize the fabrics in Prometheus, and the visibility to operate these clusters at scale.

The Backend Aggregation (BAG) network is a multi-layer hub-and-spoke network that connects building-level fabrics across the Prometheus cluster, spanning 2 km to over 100 km. It is built with Arista 7800-series chassis running deep-buffered Broadcom Jericho3 silicon, delivering multiple Pbps of bandwidth per region pair.

Running pretraining over this network required a dedicated optimization effort with Meta Superintelligence Labs to ensure training was not slowed down, especially given speed-of-light constraints at these distances.

Pulling this all together, the complexity of the Prometheus network involves millions of miles of fiber strand, tens of thousands of switches, and millions of optics — from 2×400G FR4 LITE short-reach, low-cost pluggables inside the buildings to 800G-ZR coherent optics for links beyond ~10 km.

At Prometheus’ scale, every component needs to be monitored for reliability. A single fault anywhere can halt an entire training job and waste valuable compute resources.

Maintaining reliability at this level requires a co-engineering model with NVIDIA that goes beyond a traditional vendor dynamic. Teams are deeply embedded at our data centers, sharing real-time telemetry and running joint triage sessions to decompose every system halt by its root cause.

This attribution-driven accountability model ensures both sides are focused on targeted improvements. While Meta optimizes scheduling and infrastructure, NVIDIA addresses firmware, signal diagnostics, and memory handling. This transparency eliminates ambiguity and drives faster fixes across both organizations.

The transition from the H100 generation’s NVL8 architecture to NVL72 has fundamentally redefined our approach to infrastructure resilience. In the NVL8 era, the scale-up domain — the group of GPUs tightly coupled via NVLink for tensor parallelism — was limited to eight units. With NVL72, that domain expands to 72 GPUs, increasing the fault blast radius by 9X.

The larger scale-up domain is what gives NVL72 its bandwidth advantage, but it also makes the 72-GPU rack the unit of allocation we plan capacity around. Training parallelism maps most efficiently onto power-of-two group sizes, so each NVL72 domain runs a 64-GPU slice and keeps its remaining eight GPUs as in-rack spares. When a GPU fails, a spare takes its place immediately, so the job restarts from its last checkpoint instead of waiting on hardware repair, and the rack stays in the job. The in-rack redundancy pushes the effective rack fault rate down to the floor set by failures that can remove an entire pod; for rarer whole-rack faults we hold roughly 2% additional racks in reserve. For a 32,768 active-GPU training run, this means provisioning 523 NVL72 racks, or 37,656 GPUs, about 13% above the active footprint. Most of that margin (11%) falls out of the 64-of-72 slice geometry rather than being an absolute reliability tax.

Physical complexity further intensifies these reliability requirements. A single NVL72 domain incorporates 5,184 tuned differential-pair signaling links operating at 200 Gbps, each traversing multiple potential points of failure, including GPU SerDes, compute tray PCBs, cable cartridges, and switch tray interfaces. Maintaining consistent signal integrity across this dense fabric — at 200 Gbps per lane — represents an unprecedented engineering challenge at this scale.

Fault recovery addresses how we react to faults to minimize the impact on training time. Depending on the scope (i.e., blast area) of a fault we address it through two mechanisms:

Parallelism-Aware Fault Tolerance (PAFT): When a fault can be isolated to a data-parallel model replica, we reduce the impact of a hardware failure by allowing a single faulty replica to be taken offline, while the remaining cluster repeats that step and continues productive training. The compromised slice simply restarts on available spare hosts and resynchronizes, converting a potential total system halt into a minor, temporary reduction in aggregate throughput.

Checkpointing: When a fault blast area is larger than a data-parallel model replica, the entire job is restarted back to the last checkpoint; frequent checkpointing reduces the amount of work lost in these events. Our distributed checkpointing architecture asynchronously saves the model state — including gradients and optimizer data — to multi-tier storage. We use delta checkpointing and in-memory staging to ensure these saves happen with minimal impact on training throughput.

These two fault recovery mechanisms combine with a sparing strategy that utilizes pre-allocated spare GPU trays within a rack, as well as spare racks, to minimize the time to bring up replacement hardware and reduce the amount of work lost to hardware failures

When training frontier models, infrastructure reliability requires a perspective beyond simple availability. The fundamental challenge lies in maintaining training momentum without frequent system halts. Within Prometheus, we classify an interruption as any fault — from GPU silicon defects and network fabric anomalies to storage bottlenecks or thermal limits — that forces a job to pause and initiate a recovery cycle.

These interruptions introduce a large operational overhead because our workloads are tightly interdependent; a single fault within a 72-GPU domain can affect the entire cluster’s aggregate throughput. This performance loss directly impacts our engineering iteration speed, resulting in less effective compute and longer timelines for delivering advanced AI experiences to billions of people.

During the initial phase of Prometheus in mid-2025, our interruption rates reached a peak of nearly 1,000 events per 8K GPUs daily. Through a year of rigorous cross-functional engineering, we have achieved a 99% reduction in interruptions, enabling the cluster to sustain multiple training jobs of tens of thousands of GPUs in parallel.

Attaining this stability required a granular decomposition of every interruption source, followed by the deployment of targeted engineering solutions for each failure category:

NVLink Signal Integrity: initially the primary bottleneck for GB200 deployments. With over 600,000 NVLink connections, even marginal signal degradation triggers frequent NVLink recovery cycles. We implemented continuous telemetry and automated flagging, utilizing L11 diagnostic burns to isolate weak links before production. Our classification tools distinguish transient faults from persistent signal integrity failures, guiding targeted repairs and component replacement, and we validate repaired systems before returning them to production.

Memory Reliability (HBM and LPDDR): Memory-related faults became a major source of interruptions as we scaled GB300 with higher memory capacities. Working closely with NVIDIA, we used fleet telemetry and detailed error analysis to identify recurring failure signatures, which are mitigated via refined HBM firmware settings, memory scrubbing, an improved error correction algorithm for LPDDR, and pattern-based predictive detection to retire degrading devices before they impact job throughput. We are deploying these changes across the fleet and measuring their impact on interruption rates and hardware replacement demand.

NVLink Switch and Fabric Health: A single switch fault can compromise an entire NVL72 domain. We monitor fabric health at millisecond resolution, utilizing automated recovery paths to isolate faulty components and rebalance traffic across the remaining infrastructure without a job interruption.

Backend Network Events: At the BAG super-spine layer, link events are managed transparently via hardware-based fault detection. Our planar topologies ensure that individual fiber or port failures are rerouted without interrupting the synchronized GPU traffic bursts.

Sustained improvement is driven by our operational infrastructure: A colocated triage team at the New Albany site, a comprehensive stability sprint in March that synchronized fixes across the stack, and a rigorous attribution-driven accountability framework. Every repair undergoes a mandatory quarantine to validate system integrity before returning to the compute pool.

Our reliability north star remains two interruptions per 8K GPUs per day, which is ~100k GPU hours MTBI. Driving a 99% reduction from our peak interruptions rate demonstrates that, at Prometheus’ scale, stability is an engineering challenge with solvable engineering outcomes.

Deploying hundreds of thousands of first-generation accelerators reveals quality issues that are hidden at lower volumes. The GB200 deployment has transformed hardware maturation into a primary engineering discipline for our teams and partners.

Each new platform architecture — from Blackwell to Vera Rubin — incorporates these real-world operational insights, tightening our screening and design specifications at every step.

Maximizing throughput for a given power envelope, minimizing stranded power, and synchronous distributed training introduces a power delivery hurdle. When tens of thousands of GPUs execute compute-heavy phases and communication cycles in unison, they trigger large power transients — sudden load fluctuations of 30-40% within the cluster. Initiating or pausing a major training job can shift loads of more than tens of megawatts in just seconds.

We address this with Dynamo Dimmer, our cluster-wide power management framework that enables intelligent power capping. Instead of hard-stopping workloads, Dimmer gradually adjusts GPU power limits as the cluster nears its electrical ceiling, ensuring training continues uninterrupted. This system is reinforced by rack-level battery backup (BBU) modules that handle immediate spikes, capacitor trays (CBUs) for peak smoothing, facility-level battery energy storage systems (BESS), and deep coordination with utility providers to manage these unique AI profiles.

While our distributed approach has allowed us to build Prometheus quickly, it has also meant navigating site-specific power and cooling constraints across different locations.

Our physical infrastructure incorporates multiple cooling generations to deliver the liquid cooling required by these high-power racks. Prometheus runs on a mix of modular air-assisted liquid cooling (AALC), including our free-air data center buildings and our rapid-deployment structures. Our purpose-built AI-optimized building now uses facility liquid cooling (FLC) for maximum power density.

By integrating leased capacity from strategic partners in addition to our own buildings, we accelerate our deployment timelines and total deployable capacity. Meanwhile, our industrial-grade rapid-deployment structures have redefined the construction playbook, delivering production-ready compute with faster timelines.

In New Albany, we’ve long partnered with local utilities to ensure we are paying the full cost of energy for our site and not adding any unnecessary strain to the local grid. We’ve also partnered with energy developers to match our energy use with new clean and renewable energy.

Meta’s newest AI-optimized data centers use closed-looped, liquid cooling as it is the most efficient way to cool GPU servers, and supports efficient use of water. Direct-to-chip, closed-loop liquid cooling helps save space by allowing us to fit more GPUs on a server rack. We also work to extend our sustainability work by sharing designs for systems like IcePack, our liquid-cooled network rack, with the wider industry through OCP.

We’ll continue to work closely with NVIDIA to expand Prometheus’ capacity with a mix of NVIDIA GB300 NVL72s and NVIDIA Vera Rubin NVL72s.

NVIDIA’s next-generation Vera Rubin NVL72 platform will be a generational leap in per-GPU performance and will integrate directly into Prometheus’s existing network fabric. As the cluster matures, it will deliver even more compute per watt by augmenting its capacity (primarily interconnected GB300s) with Vera Rubin via our upcoming San Miguel platform.

It will feature closed-loop liquid cooling, full grid connectivity with our utility partner Entergy, for which Meta pays the full cost, and next-generation network designs.

#3869. The Robot I Didn’t Expect to Build

📅 19 Oct 2026 | Category: other | Source


Agility Robotics, the company I cofounded out of my robotics lab at Oregon State University in 2015, has just launched Digit 5. It’s designed to be the first humanoid robot that can safely and economically do useful work at scale, allowing us to bring tens of thousands of humanoid workers into the real world. Engineered to lift 23-kilogram bins onto high shelves and with a battery that can run for 90 minutes on a 9-minute charge, Digit 5’s goal is to handle heavy warehouse tasks shift after shift, filling the need for labor in jobs that are too physically stressful or too dull for humans to want to do.

Digit 5 is what Agility has been working toward for over a decade. But the robot is heavy and bulky, lacking the graceful natural motion of its predecessors, and it’s farther than I thought possible from the robot I dreamed of building when I started this company. To deliver a useful commercial product, Agility has taken a path that is very different from what I first imagined.

From ATRIAS to Digit

My dream has always been to build robots that combine locomotion and manipulation to move through the world and interact with it as people do. I seek a mastery of motion, achieved through devices designed to navigate the constraints of physics with elegance. When I started the Dynamic Robotics Laboratory at Oregon State in 2008, we began with a theory derived from decades of work in biomechanics: that a simple “spring-mass” model (a body supported by legs that act like springs) should be able to reproduce every known walking and running gait found in animals.

We put theory into practice by building the bipedal robot ATRIAS—short for “Assume The Robot Is A Sphere,” a joke among physicists about reducing a complex system to its simplest possible form. ATRIAS looked like a large microwave on stilts because it was only intended to demonstrate the physics of human walking and running. This was the first glimmer of achieving my dream; even though ATRIAS wasn’t designed to look like us, it still seemed natural, and even somehow alive, when it settled into a walking gait.

With the success of ATRIAS, the barriers to progress shifted from scientific to engineering and organizational, and a startup company seemed like the way to bridge the gap from a one-off research concept to an actual robotic product. I felt confident of two things: that I would be able to build robots that could go where people go, and that this capability would be incredibly useful. This combination of ambition and naivete is necessary for anyone to found a new company—how hard could it be?

Good advice for starting a company is to understand a customer problem, and then find a way to solve it using your unique capabilities. My cofounders, Damion Shelton and Mikhail Jones, and I did not do this. We relied on our belief that this technology would enable new possibilities and trusted ourselves to figure out the business as we went. So we simply focused on building our first product: the bipedal robot Cassie.

Cassie was the first dynamic bipedal robot that researchers could buy, able to maintain its balance through active, fluid motion rather than careful, rigid steps. It had ostrichlike legs, not because we were imitating ground-running birds, but as a result of engineering strictly for performance. It was not intended to be commercially useful, and had no torso, arms, or head. We sold Cassie robots to Harvard, Caltech, the University of Michigan, the University of California, Berkeley, and a handful of other research institutions, ultimately saturating the market for US $250,000 bipedal research robots after having sold a total of 10 of them.

ATRIAS, Cassie, and Digit 4 EVT [left to right] are on display at Agility Robotics’ RoboFab in Salem, Ore. The 70,000-square-foot facility has the capacity to produce more than 10,000 Digit 5 robots per year.Agility Robotics

Because Cassie’s hardware enabled research into the physics of walking and running, the legged-robot research frontier shifted to control software. Traditionally, engineers controlled legged robots with detailed mathematical models that determined how the machines should move. With Cassie, researchers pioneered the new approach of reinforcement learning for bipedal legged locomotion: A simulated robot learns through trial and error, and the resulting neural network controls the physical machine. This approach is what enables virtually all of the dynamic behaviors that you see on legged robots today, and Cassie looked alive as it recovered from stumbles while climbing stairs, ran 5 kilometers across the Oregon State campus, and broke the robot world record for a 100-meter dash.

Cassie got us closer to being able to move through the world with confidence and grace, but to make it useful, we needed manipulation. After exhaustively exploring different configurations, we found that an upright torso helped the robot balance, while two arms mounted near the top of the torso allowed it to pick up large objects and to catch itself when it fell. Digit 1, which added a torso and arms to the Cassie legs, was our first mobile manipulation robot, and another step on the path toward a product that could do useful work for commercial customers.

Why Digit Began With Warehouse Bins

As we explored the first applications for Digit, potential customers kept asking us: Why legs? And why a humanoid? These were tough questions for me to answer, because they exposed a core business reality: It didn’t matter to Agility Robotics if we solved a cool science problem in a beautiful way. What mattered to the business was solving real problems for customers.

After years of engagement with industry partners, we found one answer in warehouses and manufacturing facilities: moving bins. Bin handling is a clearly defined task where two arms are needed to lift large bins up high and legs are the best way to balance and move in narrow spaces. Because the task can vary widely from one deployment to another, it hasn’t been broadly addressed by single-purpose automation. Yet it was constrained enough to give Digit an economically viable first job while it developed a broader set of skills.

In 2019, Digit began demonstrating its capabilities to do real work. With Ford Motor Company, we showed how Digit 1 could carry packages from an autonomous vehicle to a front porch. We developed Digit 2 and 3 while working with a certain global package delivery company on moving and sorting cardboard boxes. Amazon tested Digit 4 on moving and stacking bins, and it became the world’s first commercially deployed humanoid at the logistics provider GXO. In 2024, Digit appeared on the cover of Time magazine.

Despite Digit’s apparent success, we began to run into conflicts between my dream and the needs of actual customers. We were no longer working with relatively patient university research labs. Agility has to sell robots to survive, and customers will only purchase robots that provide value. If bins weren’t moving, our customers were losing money. We spent a huge amount of time and effort keeping our first deployments operational, which arguably eliminated any profit we made. Yet this also kept our company learning and alive the way no lab demo ever could.

I had no idea how hard it would be, or that running a company would involve so many things that have nothing to do with solving technical problems: deployments across the country; shipping, service, and customer support; fundraising and investor relations; more fundraising; human resources and IT; developing fleet-management software; managing a supply chain; and even more fundraising. I had no experience with any of these things. My career had focused on solving the technical challenges of robots interacting with the world, and it turned out that was only a tiny piece of building a successful robotics company. But Agility was the only path I saw to continuing the technical work and having an impact.

Turning Digit Into a Product

I knew that Agility needed to focus more on robots as a product, but I didn’t know how to make that happen. Hiring the people we needed to run the company meant giving up an immense amount of control, but we had no other choice. We recruited a new executive team with the experience required to commercialize this new technology, and they quickly made changes that were both painful and necessary.

Digit versions 1, 2, and 3 [left to right] show the evolution of the robot from a dynamic walking platform into a useful mobile manipulator. Agility

We had intended the next revision of Digit, Digit 4 EVT (Engineering Validation and Test), to be a relatively minor upgrade. But Agility’s new leadership insisted that the robot was missing key features that were essential if Digit was going to become a real product. So Digit 4 EVT instead incorporated a series of major changes in a highly compressed timeline, such as larger batteries and system-wide upgrades for safety and regulatory compliance, which added enough mass to dramatically alter the robot’s appearance and capabilities.

While early Digits were headless, Agility Robotics was able to justify a head for Digit 4 to optimize antenna and camera placement, and it turned out to be surprisingly useful for human-robot interaction.Christie Hemm Klok

I could only watch as others brought their own talents to bear to make Digit into a successful product, in gut-wrenching ways that didn’t always align with my vision. There were some especially difficult times when I felt like there were compromises made that didn’t seem necessary, but making those decisions was no longer my sole responsibility. I had to accept that Digit had grown far beyond me, and look for ways of influencing decisions rather than directing them. In the moment, it was incredibly frustrating, as Digit 4 EVT took a few steps backward from my vision of how humanoids should look and move.

Agility is in our name because agility is core to the future of the company. I want Digit to be lithe and graceful. When people see Digit, I want them to like the robot, to understand what it can do at a glance, and to feel that it’s there to help. But with Digit 4 EVT, all of that was sacrificed to functionality, because of course it had to be—the customer just needs the robot to do the work. The design process became a ruthless triage, with aesthetics and elegance of movement being delayed over and over again in favor of brutal functionality. For an engineer whose mind lives more in the future than in the present, it has been hard for me to accept this trajectory. And yet, through Digit 4 EVT’s commercial deployments, we began to really understand specific applications and what they required. We mapped the first 10 use cases Digit would perform, and then began designing Digit 5 from scratch based on everything we had learned.

Digit 5

Digit 5 will be Agility’s first real step toward a general-purpose robot. It marks our transition from imagining what a future humanoid robot should be to designing a product around the realities of customer deployments. These realities include things like European CE regulatory compliance, North American UL electrical-safety certifications, and Federal Communications Commission limits for electromagnetic radiation—all of which felt tangential to my dream for Digit, but were absolute requirements.

Digit 5 is designed to be the first humanoid robot that can operate safely next to human coworkers while working 20 hours a day and lifting more than a human can.Christie Hemm Klok

Customer requirements helped us realize that safety in particular was one of the most critical barriers to scaling. In Digit 4’s commercial deployments, the robots had to work behind physical barriers, separated from humans at all times, to meet insurance and regulatory demands. This seemed silly to me, because I’ve never felt that our robots were dangerous, but we have to prove that they’re safe. And since keeping the robots in safety cages severely limited their usefulness, enabling Digit 5 to operate safely around people became a primary design goal. We spent two years on this challenge.

Digit 5 is designed to be the first humanoid robot that can operate safely next to human coworkers. It will rely on an array of sensors to locate nearby people in a manner that’s certifiably safe, and then take actions based on that information. As a person approaches, Digit 5 is programmed to stop what it’s doing, set down its payload, sit on the floor, and cut power to its motors before the person gets close enough to touch the robot. This conservative approach reduces the risk to nearby people in a verifiable way, and we believe it’s the only approach that makes sense as a first step toward working alongside humans.

Yet this strategy also feels like a massive slowdown on the path toward robots becoming part of everyday life. Enabling coexistence with humans has influenced almost every aspect of Digit 5’s design: the shape of the legs and the body, the safety-rated sensors within protective housings, the isolated computers dedicated to safety, and more. All of these features increase the robot’s complexity, power consumption, mass, and physical size.

Digit 5 is Agility’s largest, heaviest, strongest, and most capable humanoid robot worker.

The robot’s design was driven by safety, with integrated protection for the robot and for the humans it will work around.Agility Robotics

Weighing over 120 kg, Digit 5 is a huge robot. It needed to achieve the lifting limits recommended under the U.S. National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health guidelines for human workers, which include repeatedly lifting 23-kg bins and reaching shelves 1.75 meters high. Digit is designed to perform tasks like these for 20 hours a day—something no human can do. This requires motors with enough power and torque to lift the load, but also enough precision to delicately control the force Digit applies when it makes contact with the world. To power those motors, we needed a large battery and a dock for rapid autonomous charging. The dock will deliver 240 amperes at 48 volts, which is enough current to make its thick charging cables visibly wiggle under the induced magnetic forces, while powerful fans blow air through cooling channels in the robot’s torso to keep the battery from overheating. The battery and cooling system takes up about 75 percent of Digit’s torso, but it will allow the robot to operate for 90 minutes and fully recharge during a 9-minute break, enabling near-continuous work.

All of this extra bulk means that Digit 5 can no longer run or jump the way previous generations could, and the svelte form I imagined has become physically impossible. In some ways, Digit 5 feels more like a forklift than the graceful humanoid I set out to build. But looking at the situation rationally, I believe this trade-off is necessary for any company that claims its humanoid can do this kind of work today: If you see a robot that looks like a human working in a factory or warehouse, it either can’t lift the required weight or can’t make it through the whole shift. And if it’s not covered with sensors, it’s likely not safe to stand next to.

The most visible change with Digit 5 is that humanlike legs replace the ostrichlike legs of our earlier versions. While the “backward” leg configuration was optimized for walking and running, we discovered that it created problems with squatting because the ankle will strike the ground. Digit 5 will spend a lot of time lifting heavy objects from near the floor, so we used a humanoid leg configuration with knee and hip motors that both contribute torque to help the robot stand up again.

When Digit detects an approaching human, the robot will lower itself to the floor and cut power to its motors. Agility believes this is the only near-term approach to verifiably reduce risk when the robot is working near people.Agility Robotics

Other companies building humanoid robots have copied the human form without ever understanding its purpose. It’s very frustrating that, to an outside observer, it now looks like we discarded Agility’s distinctive leg design and simply did what everyone else was doing. Yet I know that we arrived at this shape based purely on functional engineering choices. Making a robot look like a human is the easy part—without the function to match, and the understanding of the physics underlying that function, the whole point of the human form may be lost.

With the power, battery life, and safety features that we are designing into Digit 5, we will have a truly useful robot. But it also compromises on some things that are core to my vision and mission. I understand why we made the choices we did and can justify them all, but together they leave me unsatisfied. It’s the curse of the engineer.

What Comes After Digit 5

I believe Digit 5 will be the scaling moment for Agility Robotics, the inflection point where we can begin deploying robots in large numbers. While the robot will continue carrying bins, our road map includes many more tasks, from picking individual items and tending machines, to preparing parts for assembly and shipping, and eventually doing assembly work itself. Its interchangeable tool interfaces and modular design will enable us to gradually upgrade its subsystems. It will be a platform for improvement, a source of data for future behavior training, and a viable product all at the same time.

Hurst stands between the previous version of Digit, Digit 4 EVT [left], and Digit 5.Christie Hemm Klok

But I cannot love Digit 5. It’s the largest, heaviest, most expensive robot Agility will ever build, full of design compromises that enable it to be exactly the robot it needs to be right now rather than the robot I dreamed of. In the beginning, I envisioned steady progress on agility and dexterity, with each version becoming more dynamically capable. Instead, developing Digit 5 has required large detours to incorporate requirements that seemed unrelated to my original vision. However, I now recognize that tackling these challenges was always necessary. Customers and product requirements didn’t ruin my vision; they revealed what my vision was missing. This is how robots will achieve commercial success and make a real-world impact, which will bring me closer to my scientific dream as well.

We won’t have a humanoid that matches the capabilities of humans until it achieves that mastery of motion that inspired me to found Agility over a decade ago. I saw glimmers of it with the spring-mass locomotion of ATRIAS, and more glimmers with Cassie’s running gait.

I know that such mastery is physically possible with the tools and hardware that we have right now, and despite all of its compromises, Digit 5 will give us a practical foundation for pursuing that dream. I want every future version of Digit to be lighter and more graceful, to interact more naturally with humans, and to be better at manipulating objects and moving through the world, enabling robots to enter aspects of our lives that they haven’t been able to help with before. By the time I’m old enough to need assistance at home, I expect Digit will be up to the task.

With Digit 5, Agility has built a robot that will be useful in our world. Now I’m getting back to work to make it beautiful, too.
