#4584. House House Chose Not To Put Big Walk On Xbox In Accordance With Palestinian-Led BDS Boycott Against Microsoft Games

📅 15 Oct 2026 | Category: other | Source

Untitled Goose Game developer House House launched Big Walk, its multiplayer cooperative puzzle-adventure game, on PlayStation 5, Nintendo Switch 2, PC, and Mac in August to critical acclaim. Since then, players have asked the studio to release the game on Xbox Series X/S, the only console absent from its launch lineup. Today, House House released an explanation stating that Big Walk is not on Xbox in accordance with the Palestinian-led BDS boycott against Microsoft.

"We have made the decision not to release Big Walk on Xbox, in accordance with the Palestinian-led BDS (Boycott, Divestment, Sanctions) movement call to boycott Microsoft, and their designation of Microsoft Games (and Xbox) as a priority boycott target," the statement reads. 

House House later writes that it would love for players to experience Big Walk on Xbox, but notes that won't happen until Microsoft and/or Xbox have been removed as BDS boycott targets. 

 

BDS is an international boycott movement "for freedom, justice, and equality," and "upholds the simple principle that Palestinians are entitled to the same rights as the rest of humanity," according to the organization. BDS states that Israel is occupying and colonizing Palestinian land, discriminating against Palestinian citizens of Israel, and denying Palestinian refugees the right to return to their homes. The movement, which has been around since 2005, is inspired by the South African anti-apartheid movement and urges action to pressure Israel to comply with international law, which BDS says the country has been noncompliant with since 1948.

As for the Microsoft and Xbox boycott target prioritization, BDS says on its website that "Microsoft is perhaps the most complicit tech company in Israel's illegal occupation, apartheid regime, and ongoing genocide against 2.3 million Palestinians in Gaza. [...] Microsoft partners with the apartheid regime of Israel and its prison system. It provides the Israeli military with Azure cloud and AI services that are central to accelerating Israel's genocide of 2.3 million Palestinians in the illegally occupied Gaza Strip. After 34 years of deep complicity with Israel's military, the Israeli army relies heavily on Microsoft to meet technological requirements of its genocide and apartheid regime." 

The BDS movement also calls on people to boycott Microsoft's gaming products, including canceling Xbox Game Pass subscriptions, boycotting Candy Crush, Minecraft, and Call of Duty, and boycotting all Microsoft Gaming products, including Xbox-branded consoles, headsets, and all games published by Microsoft-owned publishing labels. 

House House closes its statement, which you can read in full via the image below, stating, "We've made this decision with the belief that meaningful change can be made in the world, that coordinated collective action can confront even the most powerful interests, and that one day, Palestine will be free."

#35831. 2026 World Cup: Breaking Records, Spiky Charts and Reliability at Scale

📅 15 Oct 2026 | Category: technology | Source

Every four years, the World Cup becomes the largest planned stress test of our infrastructure. This year it also set an all-time record!

There are a few moments throughout the year, like New Years Eve, that really let us see the collective impact of everything we’ve built — but none are bigger than the quadrennial World Cup tournament. The 2026 World Cup broke records across messaging, video, recommendations, and networking at the same time.

Every World Cup match caused surges in traffic. During the final, for example, traffic surged +46% in about four minutes at the end of regular time, and +57% in about 15 minutes at the final whistle.

But by lining game events up against global egress traffic we discovered a striking pattern – surges in sharing were primarily happening during breaks (hydration breaks, halftime, extra time, the final whistle, the awards ceremony). The end-of-regulation whistle produced the single biggest peak of the tournament.

It wasn’t the gameplay that moved our charts. It was the conversations after it. The world came to Meta to watch the game and to share what it made them feel.

Every capacity decision below follows from that one pattern.

Those spikes were absorbed by systems built for exactly this. Taiji balanced load across datacenters, Katran and Proxygen handled L4/L7 traffic distribution, our global load balancing system routed users to the best-positioned edge, and AutoScaling provisioned capacity on demand.

Months before kickoff, Meta’s edge teams analyzed network capacity and worked closely with ISP partners so that upgrades landed where they would most improve quality of experience for end users. That targeted pre-build is now permanent capacity that will keep serving demand long after the tournament.

For Instagram, the tournament meant 39 straight days of traffic spikes as billions of fans checked scores, posted Stories, and flooded Feed during breaks. Machine learning inference, Feed serving, Stories, Explore, and data infrastructure all had to absorb it without degrading the experience.

During the final, halftime Instagram Feed traffic rose 76% above the previous day in the minute the match ended, and stayed about 21% above for the following two hours.

Our approach rested on four things, deployed incrementally as the tournament moved from group stage to final.

All of this, including the phased deployment timeline and the degradation activation criteria, is now a reusable runbook for future large-scale events like the 2028 Olympics.

Goals and the final whistle drove WhatsApp spikes lasting four to five minutes at a time. In its single highest-traffic five-minute window, WhatsApp delivered nearly 8.4 billion messages without interruption.

That came from months of preparation. More than 100 internal services were load-tested and resized ahead of the tournament, then retested, revalidated, and resized as the bracket narrowed and demand climbed.

We didn’t shift load between regions. For past high-traffic events, our default has been to spread demand across multiple regions, letting capacity in quieter parts of the world absorb the spike. That works when peaks land hours apart — a global New Year’s Eve, for example. This event was different. Every region peaked in the same instant, so there was no idle capacity to borrow. Instead of deriving regional demand from a global multiplier, we forecast each region’s peak on its own terms.

We didn’t freeze. The other standard playbook move for a major event is to pause continuous deployment and hold off on riskier operations like disaster recovery exercises. This time we skipped the freeze entirely, on the strength of years of reliability enhancement. Teams shipped on their normal cadence throughout the entire event window, including during the peak moment itself.

Enabling connection in moments like this doesn’t just happen. Powering every message sent, every follower, and every Reel shared in real time across 48 teams and 104 matches is a story of infrastructure and product working as one.

Capacity planning, load testing, traffic management, and rapid incident response come together across these teams so that billions of people can connect in the moments that matter most — like an entire continent celebrating a last-minute goal at the same second.
